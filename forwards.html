The forward is the real underlying

Options are not priced off the stock price. They are priced off the forward, and once you see that, options on futures become the simple case and options on shares become the complicated one — including a rule from page 08 that inverts completely.

Start here

This is the plumbing chapter the series skipped. Page 01 priced an option on a share and quietly used a forward; page 07 traded the financing without ever defining it. This page defines it, and then shows what changes when the underlying is a futures contract instead of a share.

  1. Chapter 01 is the whole idea. A forward price is not a prediction. It is the cost of a package you can assemble today, and the package's P&L is exactly zero at every possible outcome.
  2. Chapter 05 is the one that will surprise you. Page 08 proved an American call on a non-dividend payer is never exercised early. Put the identical option on a futures contract and both sides exercise early.
  3. Chapter 03 is the least glamorous and the most frequently expensive. Multipliers, settlement style and exercise style decide what actually lands in your account.
Notation — every symbol used on this page
FThe forward price for delivery at T. Not a forecast of the spot price — the price agreed today for a trade that settles later.
CarryThe net cost of holding the underlying to the delivery date: financing minus whatever the asset pays you.
qContinuous dividend yield. A modelling convenience; real shares pay lumps, which chapter 02 handles separately.
BasisThe gap between the futures price and spot. It converges to zero at delivery by construction.
Black-76Black–Scholes rewritten for an underlying that is already a forward. The carry term disappears and only discounting is left.
MultiplierHow many units of the underlying one contract controls. The difference between a number on a screen and an amount of money.
Cash settledSettled by a payment computed from a reference price, with nothing delivered. Standard for index products, because you cannot deliver an index.
Variation marginDaily cash settlement of a futures position's mark-to-market. It is why a futures contract has no financing built into it.
01

A forward price is built, not forecast

Buy the share, borrow the money, collect the dividends, sell it forward. The P&L is zero at every possible outcome, and that zero is what fixes the price.

Ask what a stock will be worth in six months and you get an opinion. Ask what price you can agree today to trade it at in six months and you get a number that follows from arithmetic, because the trade can be assembled out of things you can already do.

Here is the package. Borrow 100 at 5%. Buy the share. Hold it for six months, collecting dividends at 2%. Simultaneously agree to sell it forward at F. At delivery you hand over the share, receive F, and repay the loan. If F is anything other than 100 · e(0.05−0.02)·0.5 = 101.5113, that package makes or loses money with no risk, and somebody will do it until it doesn't.

The figure runs the ledger across every terminal spot price from 60 to 160. The locked P&L is 0.0000000000 in every column. Not small, not approximately zero — the position has no exposure to the outcome at all, which is exactly why the forward price is determined rather than debated.

That has a consequence people find hard to accept. A forward is not a forecast. Change your expected return on the stock to −20% a year and the expected spot in a year drops to 80.25; change it to +25% and it rises to 125.86. The forward stays at 103.0455 through all of it, because no step in the construction used an expected return. This is the same argument as page 12's replication, applied one instrument earlier.

So when a futures curve is upward sloping, the market is not predicting a rise. It is telling you what it costs to carry the thing — and reading contango as bullishness is one of the most durable misreadings in finance.

F = S · e(r − q)T

Reads as: the forward is today's price, grown at the financing rate and reduced by whatever the asset pays you while you hold it. Two costs, netted, and no opinion anywhere.

Check yourself

A futures curve slopes upward. What is the market telling you?

It is the carry, not a forecast. An upward-sloping curve on an equity index says the interest on the money exceeds the dividends on the shares, and nothing more. The tell that this is arithmetic rather than sentiment: the slope changes the day rates change, with no revision to anybody's view of the market.
Derivation — the arbitrage that pins F, in both directions

Suppose the forward trades above S·e(r−q)T. Borrow S, buy the share, sell the forward.

at T:   receive F,   repay S·erT,   keep dividends worth S(eqT − 1)

Reads as: you end with the forward price, minus the loan, plus whatever the share paid you. The share itself is gone — you delivered it — so its price at T never enters.

The profit is F − S·e(r−q)T, locked at inception and independent of ST. If that is positive, everyone does it until it isn't.

Now suppose the forward trades below. Reverse every leg: short the share, invest the proceeds, buy the forward. The profit is S·e(r−q)T − F, again locked. Both inequalities close, so the forward is pinned to a point rather than a band.

Where it breaks. The second argument is the fragile one, and it is fragile in practice rather than in theory. It requires shorting the share, which requires borrowing it, which costs a fee that is not the risk-free rate and can spike without warning. On a hard-to-borrow name the lower arbitrage stops working and the forward can sit persistently below the formula — the difference is the borrow cost, which is how the market quotes it. Page 07 trades that gap directly. The other assumptions are milder: financing at a single rate rather than a bid and an ask, dividends known rather than estimated, and no cost to holding the share, which is false for physical commodities and is where convenience yield comes from.

02

What carry is actually made of

One number on a screen, assembled from four things that move independently.

The continuous yield q in chapter 01 is a modelling convenience. Real shares pay discrete lumps on announced dates, and the forward drops by each dividend's future value at the moment it goes ex. Getting this right matters because the difference is not small.

On a 100 stock at 5% over one year, the no-dividend forward is 105.1271. Three quarterly dividends of 60 cents pull it to 103.2814. A single 2.50 special dividend paid at three months pulls it to 102.5316 — a bigger effect than the three regular payments combined, and one that arrives with a few weeks' notice.

Four components make up the carry, and they behave differently.

Financing. The cost of the money, at your rate rather than the government's. It moves with the curve and with your credit.

Dividends. Known in the near term, estimated further out. A forward two years away contains eight dividend forecasts, and each is somebody's guess.

Borrow. Only relevant if you are short the underlying, and it is the component that misbehaves — usually a few basis points, occasionally hundreds, and it can be recalled.

Storage and convenience. Absent for shares, decisive for physical commodities, where holding the actual barrel has a cost and also an option value that shows up as a negative carry term.

The figure builds the forward curve from these pieces so you can watch each one bend it. Notice that dividends produce steps rather than a slope: the curve is smooth between ex-dates and drops discontinuously through them, which is why a futures contract expiring a day either side of an ex-date is a materially different instrument.

F = ( S − PV(dividends) ) · erT

Reads as: strip out the cash the share will pay before delivery, then finance what is left. The dividends are subtracted at present value because you receive them before T, not at it.

Try this

Put a large special dividend just before the one-year point, then move it to just after. Predict what happens to the one-year forward.

Check yourself

A stock's dividend forecast for next year is cut. What happens to its one-year forward?

It rises. This catches people because the instinctive reaction to a dividend cut is bearish, and the forward's response is mechanically the opposite. Spot may well fall on the news; the forward relative to spot widens, because the dividend was the thing making the carry cheap. Someone long a forward and short the stock is long the dividend forecast, whether they thought about it or not.
03

What one contract actually controls

The least interesting page in the specification is the one that decides how much money is at stake.

Everything in this series so far has been priced per unit of underlying. A real trade is a number of contracts, each controlling a multiple of something, settling in a specific way on a specific date. Getting that chain wrong is not a modelling error, it is a wire transfer.

The multiplier is the first link. A single-stock option on a 172.40 share controls 100 shares — $17,240 of notional per contract. An index option with a 100 multiplier on a 5,280 index controls $528,000. An E-mini equity index future with a 50 multiplier on the same index controls $264,000. Three instruments on similar-sounding things, differing by a factor of thirty in what one unit commits you to.

Then settlement style. A single-stock option is physically settled: exercise and shares move. An index option is cash settled, because there is nothing to deliver — you receive the difference. On a 5,200 call settling at 5,283.47 with a 100 multiplier, that is (5,283.47 − 5,200) × 100 = $8,347, and no position in anything remains afterwards. That distinction changes what happens on expiration weekend entirely: page 08's pin risk is a physical-settlement problem and largely evaporates under cash settlement.

Then exercise style. American for most single-stock options, European for most index options — which is why page 08's early-exercise machinery applies to one and not the other.

And finally the settlement price itself, which is not always the closing price. Some index products settle on a special quotation calculated from opening prices on the expiration morning, a value that may never trade as a level anywhere. A position held to expiry settles against a number the screen never printed.

The figure walks the chain from a quote to an amount of money, with the multiplier and settlement style as controls. It is deliberately mundane. It is also the calculation most likely to be wrong by a factor of ten in a real book.

Check yourself

You are short 10 index calls, cash settled, that finish 83.47 points in the money on a 100 multiplier. What happens on Monday?

You pay and you are flat. Ten contracts × 83.47 points × 100 = $83,470. The absence of a residual position is the whole point of cash settlement, and it is why index expirations do not produce page 08's Monday-morning surprise. The trade-off is that you cannot influence or hedge the settlement print, which for a special opening quotation is a number assembled from prices you never saw as a level.
04

Options on futures: the same model, simplified

When the underlying is already a forward, the carry term has nowhere left to go.

A futures contract costs nothing to enter and pays nothing to hold — its mark-to-market is settled in cash every day through variation margin. So there is no financing to do and no dividend to collect, which means the carry term that dominated chapters 01 and 02 simply vanishes from the option pricing problem.

What is left is Black–Scholes with the forward substituted for the spot and the drift removed. This is Black-76, and it is not a different model — it is the same model told the truth about its underlying. Price a six-month at-the-money call on a 100 stock at 6% two ways: Black–Scholes on the share gives 9.882656, and Black-76 on the corresponding forward of 103.0455 gives 9.882656. They agree to 1.2 × 10−14, because they are the same equation.

One practical difference does survive, and it catches people. The delta of a futures option is measured with respect to the futures price, and it comes out discounted: exactly e−rT times the corresponding spot delta. At the money in our example that is 0.5801 against 0.5977 — a ratio of 0.970446, which is e−0.03 to six decimals.

So hedging a futures option requires fewer futures than the spot-model delta suggests — about 1.8 contracts fewer per hundred at the money, and 2.7 fewer per hundred at a 20% in-the-money strike. The reason is that a futures position's profit arrives as cash today through margin, while the option's payoff arrives at expiry; matching them means discounting one to the other.

It is a small number and it is a systematic one, which is the worst combination — large enough to matter on size, small enough to be invisible in a single trade.

c = e−rT [ F·N(d1) − K·N(d2) ]     d1 = [ ln(F/K) + ½σ²T ] / σ√T

Reads as: the same formula, with the forward in place of the spot and nothing left to grow. Every appearance of the carry rate has gone except the discount factor out front.

Try this

Push rates to 12% and the expiry out to two years. Predict how many contracts per hundred the gap becomes before you look.

Check yourself

Why does an option on a futures contract have no carry term in its pricing formula?

There is nothing to carry. Variation margin means a futures position never ties up the notional, so the cost of holding it is zero and the forward is already the expected settlement under the pricing measure. The first answer is a symptom rather than the cause — the deeper point is that a future is already a forward, so the carry has been done for you.
05

Where page 08's rule inverts

An American call on a non-dividend payer is never exercised early. Put the identical option on a futures contract and both sides exercise early.

Page 08 opened by proving something absolute: on a stock that pays no dividend, the right to exercise an American call early is worth exactly zero, measured at 1.85 × 10−13 across six hundred cases. The proof was one line — a call is worth more alive than dead because selling it recovers the discount on the strike.

Now price the same option on a futures contract. On a one-year 100-strike with 30 volatility and rates at 8%, the numbers come out like this.

At F = 130 the European call is worth 30.9927 and the American is 32.2092 — an early-exercise premium of 1.2166. At F = 70 the European put is 29.0132 and the American is 30.4624, a premium of 1.4492. At the money both sides carry a premium of 0.2205, identically, because futures options are symmetric in F and K in a way stock options are not. Meanwhile the stock call's premium at the same moneyness is 0.00e+0, exactly as page 08 promised.

The mechanism is visible in one number. A European futures option can trade below its intrinsic value. At F = 140 the European call is worth 39.0630 against an intrinsic of 40 — below by 0.9370. At F = 200 it is 92.4494 against 100, below by 7.5506.

That is impossible for a stock option, and the impossibility is precisely page 08's proof: the bound there was c ≥ S − Ke−rT, which sits above the intrinsic S − K. For a futures option the corresponding bound is c ≥ e−rT(F − K), which sits below the intrinsic F − K. Both terms get discounted instead of just one, so the floor drops beneath the exercise value and exercising becomes worth doing.

The intuition, stripped of algebra: exercising a deep futures option converts a claim you will collect later into cash you hold now, and holding cash earns interest. Nothing is given up in exchange, because a futures position — unlike a share — costs nothing to hold. So the interest on the intrinsic is free money, and it beats the remaining time value once the option is deep enough.

Two consequences worth carrying. Deep in-the-money futures options should not be assumed to have time value; check. And the early-exercise premium is symmetric here, so a futures options book carries the risk on both wings, where an equity book carries it almost entirely on the put side.

stock:   c ≥ S − Ke−rT > S − K      futures:   c ≥ e−rT(F − K) < F − K

Reads as: on a share only the strike gets discounted, so the floor sits above intrinsic; on a future both sides get discounted, so the floor sits below it. One discount factor is the whole difference between "never exercise" and "exercise both sides".

Take rates to zero. Predict what happens to both futures curves before you press it — the algebra above tells you.

Check yourself

A deep in-the-money American futures call is trading at exactly its intrinsic value. Is something wrong?

It is correct, and it is the signature of a futures option. Once the European value drops below intrinsic, the American value is held at intrinsic by the exercise right — it cannot go lower and there is no reason for it to be higher. Anyone who learned "options always have time value" on equities will read this as a broken quote; it is the discount factor applying to both legs instead of one.
06

Index products, and the dividends you already own

You cannot deliver an index, cannot capture its dividends by exercising, and cannot trade the price it settles at.

Index derivatives look like equity derivatives and differ in three ways that each remove a whole chapter of complication — and add a different one.

The dividends are inside the index, not attached to it. A price index falls as its members go ex, so the dividend stream shows up in the carry rather than as something you can collect by exercising. That deletes page 08's entire dividend-capture apparatus for index options: there is no ex-date on which owning the deliverable pays you something the option does not.

Settlement is cash and exercise is usually European. Between them, the two remove early exercise and pin risk almost entirely. There is no Monday-morning share position, because there was never a share.

But the settlement price is its own hazard. Several index products settle against a special quotation computed from opening prices on expiration morning rather than from a traded close. The resulting number is a weighted assembly of prices that may never have coexisted, and it can differ from any level the index printed. A position carried to expiry settles against something you could not have traded, which is a small, specific and entirely one-directional risk.

The figure prices the same strike as an index option and as a single-stock option to show which complications survive the change of underlying. The comparison is worth having explicitly, because most option education is written about single stocks and most option volume is in index products.

Check yourself

Why is dividend capture not a consideration for index options?

The dividends are inside the index. Exercising an index option gives you cash, not a basket you could hold through an ex-date, so there is nothing to capture. Cash settlement is a genuine reason early exercise is rare, but the deeper reason is that the economic incentive page 08 identified never exists here in the first place.
07

Where it breaks

The forward formula has one rate in it. Real financing has two, and one of them can be recalled.

Chapter 01's derivation closed the arbitrage from both sides and therefore pinned the forward to a point. Every failure on this page is a failure of one of those two legs.

Borrow breaks the lower arbitrage. Selling the share requires borrowing it, at a fee that is not the risk-free rate. On a hard-to-borrow name that fee is large, variable and revocable — a recall can force you to close at the worst moment. When it bites, the forward sits persistently below the textbook level and the gap is the market's borrow quote. Page 07 shows the same effect arriving through put–call parity, which is the same arbitrage wearing options.

There is no single rate. You borrow at one rate and lend at another, so the arbitrage closes to a band rather than a point. The band is narrow for a large institution and not narrow for anybody else, and it widens exactly when funding markets are stressed.

Dividends are forecasts. Anything beyond the next declared payment is an estimate. A two-year forward contains eight of them, so a long-dated forward is partly a bet on a dividend policy — and special dividends, buybacks and cuts all arrive with little notice.

Futures are not forwards. The two are equal only when interest rates are deterministic. A futures contract settles its mark-to-market daily, so gains are financed at rates that themselves move; when rates and the underlying are correlated, that daily settlement is worth something and the futures price separates from the forward. The effect is negligible on short-dated equity index products and material on long-dated rate-sensitive ones.

And the basis does not always converge quietly. It must reach zero at delivery, but it can behave badly on the way — particularly around index rebalances, dividend seasons and quarterly rolls, when a lot of positions have to move at once.

The figure puts the borrow rate and the financing spread on sliders so you can see the pinned point become a band. What survives all of it is the direction of every argument: carry sets the forward, the forward is not a forecast, and options are priced off the forward rather than off spot.

Check yourself

A stock's forward trades persistently 3% below the textbook value. What is the most likely explanation?

Borrow cost. Persistent, one-directional deviations in a forward are almost always financing rather than opinion, because the opinion channel is exactly the one chapter 01 showed does not exist. The fourth answer has the sign backwards: a dividend cut raises the forward relative to spot.

Question 1 · chapter 01

What does an upward-sloping futures curve tell you about expected prices?

Nothing about expectations. The cash-and-carry package has zero P&L at every outcome, so no expected return could have entered the price. Reading contango as bullishness survives mostly because the two happen to look alike on a chart.
Question 2 · chapter 02

A company announces a surprise special dividend before your forward's delivery date. What happens to the forward?

It falls. On a 100 stock at 5%, a 2.50 special at three months takes the one-year forward from 105.13 to 102.53 — a bigger move than three regular 60-cent dividends combined.
Question 3 · chapter 03

An index option with a 100 multiplier on a 5,280 index. How much notional does one contract control?

$528,000. Thirty times a single-stock option on a 172.40 share, and the two look like the same kind of object on a screen. This is the arithmetic most likely to be wrong by a factor of ten in a real book.
Question 4 · chapter 04

Your spot model says delta is 0.60. How many futures do you sell to hedge a hundred-lot?

About 58. At 6% over six months the ratio is 0.970446, so 0.5977 becomes 0.5801 — roughly 1.8 contracts fewer per hundred. Small, systematic, and invisible on any single trade.
Question 5 · chapter 05

Which is true of American options on futures?

Both sides. At F=130 the call premium is 1.2166 and at F=70 the put premium is 1.4492; at the money both are 0.2205 exactly. The stock call at the same moneyness is 0.00e+0. One extra discount factor separates the two worlds.
Question 6 · chapter 07

Why does a hard-to-borrow stock's forward sit below the textbook value?

The lower arbitrage is broken. Capping the forward needs only money; flooring it needs somebody willing to lend you shares. That asymmetry is why deviations in a forward are almost always financing rather than opinion.

Contract specifications on this page are illustrative and every one of them is set by an exchange and revised periodically — multipliers, settlement style, exercise style, expiration timing and the definition of the settlement price all differ by product and by venue, and several have changed within living memory. Nothing here should be used in place of the current specification for a specific contract. The pricing arguments are more durable but still assume a single financing rate, known dividends, and interest rates that do not move, which is what makes futures and forwards identical here and not quite identical in a long-dated rate-sensitive market.
