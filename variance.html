Only one of them is tradeable

Variance can be manufactured out of a fixed basket of options bought today and never touched again. Volatility — its own square root — cannot. Everything difficult about this subject descends from that one asymmetry.

01

The square is not a detail

A variance swap and a volatility swap on the same underlying, over the same window, do not have the same fair value. The gap is Jensen's inequality, and it is large.

People say "volatility" and write down variance, and most of the time nothing goes wrong. It goes wrong here. Variance is the square of volatility, squaring is a convex function, and the expectation of a convex function is not the function of the expectation. So the fair strike of a contract paying realized variance sits strictly above the square of the fair strike of one paying realized volatility — on the same underlying, over the same window, with nothing else different.

The size of the gap is set by how uncertain the volatility itself is. If you knew realized vol would be exactly 25%, the two contracts would be identical and the gap would be zero. Put some uncertainty around that 25% and the gap opens immediately.

The figure draws realized volatility as a distribution centered on 25 and reports both numbers. At a vol-of-vol of 0.4 the mean volatility is still 25.0% but the square root of mean variance is 27.1% — a gap of 2.08 volatility points. Push vol-of-vol to 0.6 and the gap is 4.93 points, which is not a rounding difference, it is most of a trade.

This matters in one direction more than the other. Someone short a variance swap is short that convexity: their losses grow with the square of how wrong they were, while their gains are capped by volatility not being able to fall below zero. That asymmetry is the whole risk profile of the product and chapter 05 returns to it.

E[σR²] > (E[σR])²   whenever σR is uncertain

Reads as: the average of the squares beats the square of the average. The two are equal only when there is nothing to average over — that is, when you already know the answer.

Check yourself

A dealer quotes you a variance swap at 25 and a volatility swap on the same window at 25. Which is cheaper for you to buy?

The variance swap. Its fair strike carries the convexity premium — 2.08 points at a vol of vol of 0.4 — so a variance swap quoted at the volatility swap's level is being handed to you cheap. The last answer is the tempting one, because it sounds appropriately humble, but the comparison is between two fair values today and does not require knowing the outcome.
02

One contract whose hedge pays exactly the variance

Not approximately, and not on average. On every path, to five decimals.

Page 01 delta-hedged a call and found a residual that shrank with effort but never vanished. Here is a payoff that behaves completely differently. Take the log contract — a claim paying −(2/T)·ln(ST/S0) at expiry — delta-hedge it mechanically, and add up the money at the end. The answer is the realized variance of the path. Every time.

The figure runs a few thousand paths and plots each one's hedged P&L against the realized variance it actually delivered. The result is not a cloud with a trend through it, it is a line: hedging daily, the correlation between the two is 0.99998 at 10 volatility and still 0.99966 at 45. Mean hedged P&L matches mean realized variance to five decimal places. The figure opens at thirty volatility and sixty-three hedges rather than 252, where the same correlation reads 0.99946 — the gap is discretization, and dragging the hedge count to the top closes it.

The reason is a single structural fact. A delta-hedged option's P&L over a small step is ½·S²Γ·(realized − implied) — the gamma rent from page 01. That expression is weighted by dollar gamma, which for a normal option is a bump centered on the strike — so an ordinary option pays you for movement near its strike and almost nothing for movement far away. The log contract's dollar gamma is constant. It pays the same for a 1% move wherever the stock happens to be, which is precisely what "measure the variance" means.

So the log contract is not one instrument among many. It is the unique payoff that turns a delta hedge into a variance meter, and everything in the rest of this page is an attempt to build it out of things that actually trade.

hedged P&L  =  σR²     because   S²Γ  is constant

Reads as: a contract that pays the same for a percentage move at any price level accumulates exactly the sum of squared percentage moves. Constant dollar gamma is the whole trick.

Try this

Drop the number of hedges to 12 and predict whether the dots move off the line in a biased direction or just spread around it.

Check yourself

Why can't you build a variance meter out of a single at-the-money straddle, delta-hedged?

The dollar gamma is not flat. A hedged straddle is a perfectly good approximation to a variance position while spot stays near the strike, which is why it is used that way in practice, and it decays into something else entirely once the stock trends away. The whole point of the 1/K² strip in chapter 03 is to add up enough hills to make a flat surface.
Derivation — why constant dollar gamma means variance

Hold a payoff f(S) and hedge its delta continuously. Itô says the value change splits into a delta piece, which the hedge cancels, and a curvature piece, which it does not.

d(hedged) = ½ · f″(S) · S²σ² dt

Reads as: what survives the hedge is curvature multiplied by the size of the move squared. This is page 01's gamma rent with the implied leg removed.

Now choose f(S) = −(2/T)·ln(S). Differentiating twice gives f″(S) = (2/T)/S², so the S² cancels exactly.

½ · (2/T)/S² · S²σ² dt = (σ²/T) dt

Reads as: the price level drops out and you are left accumulating variance per unit time. Integrate over the life and the total is the realized variance.

That cancellation is the entire subject. It happens for the logarithm and for nothing else, which is why the log contract has a name.

Where it breaks. Itô's lemma assumes the path is continuous. A jump moves the stock a finite distance with no intervening hedge, so the hedge does not cancel the delta piece and the log contract stops measuring quadratic variation — chapter 07 puts a number on it. Discrete hedging causes scatter but no bias, which is the difference the try-this box above is asking you to notice. And the derivation says nothing about how you would own f(S), which is the next chapter's problem.

03

Building it out of things that trade

Nobody lists a log contract. You assemble one from ordinary options weighted by 1/K², and then never touch it again.

The log payoff is a curve, and the only curves you can buy are made of options. Any twice-differentiable payoff can be written exactly as a fixed portfolio of calls and puts, with each strike weighted by the payoff's second derivative there. For the log contract that second derivative is (2/T)/K² — so the weight on the option struck at K is proportional to 1/K², and that is where the famous weighting comes from. It is not a convention or an approximation. It is the curvature of a logarithm.

What makes this different from every other hedge in the series is that it is static. You buy the strip once, at inception, and you do not rebalance it. Page 01's replication needed continuous attention and still left a residual; page 06 charged you for that attention. Here the options portfolio is fixed at the start and the only ongoing work is the delta hedge, which is the part that does the measuring.

The figure builds the strip in front of you. Start with a handful of strikes and the assembled payoff is a jagged approximation with visible corners; add strikes and the corners round off onto the smooth log curve. In numbers, on a chain running from 40% to 250% of the forward against a true 25% volatility: strikes every $40 recover 22.35%, every $10 recover 25.02%, and every $2.50 recover 25.00%. Twenty-one strikes is already enough to be within two basis points.

That convergence is fast and it is the reassuring half of the story. The alarming half is the next chapter, because none of those numbers survive if you shorten the range rather than the spacing.

strip weight at strike K  ∝  ΔK / K²

Reads as: buy more of the low strikes and less of the high ones, in proportion to one over the strike squared. A $50 option gets sixteen times the weight of a $200 one.

Check yourself

Why does the strip need no rebalancing when page 01's replication did?

The curvature is matched exactly, once. A payoff decomposed into options by its own second derivative is not an approximation that decays — the identity holds at every spot and every time, so the basket is bought and left. This is what "static replication" means and it is the genuinely new machinery on this page. The third answer describes something that would be very convenient and does not exist: the weights are fixed at inception.
04

The wings are not a detail either

Cut the strike range to 92%–108% and a 25 variance swap prices at 17.4. The strikes nobody wants to quote are a third of the contract.

Chapter 03 showed the strip converging quickly in the number of strikes. It is easy to read that as "the replication is robust" and it is the wrong lesson, because the quantity that matters is not how finely the strikes are spaced but how far they extend.

Hold the spacing at $5 and pull the range in. From 40%–250% of the forward the strip recovers 24.97% against a true 25%. Narrow it to 75%–140% and you get 23.69%. Narrow again to 85%–120% — still a wider range than many single-name chains actually quote — and you get 20.81%, low by 16.8%. At 92%–108% the answer is 17.42%, low by 30.3%, on a contract that was supposed to be replicable.

The reason is the 1/K² weight, which is doing something people consistently underestimate. Low strikes get enormous weight — a 50 strike carries sixteen times the weight of a 200 strike — and while each of those options is individually cheap, there are a lot of them and the weighting is aggressive. Break the total down by where it comes from and the at-the-money region between 90% and 110% supplies only 50.7% of the variance. The band from 70% to 90% supplies 22.9%, and everything below 70% another 3.5%.

So roughly a quarter of a variance swap's value lives in strikes below 90%, in options that are individually near-worthless, frequently untradeable in size, and quoted on a volatility that is largely a market-maker's opinion. That is the practical fragility of the product, and it is why the honest version of "variance is replicable" carries the words "given a continuum of strikes" in front of it.

σR² ≈ (2erT/T) · Σ (ΔK/K²) · O(K)

Reads as: add up every out-of-the-money option, each weighted by one over its strike squared. O(K) means the put below the forward and the call above it — always the out-of-the-money side, because that is the one that trades.

Set the range to 85%–120%, which is a plausible listed chain on a mid-cap. Predict the recovered volatility, then read it. Then move only the lower bound to 60%.

Check yourself

A market maker quotes you a variance swap on a name whose listed options stop at 85% and 120% of spot. What have they actually sold you?

An approximation plus somebody's wing mark. The contract is perfectly writable and it is exactly as hedgeable as the strikes available to hedge it. Whatever the strip cannot reach is warehoused risk, priced from a model rather than a market — which is why variance swaps on illiquid underlyings are quoted wide and why they behave badly in exactly the moves that were supposed to make them pay.
05

What the contract actually pays

Quoted in volatility points, settled in variance, and convex in a direction that matters enormously to whoever is short.

A variance swap is quoted as a volatility — "we'll trade var at 25" — and settles on variance. The payoff is the difference of the squares, scaled by a notional, and that mismatch between the quoting convention and the settlement convention is where most of the practical confusion lives.

Because nobody wants to think in units of variance, the market sizes these in vega notional: the amount you make or lose for the first volatility point of difference. Variance notional is then vega notional divided by twice the strike. The consequence is that the first point behaves like a normal vega position and every subsequent point pays more than the one before.

Run the numbers on a 25 strike with $100,000 of vega notional. Realized comes in at 26 and you make about $100,000. At 30, a five-point miss, you make roughly $550,000 — not five times the one-point figure but five and a half. At 45 you make $2.8 million. On the other side, realized at 20 loses you about $450,000 and realized at 10 loses $1.05 million, which is where the asymmetry shows: volatility cannot go below zero, so the short's losses are unbounded while the long's are capped at the strike.

One more thing the strip explains rather than assumes. The fair variance strike is not the at-the-money volatility — it is an average over the whole surface with 1/K² weights, so skew pushes it up. On a 25% at-the-money vol, a skew of −0.10 gives a variance strike of 25.74%, and a skew of −0.25 gives 30.44%. That five-and-a-half-point premium is not a forecast that the stock will be more volatile. It is the put wing being expensive, mechanically loaded into a single number.

payoff = Nvega · (σR² − Kvar²) / (2·Kvar)

Reads as: you are paid the difference of the squares, sized so that the first volatility point is worth your vega notional. The division by twice the strike is purely the unit conversion that makes the quote readable.

Check yourself

The at-the-money vol is 25 and the variance strike is 30.4. What is the market saying?

It is a statement about the surface's shape, not its level. This is the single most common misreading of a variance quote, and it has a real consequence: comparing a variance strike to an at-the-money volatility and calling the difference "the variance risk premium" double-counts the skew. Strip the skew contribution out first, or you are measuring the smile and calling it a forecast.
06

VIX is this, implemented

Not a forecast, not a sentiment gauge. A 30-day variance strip with a published rulebook.

The index everyone quotes as "the fear gauge" is the calculation from chapter 03 run on a fixed schedule. It takes out-of-the-money options on two expiries bracketing 30 days, weights each by ΔK/K², adds them up, interpolates between the two expiries to land exactly on 30 days, and reports the square root as an annualized percentage. There is no model in it, no volatility forecast, and no opinion.

Three consequences follow directly from the chapters above, and each explains something people find puzzling about the index.

First, it is a variance strike, so it carries the skew premium. VIX sits above at-the-money 30-day implied volatility essentially always, and the gap widens when skew steepens. That is chapter 05's 5.4 points, not the market becoming more afraid.

Second, it inherits the truncation problem. The rulebook stops including strikes once it meets two consecutive zero bids, so the effective wing of the strip is set by what happens to be quoted that morning. In a stressed market more deep puts get bids, the strip extends, and the index rises partly because more of the tail became visible.

Third, it is a strike, not an asset. You cannot hold VIX. What trades is a futures contract on where the index will be, which is a different thing with its own term structure and its own roll — and that is the source of the long-run behavior of every product built on top of it.

The figure runs the calculation on a synthetic chain so you can watch the index move for structural reasons rather than emotional ones: steepen the skew and it rises with volatility unchanged; truncate the quoted wing and it falls with everything else held fixed.

Check yourself

Thirty-day at-the-money implied volatility is unchanged on the day, but the index is up two points. What is the most likely explanation?

The wing repriced. The index is a weighted average across the whole surface, so it can move a long way with the at-the-money option untouched — and on a lot of days that is exactly what happens. Reading the index as a level of fear rather than as an average of a shape is the most common mistake made about it.
07

Where it breaks

The replication is exact under one assumption that markets violate on their most important days.

Chapter 02's derivation used Itô's lemma, and Itô's lemma assumes the price path is continuous. A jump moves the stock a finite distance in no time, with no opportunity to rebalance in between, and the neat cancellation that made the log contract a variance meter does not survive it.

The consequence is a bias with a definite sign. The log contract prices −(2/T)·E[ln(ST/F)], and under jumps that quantity is no longer the expected quadratic variation. On a 20% diffusive volatility with one expected jump a year of −20%, true quadratic variation corresponds to 29.97% while the strip prices 29.38% — the replication is 0.59 volatility points light. With a half-yearly −30% jump the gap widens to 1.10 points. The strip under-hedges a downward-jumping stock, and it does so exactly when the contract is paying out most.

Three more failures worth naming, none of them exotic.

Discreteness. The contract settles on daily closing returns, not on a continuous integral. That introduces sampling error with no bias, which the chapter 02 figure shows directly when you drop the hedge count.

Caps. Most single-name variance swaps are sold with a cap, usually at two and a half times the strike. That cap makes the payoff no longer variance — it is a variance swap minus a call on variance — and the replication chapter no longer applies to the capped part. The cap exists because of everything above.

Liquidity in the wing. Chapter 04's arithmetic assumed you could buy every strike at the quoted price. In the move that makes a variance swap pay, the put wing gaps and widens simultaneously, so the hedge you needed becomes expensive precisely when you need to put it on.

None of this makes the product unsound. It makes the phrase "variance is perfectly replicable" a statement about a model rather than about a market, and the gap between those two is the entire job.

Check yourself

You are long a variance swap, hedged with the replicating strip. The stock gaps down 25% overnight on a takeover collapse. What happens?

You make money, and you under-collect. Being long variance through a crash is the right side of the trade and the replication still leaves a gap, because the argument that made it exact assumed the price never skipped. This is the honest reason variance swaps on single names carry caps and wide bid–offers: the product's one structural weakness shows up in the one scenario that defines it.

Question 1 · chapter 01

When are a variance swap and a volatility swap worth the same?

Only under certainty. A flat surface removes the skew premium of chapter 05 but does nothing to Jensen — the gap here is about uncertainty in the outcome, not about the shape of today's smile.
Question 2 · chapter 02

What makes the log contract special?

Constant dollar gamma. Ordinary options have a dollar-gamma hill centered on their strike, so they measure movement only where they are standing. The logarithm is the one payoff whose second derivative cancels the S² and leaves a flat meter.
Question 3 · chapter 03

Where does the 1/K² weighting come from?

It is the curvature of a logarithm. The static replication result says a twice-differentiable payoff equals a fixed portfolio of options weighted by its second derivative at each strike. Everything else about the weight is a consequence.
Question 4 · chapter 04

Which change damages the replication more: doubling the strike spacing, or halving the strike range?

The range, and it is not close. Convergence in spacing is fast and convergence in range is slow, which is the opposite of most people's intuition and the reason variance products are fragile on names with thin wings.
Question 5 · chapter 05

Realized volatility comes in at 10 against a 25 strike. You were short $100,000 of vega notional. Roughly what did you make?

About $1.05 million. The payoff is in the difference of squares, so the same number of points is worth less further below the strike and more further above it. That asymmetry is the entire risk profile: bounded gains, unbounded losses.
Question 6 · chapter 07

Why do single-name variance swaps usually carry a cap?

Because the tail is the unhedgeable part. A cap converts the contract into a variance swap minus a call on variance, which is an admission that the replication argument stops working in the scenario the contract is really about.

Every figure on this page assumes European options on a single underlying with no dividends, deterministic rates, and — except where chapter 07 removes it — a continuous price path. The jump numbers come from a Merton model with one jump size, which is a caricature of a real tail. Vol of vol in chapter 01 is a parameter on a slider, not a measurement. Real variance swap documentation specifies the return definition, the observation schedule, the treatment of missing days, the cap, and what happens on a corporate action, and every one of those clauses moves the fair value by more than the modeling questions discussed here.
