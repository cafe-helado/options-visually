The sum of the parts

Ten pages have priced one option at a time. A book is a portfolio, its risk is not the sum of its positions, and the number that looks safest on the report — a net vega near zero — is frequently the one hiding the most.

01

Some greeks add and some only look like they do

Addition is a statement about units. Get the units right and the aggregation question answers itself.

The first thing that goes wrong when you put positions together is that people add numbers which are not in the same units. A delta of 0.54 on one option and 0.31 on another do not sum to anything meaningful, because delta is a rate — shares per share — and the two options are written on different quantities of stock. Convert to money first and the problem disappears.

Dollar delta adds, on one underlying, without qualification. Multiply delta by the contract multiplier, by the position size, and by spot, and you have a number of dollars that moves one-for-one with a percentage change in the stock. Our four-position book carries a share delta of 557 — about $57,000 of exposure — assembled from legs of −2,496, +1,430, +709 and +914 shares.

Gamma cash adds, once expressed as dollars of delta gained per 1% move. Our book's is $2,042 per percent, and it is worth noticing how it got there: the short front-month 100 calls contribute −$18,002 and the long 110 calls +$20,033. Two large opposing numbers netting to something small is the normal condition of an options book, and it is precisely the condition under which a net figure stops describing the risk.

Vega adds arithmetically and lies. You can total it — ours is $417 per volatility point — but the total presumes every volatility moves together, which is the one thing volatilities reliably do not do. Chapter 04 takes this apart.

And two things do not add at all. Gamma across different underlyings is not additive in any useful sense, because a 1% move in one name is not the same event as a 1% move in another; you need a covariance structure, which is page 10's subject. Greeks across dates are not comparable either — a one-week gamma and a one-year gamma are different animals wearing the same label.

$Δ = Δ × multiplier × contracts × S     Γcash = Γ × multiplier × contracts × S² × 1%

Reads as: turn every rate into money before you add anything up. The conversion is mechanical and skipping it is the most common error on a real blotter.

A book shows gamma cash of $2,000 per 1%, assembled from a −$18,000 leg and a +$20,000 leg. What should worry you?

The offset is local. Each leg's gamma is a hill centred on its own strike — the same fact that made page 09's log contract necessary — so a book flat by netting at 102 can be substantially long or short gamma at 95 or 115. This is exactly why chapter 02 exists: a single netted number describes one point, and a matrix describes the neighbourhood.
02

The grid a risk manager actually reads

Reprice the whole book in every cell. Then put the greek prediction beside it and look at the difference.

A greek report is a description of the book at one point, expressed in derivatives. A scenario matrix is the book itself, evaluated at many points. The difference between them is the subject of this page, and the fastest way to see it is to compute both and subtract.

The grid below moves spot across the columns and volatility down the rows and puts the full repriced P&L in each cell. Every position is revalued from scratch at the new spot and the new volatility — no approximations, no local expansions. This is what the book would actually be worth.

Then switch the display to error and you are looking at what a greek report would have missed. At the centre the errors are trivially small, which is exactly what "the greeks are a local approximation" means. Move to the corners and they are enormous: at spot +20% with volatility up 10 points, the book makes $49,061 and the greek estimate is short by $29,456 — the report would have predicted well under half the move.

The asymmetry matters as much as the size. The errors are not symmetric in spot, because gamma is not constant, and they are not symmetric in volatility, because vega is not constant either. A book that looks balanced in the greeks can be strongly convex in one direction and concave in the other, and only the matrix shows it.

This is why the matrix, not the greek line, is the artefact that gets escalated. A greek report answers "what is the book doing right now"; a matrix answers "what happens if", and the second question is the one a risk committee is actually asking.

Check yourself

Your greek report and your scenario matrix disagree about a −20% cell by $12,000. Which is wrong?

Neither is wrong; they answer different questions. Greeks are derivatives evaluated at a point and their accuracy decays with distance from that point — chapter 03 measures how fast. Treating a disagreement as a reconciliation break rather than as expected behaviour is a common and expensive misunderstanding, because the fix people reach for is to "correct" the matrix.
03

How far can you trust a greek report?

About one percent. After that the answer degrades quickly and in a measurable way.

"The greeks are a local approximation" is true and useless without a number attached. Here is the number. Take our book, move spot, and compare the full reprice against what delta and gamma predicted.

At 1% the book makes $582 and the greeks predict $578 — an error of 0.7%, entirely negligible. At 2% the error is 2.7%. At 5% it is 13.8%: the book makes $3,589 and the estimate says $3,093. At 10% the estimate captures barely two-thirds. At 20% the book makes $36,971 and the greeks predict $15,436 — an error of 58%, which is to say the report is wrong by more than it is right.

The shape of that decay is the useful part. The error grows roughly like the cube of the move, because delta and gamma capture the first two terms of the expansion and the first thing they miss is the third. So halving the move you care about cuts the error by about eight, and doubling it multiplies the error by about eight. That is a fast enough exponent that the boundary between "greeks are fine" and "greeks are useless" is narrow, and it sits at a smaller move than most people assume.

The practical rule that falls out: use greeks for hedging decisions and daily attribution, where moves are small and the derivative is exactly what you want. Use the matrix for limits, stress and anything that gets escalated, where the moves are large by construction. Using a greek report to answer a stress question is the specific error, and it always errs in the direction of comfort — it under-predicts big moves in both directions, because it is missing the convexity that makes them big.

Derivation — why the error grows like the cube of the move

A greek report is the first two terms of a Taylor expansion of the book's value in spot.

V(S+h) = V(S) + Δh + ½Γh² + ⅙·(∂³V/∂S³)·h³ + …

Reads as: value, plus delta times the move, plus half gamma times the move squared, plus a third-order term nobody reports. Delta and gamma are the first two; everything after is what the report is missing.

So the error of a delta-plus-gamma estimate is the first term you dropped, and that term carries h³.

error ≈ ⅙ · speed · h³

Reads as: the mistake grows with the cube of how far the market moved. The third derivative of value with respect to spot is sometimes called speed, and it is largest where gamma is changing fastest — near the strikes.

A cubic law is steep. Doubling the move multiplies the error by roughly eight, so the transition from "the greeks are fine" to "the greeks are useless" happens over a surprisingly narrow band of move sizes. Our book's measured errors — 0.7% at 1%, 2.7% at 2%, 13.8% at 5%, 58.2% at 20% — grow faster than the cube because vega and its own curvature are also being left out once volatility moves.

That also explains the shape of the lower panel: it is flat and negligible out to a couple of percent and then turns upward sharply, because a cubic sitting under a linear-plus-quadratic term contributes essentially nothing until it suddenly contributes everything.

Where it breaks. The expansion assumes the book's value is smooth in spot, and it is not. A digital or barrier payoff has a discontinuity in value or in delta, at which point no number of Taylor terms converges and the only honest answer is a full reprice — which is the practical argument for building the matrix out of repricings rather than out of higher-order greeks. The expansion also says nothing about paths: two scenarios that end at the same spot with the same volatility can imply very different hedging costs on the way, and a static matrix cell prices neither.

Check yourself

Adding a third-order term to the estimate would help most in which situation?

Large moves. At small moves the error is already negligible so there is nothing to fix. The reason desks generally do not bother adding higher-order terms is that once you are far enough out for them to matter, a full reprice is both exact and cheap — the approximation exists to save computation that is no longer worth saving.
04

One vega number across the term structure is a lie

Our book's net vega is 417. Four different volatility moves that all "look like two points" produce P&L from 743 to 1,238.

Vega is quoted as a single figure and it is the most misleading number on a risk report, because summing it presumes that every implied volatility on the surface moves together. They do not. Front-month volatility is jumpy and event-driven; back-month volatility is stable and mean-reverting; and the front can rise five points on an earnings date while the back does not move at all.

Our book's vega breaks into +99 in the front two months, −495 in the two-to-six month bucket, and +813 beyond six months, netting to +417. That net is a true arithmetic total and it describes almost nothing, because the book is really a term-structure position — short the belly, long the wings of the calendar — wearing the disguise of a modest long-vol book.

Watch what that does. A genuine parallel move of +2 volatility points makes $863, close to the naive net-vega prediction of $834. But a front-up-ten, back-down-one-and-a-half twist makes $864; a front-down-six, back-up-five twist makes $1,238; and a front-up-twelve, back-down-three twist makes $743. Same book, same net vega, and a spread of nearly 67% between the outcomes — with nothing that a single number could have distinguished.

Which is why real risk systems bucket. The convention is a set of tenor buckets with vega reported in each, and the discipline is that limits are set per bucket rather than on the total. A desk that is flat on net vega and running plus-and-minus a thousand in adjacent buckets is not flat; it has a term-structure position that nobody has approved.

The same argument applies twice more, and for the same reason. Bucket by strike as well as by tenor, because a book long wings and short the body has zero net vega and a large skew position. And remember from page 02 that the delta you are bucketing is itself model-dependent — sticky-strike and sticky-delta conventions produce different vega attributions from identical prices.

A desk reports net vega of zero. What have you learned?

Almost nothing. The third answer is closest to being right and still misses the point: "hedged against a parallel shift" describes the one scenario that essentially never happens cleanly. This is why bucketed limits exist, and why a risk manager's first question on seeing a zero is not "good" but "show me the buckets".
05

Limits, and the question underneath them

A limit is not a prediction. It is a statement about how much of a specific bad day the firm is willing to own.

Limits look like bureaucracy from a trading seat and like arithmetic from a risk seat, and both readings miss what they are for. A limit is a pre-committed answer to a question nobody can think clearly about while it is happening: how much are we prepared to lose in a scenario we have decided is plausible?

They come in layers, and the layers exist because each one fails differently.

Greek limits — maximum dollar delta, gamma cash, bucketed vega. Cheap to compute, easy to monitor continuously, and subject to exactly the failure of chapter 03: they describe the book near today's spot and say nothing about the corners. A book can sit inside every greek limit and still be carrying a scenario loss nobody has authorised.

Scenario limits — the worst cell of the matrix must be better than some number. This is the one that actually binds, because it is computed from full repricings and therefore does not degrade with move size. Its weakness is that it only covers the scenarios in the grid.

Stress limits — named historical or hypothetical events, deliberately chosen because they hurt rather than because they are likely, and usually including a correlation-goes-to-one case for the reasons on page 10.

The figure makes the first layer's weakness concrete. Adjust the position sizes until every greek sits inside its limit, then look at the worst cell of the matrix. It is entirely possible — and on this book, easy — to be green on every greek and red on the scenario, because the greeks are measuring the middle of the grid and the limit that matters lives in a corner.

One more thing worth saying plainly, because it is where limits are most often misunderstood. A limit breach is not a prediction that you will lose the money. It is a statement that the position has grown past the size at which the firm is willing to find out. The correct response is to reduce, not to argue about the probability.

Check yourself

A book is inside every greek limit but breaches its worst-case scenario limit. What is the most likely explanation?

The book is convex, and that is what the two limits are for. They are not redundant controls that should agree — they are deliberately different instruments measuring different regions, and the whole reason for running both is that a book can pass one and fail the other. If they always agreed, one of them would be pointless.
06

Reading the report end to end

The order in which you look at it is most of the skill.

Everything above assembles into one page of output that a desk looks at every morning. Reading it well is a routine, and the routine is worth stating explicitly because the natural reading order is close to the worst one.

Start with yesterday's P&L against yesterday's prediction. Attribution before position. If the book made money the greeks cannot explain, something is wrong with your marks or your understanding, and no amount of looking at today's risk will surface it. An unexplained residual is the single most informative number on the report.

Then the scenario matrix, corners first. Not the greeks. The corners are where the limits bind and where chapter 03 says your intuition is least reliable. Look for asymmetry: a book that loses twice as much down as up is a different animal from one that is symmetric, whatever its delta says.

Then bucketed vega and bucketed gamma. Look for large opposing buckets, which chapter 04 showed are invisible in the totals. The question is not "what is the net" but "what is the gross, and is it deliberate".

Then the greeks, as a hedging instruction. This is what they are good at: telling you what to trade today. It is the last thing to look at, not the first.

Then concentration. How much of the risk is in one name, one expiry, one strike. A book can be well-behaved on every aggregate measure and be a single position with a hedge around it.

The table below assembles the report for our four-position book, and the small print underneath it is the part worth internalising: every number in it is conditional on the marks, and the marks on the illiquid lines are somebody's model. A risk report is a measurement of a book made with instruments that the book itself can bend.

Check yourself

What should you look at first on a daily risk report?

Attribution first. Every forward-looking number on the report is computed from the same marks and the same model that produced yesterday's prediction. If that prediction failed, the rest of the page is being computed with a broken instrument — and finding that out before you act on the risk numbers is the whole reason attribution is run daily rather than monthly.

Question 1 · chapter 01

Which of these adds cleanly across a book on one underlying?

Dollar delta. Rates do not add across different position sizes; money does. This is the same units discipline that page 01's chapter 08 built the blotter around, applied to a whole book instead of one line.
Question 2 · chapter 03

Roughly how wrong is a delta-plus-gamma estimate on a 20% move, for our book?

About 58%. Error grows roughly with the cube of the move, so it is negligible at 1% and dominant at 20%. The practical consequence is that greeks are a hedging tool and the matrix is a limits tool, and swapping them is how books get sized wrongly.
Question 3 · chapter 04

Our book's net vega is 417. What is the range of P&L across volatility moves that all average about two points?

$743 to $1,238 — a spread of about 67%. The book is a term-structure position wearing the disguise of a modest long-vol book, and the disguise is the net vega number itself.
Question 4 · chapter 02

Why does a scenario matrix use full repricing rather than greeks?

Because the interesting cells are far away. The matrix is deliberately built where the approximation fails; using the approximation to build it would defeat the purpose. Full repricing costs more computation and the corners are the only place the extra cost buys anything.
Question 5 · chapter 05

What is a limit breach actually telling you?

It is a statement about appetite, not a forecast. Limits are pre-committed answers to a question that becomes impossible to think about clearly once the scenario is actually happening — which is the entire reason they are set in advance.
Question 6 · chapter 06

The book made $40,000 yesterday and the greeks predicted $5,000. What is the first thing to check?

Marks and model. An unexplained profit is exactly as alarming as an unexplained loss and considerably less likely to be investigated, which is precisely why it is worth naming as the first item in the routine. Attribution is a test of your instruments, and it happens to be denominated in money.

The book on this page is four positions on one underlying with a flat volatility surface per line, no dividends, deterministic rates and a single pricing model. A real book has thousands of lines across hundreds of underlyings, a surface per name that must itself be marked, cross-gamma between correlated underlyings that no single-name figure captures, and positions whose marks are model outputs rather than observed prices. None of that changes the arguments here — dollar units still add, local expansions still fail at the same rate, and net vega still hides the same term-structure risk — but all of it makes the marks less reliable than the arithmetic performed on them. The largest risk on most real reports is not in any of the numbers; it is in the lines whose prices nobody can check.
