Reading the screen

Sixteen pages of theory, and none of them told you how to look at a chain. The skill is converting what is printed into what matters — strike distances into standard deviations, dollars into deltas — and it takes about five seconds once it is automatic.

Scroll ↓

Start here

Everything else in this series builds a concept. This page is about a screen — the grid of strikes and expiries a trader looks at several hundred times a day — and the small conversions that turn it from a wall of numbers into a picture. It is the most practical page here and the least theoretical, and it assumes you have met delta and implied volatility somewhere before.

  1. Chapter 02 is the one to take away. A strike's distance in dollars means nothing. Its distance in standard deviations means everything, and converting between them in your head is the whole skill.
  2. Chapter 03 fixes a rule of thumb almost everyone half-knows. The 16-delta option is about a one-sigma strike — inside three months. At a year it is 1.56 sigma, and the put side is not symmetric with the call side at all.
  3. Chapter 06 is a procedure, not an idea: what to look at first, second and third, on a live chain you can re-parameterise.
Notation — every symbol used on this page

σImplied volatility, annualized, as quoted in the IV column.

01

A chain is a distribution printed sideways

The column of prices down a single expiry is a probability distribution. Once you can see that, the screen stops being a list.

Open any chain and the first instinct is to read it like a table — find the strike you care about, read its row, move on. That is the slow way, and it is the way that misses everything.

Page 02 established that the second derivative of call price across strikes is the risk-neutral density. Which means the column you are looking at is a distribution, drawn in a coordinate system nobody would have chosen. Every skill on this page is a way of undoing that coordinate system fast enough to be useful.

Three conversions do almost all of it. Turn dollars into sigmas, so strikes on a $30 stock and a $600 stock are comparable. Turn prices into deltas, so moneyness is comparable across expiries. And turn the IV column into a shape rather than a list of numbers — a slope and a curvature, which is what your eye is good at and what arithmetic is slow at.

Check yourself

Why is reading a chain row by row the wrong default?

The information lives across the column, not down it. Slowness is a symptom. The real cost is that a single row cannot tell you whether a price is high or low — that judgement is always relative to its neighbors, and the neighbors are what the eye is for.
02

A strike's distance in dollars is meaningless

Five points away is 2.33 standard deviations on one stock and 0.12 on another. The conversion is one multiplication and it is the most valuable habit on this page.

Every chain is laid out in dollars, and dollars are the one unit that does not travel. Take four stocks at 30 volatility with thirty days to expiry, all with five-point strike spacing, and ask how far apart the strikes really are.

On a $25 stock, one standard deviation is $2.15, so consecutive strikes are 2.33 sigma apart — you get less than half a strike inside the entire expected move, and the chain is hopelessly coarse. On a $500 stock the same five points is 0.12 sigma and there are 8.6 strikes inside one standard deviation. Same spacing on the screen; completely different instruments.

The conversion is this, and it should become automatic:

one sigma = S · σ · √T z = (K − S) / (S · σ · √T)

Reads as: multiply the spot by the volatility by the square root of the time, and you have the dollar size of a one-standard-deviation move. Divide any strike's distance by it and you have a number that means the same thing on every name on the screen.

The mental shortcut worth carrying is √T for the common tenors: a month is about 0.29, a quarter about 0.5, a year is 1. So a $100 stock at 30 vol has a one-sigma move of about $8.60 over a month, $15 over a quarter and $30 over a year. Those three numbers, held in the head, convert most of a screen without a calculator.

The same arithmetic run across expiries is where the intuition really lands. On one stock with one strike spacing, consecutive strikes are 3.18 sigma apart on a one-day option and 0.17 sigma apart on a one-year one. A weekly chain is a blunt instrument by construction, and that has nothing to do with liquidity — it is geometry.

Set the spot to 25 and predict how many strikes fit inside a one-sigma move on the monthly. Then set it to 500 and see the same screen become a different instrument.

Check yourself

A $40 stock at 45 volatility, weekly expiry, 2.5-point strikes. Roughly how far apart are the strikes in sigmas?

About one sigma per strike. Which tells you immediately that there are perhaps three strikes worth quoting in that expiry, that a one-strike-wide spread there is a very different animal from a one-strike-wide spread on an index, and that the delta will move in large jumps as you walk down the column. All from one multiplication done before you looked at any prices.
03

Delta is the ruler, and the rule of thumb has a shelf life

The 16-delta option is roughly a one-sigma strike. Roughly holds for about three months, and the put side was never symmetric with the call side.

The second conversion is easier because the screen does it for you. Delta is printed, it runs from zero to one on every name and every expiry, and it is the closest thing a chain has to a universal coordinate.

The rule everyone half-knows is that a 16-delta option is about a one-sigma strike, because 16% is the tail beyond one standard deviation of a normal. It is a good rule and it is worth knowing exactly how good. On a $100 stock at 30 volatility the 16-delta call sits at 1.06 sigma at a week, 1.13 sigma at a month and 1.24 sigma at three months — so inside a quarter it is accurate to about a tenth of a sigma, which is fine. At a year it is 1.56 sigma and the rule has stopped being useful.

The part almost nobody mentions is the asymmetry. Over the same tenors the 16-delta put sits at 0.94, 0.88, 0.80 and 0.64 sigma — it moves the other way. The lognormal is not symmetric, so equal deltas are not equal distances, and a "16-delta strangle" is not centered on anything.

One more, because it catches people constantly: the at-the-money call's delta is 0.53 here, not 0.50. Scanning a column for the 50-delta to find the money will put you one strike low.

Try this

Drag the expiry out to a year and watch the rule of thumb fail. Predict first whether the call or the put drifts further.

Check yourself

Someone asks for "the 25-delta call" on a one-year chain. How far out is that?

A delta names a strike, not a distance. That is exactly why desks quote in deltas — a 25-delta option has roughly comparable character across expiries even though it sits at very different moneyness. Trouble only starts when someone treats the delta as if it were a fixed number of standard deviations, which is the mistake this chapter exists to prevent.
04

What each column is actually telling you

The IV column is a single number chosen out of a range, and the width of that range is the bid-offer.

A chain prints a lot of columns and they are not equally trustworthy. The one that misleads most is the one that looks most authoritative.

Implied volatility is computed from a price, and the screen has at least two prices. Take a 105 call on a $100 stock, thirty days out, genuinely worth 1.662 at 30 volatility. Quote it two cents wide and the implied volatility runs from 29.90% to 30.10% — a fifth of a point, fine. Quote it ten cents wide, which is unremarkable on a single name, and the band is 29.51% to 30.49%: a 0.98 volatility point range, and the column shows you one number from inside it. Twenty cents wide and it is nearly two points.

So the practical rule is that an IV column is only as precise as the spread it was computed from, and comparing two names' implieds to a decimal place is usually comparing their liquidity. Page 05 takes this much further — on a short-dated wing a single tick can be eleven volatility points.

Volume and open interest answer different questions and get confused constantly. Volume is what traded today; open interest is what is still outstanding. A strike with enormous volume and no open interest was traded and closed — somebody was in and out. Enormous open interest and no volume is a position somebody is sitting on, and it is the one that matters for pinning.

The mid is not a price. It is the average of two prices, one of which you can hit and one of which you can lift, and on a wide market it is a number nobody will trade. Every apparent edge under about half a spread is an artifact of quoting the mid.

Check yourself

Two names both show 32.4% implied on the at-the-money. One is quoted a penny wide, the other twenty cents. What can you conclude?

The number is only as good as the spread behind it. The fourth answer is the tempting one and it is exactly backwards: illiquidity does not merely make a market harder to trade, it makes the derived quantities imprecise, because they are computed from a midpoint that is a guess. Any screen comparison across names should carry the spread alongside the implied, and most do not.
05

Skew and term structure, read by eye

Two shapes on a screen, both readable in a glance once you know what you are looking for.

Scan the IV column downward. If the numbers fall as the strike rises, the column is skewed, and how fast they fall is how skewed. On the example chain here — thirty days, 28% at the money — the 90 strike shows 32.10% and the 110 shows 24.99%, so the column drops seven volatility points across four strikes.

The standard way to compress that into one number is the risk reversal: the 25-delta put's implied minus the 25-delta call's. On this surface it is 3.77 points at thirty days, 6.47 at ninety and 12.29 at a year, because a skew defined in log-moneyness reaches further out as the strikes spread.

Now scan across expiries instead, at the money. A flat term structure is a stock with nothing scheduled. A front month standing well above the back is the screen telling you a date exists — and the size of the step says what the market has priced for it. With a 28% baseline and one event worth a 7% move, the quoted at-the-money runs 82.1% at three days, 57.8% at a week, 37.2% at a month and 29.7% at six months. The event has not moved; its share of the remaining variance has. Page 03 is that arithmetic in full.

Try this

Put an event worth 7% into the term structure and predict what the three-day implied does before you look. Most people guess about 40%.

Check yourself

The front week shows 82% implied and the six-month shows 30%. What is that?

A date, not a mispricing. The giveaway is the size and the shape: an 82% front against a 30% back is far too large to be an opinion about ordinary volatility and far too neat to be an error. Before ever concluding that a front month is expensive, find out what is in it — earnings, an FDA decision, an index reconstitution — because the arithmetic that makes it look expensive is the arithmetic of dividing by a small number.
06

The five-second scan

A procedure, in order. It is short because the point is that it fits in the time before somebody expects an answer.

Everything above is a conversion. This is the order to do them in, and it is worth doing in the same order every time until it stops being a list and becomes a glance.

One — get the sigma. Spot times the at-the-money implied times √T. One number, and every strike distance on the screen is now readable. On the chain below it is printed at the top of the panel, and after a hundred chains you will be computing it before the page has finished loading.

Two — locate the money. Not the 50-delta; the strike nearest the forward. On short-dated equity chains they are nearly the same, and on anything long-dated or high-carry they are not.

Three — read the shape of the IV column. Not the values, the slope. Falling as strikes rise is ordinary equity skew. Falling then rising is a smile, which means the wings are bid on both sides and usually means jump risk. Flat is either an index in a calm week or a screen showing you a model rather than a market.

Four — check the front against the back. One step at the front is an event. A smooth slope is a view.

Five — look at the spreads before believing any of it. Everything in steps one to four was computed from mids.

The chain below tags every strike as body, shoulder or wing by its distance in sigmas, which is the classification the scan is really producing. It is not standard terminology; the distinction it draws is completely standard.

Try this

Cut the expiry to three days without changing anything else, and count how many strikes are left that are not wings.

Check yourself

Why does the scan start with the sigma rather than with the prices?

It is the unit everything else is measured in. Reading prices before you know the scale is like reading a map with no key: you can see that one number is bigger than another and you cannot tell whether the difference matters. It is also the cheapest step — one multiplication — which is why it goes first.

Drill the conversions

Sigmas, strike distances and deltas, at the speed the screen moves.

Question 1 · chapter 02

A $100 stock at 30 volatility, thirty days out. How big is a one-sigma move?

$8.60, and the third answer is the interesting wrong one. Dividing the volatility by twelve rather than by √12 is the single most common units error in the subject. Time enters through its square root, always — a month is 0.287 of a year in volatility terms, not 0.083.
Question 2 · chapter 02

Same stock and expiry, five-point strikes. How many strikes fit inside one standard deviation?

1.7 strikes per sigma. The third answer inverts it, which is worth doing deliberately once: 0.58 is the number of sigmas per strike, and both quantities are useful as long as you know which one you are holding.
Question 3 · chapter 03

How good is "the 16-delta is the one-sigma strike"?

A short-dated rule, and the put side is not its mirror. The 16-delta put runs 0.94, 0.88, 0.80 and 0.64 sigma over the same tenors — moving the opposite way. The asymmetry is the lognormal, and it is why a delta-defined strangle is not centered on the spot.
Question 4 · chapter 04

A 105 call worth 1.66 is quoted ten cents wide. What is the implied volatility?

A band, not a number. Ten cents is unremarkable on a single name and it is already a point of volatility. Comparing two screens' implieds to a decimal place, without their spreads beside them, is usually comparing their liquidity.
Question 5 · chapter 05

The front week is 82% implied and the six-month is 30%. What is the most likely explanation?

A date. With a 28% baseline and one event worth a 7% move, the quoted at-the-money runs 82.1% at three days and 29.7% at six months — the event has not changed, only its share of the remaining variance. Find out what is scheduled before calling anything expensive.
Question 6 · chapter 06

A strike shows enormous open interest and almost no volume today. What does that tell you?

An existing position, not today's activity. The third answer describes the opposite pattern — big volume with no open interest is somebody in and out. The two columns answer different questions and the confusion between them is common enough to be worth a deliberate check: today, or outstanding?

Every chain on this page is generated from a smooth parametric surface, which makes it cleaner than any real screen. A real chain has strikes with no market at all, implieds computed from stale mids, and a spot that has moved since the last print — and the wings, where this page's arithmetic is least reliable, are exactly where those problems are worst. The sigma conversion in chapter 02 uses the at-the-money implied for every strike, which is a deliberate simplification: on a skewed surface each strike has its own volatility, and the correct distance uses that one. It changes the numbers in the wings by a fair amount and changes none of the judgements. And every implied volatility here is derived from a mid, which page 05 argues is not a price at all.
