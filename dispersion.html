Correlation, whether you meant to or not

Twenty stocks averaging 39% volatility, bundled into an index, produce an index that can be anything from 9% to 39% depending on one number nobody quotes directly. Trade the index against its own members and that number is the only thing you own.

Start here

This page needs the smile vocabulary from page 02 and the variance machinery from page 09 — dispersion is almost always traded in variance rather than volatility, for reasons chapter 03 makes concrete.

  1. Chapter 01 is one line of algebra and the rest of the page is consequences. Index variance is a weighted double sum, and the off-diagonal terms all carry a correlation.
  2. Implied correlation is not quoted; it is inferred. Chapter 02 backs it out of an index price, which means it inherits every assumption in that inversion.
  3. The trade is short correlation whatever you call it. Chapter 03 shows the P&L is a straight line in realized correlation, crossing zero exactly at the implied level.
  4. Chapter 05 is the one that matters. Correlation is bounded above by one, and it gets there in precisely the scenario the position cannot survive.
Notation — every symbol used on this page

01

An index is not the average of its parts

Twenty names averaging 39% volatility make an index of 9% or 39%, and the whole range is one parameter.

Take twenty stocks, equally weighted, with volatilities running from 22% to 56% — an average of 39.10%. What is the volatility of the index they form? The question has no answer until you say how they move together, and the range of possible answers is enormous.

The algebra is a double sum. Index variance adds up every pair of names, each pair contributing its two weights, its two volatilities, and the correlation between them. The n terms where a name meets itself carry a correlation of one and cannot be diversified. The n²−n terms where two different names meet carry the correlation between them, and those are the terms that do all the work.

Run the extremes. At ρ = 0 every cross term vanishes and only the diagonal survives, giving an index volatility of 9.05% — a fifth of the average name, purely from averaging away independent noise. At ρ = 1 every term survives at full weight and the index volatility is exactly 39.10%, the weighted average, which is also the mathematical ceiling. At a realistic ρ = 0.30 the index sits at 22.71%.

So an index option is not a bet on its members' volatility. It is a bet on their volatility and on how much of it survives aggregation, and the second half is much larger than people expect. Between ρ = 0.15 and ρ = 0.50 — an unremarkable range — index volatility moves from 17.29% to 28.38%, with every single-name volatility held perfectly fixed.

The lower panel checks that this is not just algebra. It simulates twenty genuinely correlated price paths, measures the realized volatility of the basket they produce, and compares it with the formula: 24.21% simulated against 24.25% predicted, a gap of four hundredths of a point. The identity is real, and it holds on paths rather than only on paper.

σI² = Σi wi²σi²  +  ρ · Σi≠j wiwjσiσj

Reads as: index variance is what each name contributes on its own, plus a correlation-weighted share of every pairing between different names. The first sum is small because the weights are squared; the second is large because there are so many pairs.

Check yourself

Every stock in an index doubles its volatility overnight, and correlation is unchanged. What happens to index volatility?

It exactly doubles. The formula is homogeneous of degree one in the volatilities — pull a common factor out of every σ and it comes straight through to σI. That is worth knowing precisely because it isolates what dispersion is really trading: not the level of volatility, which passes through untouched, but the correlation, which is the only thing left.
Derivation — where the double sum comes from, and the one-number simplification

The index return is the weighted sum of the component returns, so its variance is the variance of a sum.

Var(Σ wiri) = ΣiΣj wiwj Cov(ri, rj)

Reads as: the variance of a portfolio is every pairwise covariance, weighted twice. Nothing here is specific to options; it is the same identity that produces portfolio theory.

Write each covariance as ρijσiσj and split the sum into the terms where i = j and the terms where it does not.

σI² = Σi wi²σi² + Σi≠j wiwjρijσiσj

Reads as: the diagonal is undiversifiable and the off-diagonal is where correlation lives. With n names there are n diagonal terms and n²−n off-diagonal ones, so for any decent-sized index the second sum dominates.

Everything on this page then replaces the whole correlation matrix with a single average ρ. That is a real simplification and it is the one the market makes too, because a single number is what can be quoted and hedged.

Where it breaks. One ρ cannot describe a market where sectors move together and against each other, and the failure is not symmetric: a basket with two tightly correlated blocks has the same average pairwise correlation as one that is uniformly loosely correlated, and behaves nothing like it in a stress. Dispersion books hedged on average correlation carry exactly this exposure, and it shows up as an unexplained residual in precisely the moves that matter. The second simplification is weights: index weights drift with prices, so a basket matched today is not matched in three months.

02

Nobody quotes correlation, so you invert for it

Take the index price, take the component prices, and solve the formula backwards. What comes out is a number with opinions baked into it.

There is no correlation screen. What exists is an index option market and twenty single-name option markets, and the bridge between them is chapter 01's identity read from right to left. Fix every σi at its implied volatility, fix σI at the index's implied volatility, and there is exactly one ρ that makes the equation true. That number is implied correlation, and it is the closest thing the market has to a price for co-movement.

The mapping is steep, which is the first practical thing to know. On our basket, an index quoted at 20% implies 0.220; at 25% it implies 0.375; at 28% it implies 0.485. Three volatility points of index move a correlation estimate by more than a tenth, so a stale index mark or a mismatched snapshot time produces a correlation number that is badly wrong while looking perfectly plausible.

The second thing to know is that the inversion inherits every choice you made on the way in. Which expiry? Which strike — at-the-money volatility, or the variance strike from page 09, which carries a skew premium? Do you use all five hundred names or the top fifty as a proxy? Each answer produces a different implied correlation from the same market, and they do not agree to two decimal places. A quoted implied correlation without its recipe is not a number.

The lower panel splits index variance into the part that survives no matter what — the diagonal — and the part that correlation controls. On a twenty-name basket the diagonal is small: at ρ = 0.3 it supplies about a tenth of index variance, and the rest is the correlation term. That is the mechanical reason index volatility is so sensitive to a parameter nobody observes.

ρimp = (σI² − Σ wi²σi²) / Σi≠j wiwjσiσj

Reads as: take the index variance, remove the part that cannot be diversified, and express what is left as a fraction of the most it could possibly be. That fraction is the implied correlation.

Check yourself

Two desks report implied correlation on the same index on the same day: 0.38 and 0.51. Who is wrong?

Possibly neither. Implied correlation is a derived quantity, not an observed one, and the derivation has at least three free choices in it. The practical rule is that a correlation number is only comparable with another number built the same way — which is why desks track their own series rather than a published one, and why cross-desk arguments about "the level of correlation" are usually arguments about methodology.
03

The trade, and what it is actually long

Sell index variance, buy the components' variance, and the volatility level cancels. What is left is a straight line in realized correlation.

Dispersion is the trade that expresses chapter 01 directly. Sell variance on the index, buy variance on each of its members in matched size, and hold both to expiry. If the names move around a lot but independently, the components pay and the index does not, and you win. If everything moves together, the index pays more than the components and you lose.

The reason it is traded in variance rather than volatility is chapter 01's identity, which is exactly linear in variance and a mess in volatility. Match variance notionals and the position's P&L reduces to the difference between implied and realized correlation, multiplied by the size of the off-diagonal sum. That is a clean exposure. Try the same thing with volatility swaps or straddles and the relationship is approximate and drifts with spot.

The figure runs it. With implied correlation at 0.35 — an index implied of 24.25% against those components — the P&L line crosses zero at realized correlation of exactly 0.35 and is a straight line either side. Realized correlation of 0.15 and the short index leg wins comfortably; realized 0.60 and it loses about two and a half times what it made in the good case, because the line keeps going.

Three practical things the figure cannot show. Trading twenty single-name variance swaps means twenty bid–offers against one, so the entry cost is asymmetric and the trade needs a decent gap between implied and expected realized just to break even. Weights drift, so a matched basket stops being matched. And the components have their own wing problems from page 09 chapter 04 — single-name variance is exactly the product whose replication is most fragile, and you are long twenty of them.

P&L ∝ (ρimp − ρrealized) · Σi≠j wiwjσiσj

Reads as: you are paid the difference between the correlation you sold and the correlation that happened, scaled by how much correlation there was to sell. Nothing about the level of volatility survives into this expression.

Check yourself

You are short index variance and long component variance. The market crashes: every single-name volatility triples. What happened to your P&L?

Correlation decides it, and in a crash correlation is the thing that moves against you. The first answer is the seductive one because being long twenty variance swaps into a crash sounds wonderful, and it would be — except that you are short the index's variance in matched size, and the index's variance rises by more than the components' when everything moves together. Chapter 05 puts numbers on how much more.
04

Why implied correlation is persistently rich

Two large, opposite, structural flows push on the two legs. The gap between them is the trade's carry, and also the reason it is crowded.

Implied correlation has historically sat above subsequently realized correlation more often than not. That is a claim about a persistent premium, and a persistent premium needs a persistent reason, not a story about sentiment. The reason here is flow, and it is the same argument page 04's bridge chapter makes about the surface generally: durable one-way demand shows up as a price.

The two flows push in opposite directions on the two legs of the same trade.

Index volatility is bid. Institutions hedging equity portfolios buy index puts, because one index hedge is cheaper and simpler to administer than five hundred single-name hedges, and because a mandate usually specifies portfolio-level protection. That demand is structural, price-insensitive within limits, and one-directional. It lifts index implied volatility.

Single-name volatility is offered. Overwriting programmes, structured-product issuance and yield-enhancement mandates all sell single-name upside. That supply is also structural and one-directional, and it depresses single-name implied volatility.

Now put them into chapter 02's inversion. Index implied up, component implied down, and the implied correlation that reconciles them must rise — even if nobody involved held any opinion about correlation at all. The number is a residual, and residuals absorb whatever the flows do to the inputs.

Two honest qualifications, because this is exactly the kind of claim that gets stated too confidently. The premium is not a free lunch: it is compensation for taking the other side of a hedge that pays off in crashes, and chapter 05 is what you are being paid for. And it is not constant — the flows change with issuance cycles and with how much index hedging is fashionable, so a strategy calibrated to one regime's premium can find it has gone.

Check yourself

A large bank stops issuing autocallables, cutting the supply of single-name volatility. All else equal, what happens to implied correlation?

It falls. Implied correlation is the residual that reconciles two option markets, so anything that moves either market moves it — including events with no informational content about how stocks co-move at all. This is why the number is better read as a flow indicator than as a forecast, and why "correlation is cheap" is often a statement about issuance calendars.
05

Correlation goes to one exactly when you need it not to

The variable is bounded above, the position is short it, and the bound is reached in the one scenario the position was never sized for.

Every risk on this page collapses into a single sentence. A dispersion position is short correlation; correlation has a hard ceiling at one; and markets travel toward that ceiling in exactly the episodes where everything else is going wrong too. The trade's worst case is not a tail of a smooth distribution — it is a regime change, and it arrives with the rest of the portfolio's worst case.

Put numbers on it with our basket. In a calm market at ρ = 0.25, index volatility is 21.06%. Move correlation alone to 0.90, touching no single-name volatility at all, and index volatility goes to 37.20% — a rise of 16.14 points produced entirely by co-movement. Now do what actually happens and let single-name volatilities rise as well, say by 1.8×: index volatility reaches 66.97%, up 45.91 points from calm.

Watch what that does to the two legs. The short index variance leg loses on a move of 4,041 variance units. The long component leg, which is supposed to be the hedge, gains only 3,425. The trade is not volatility-neutral in a crash because it was never a volatility trade — the legs only cancel while correlation is where you left it.

Two more failure modes worth naming, both of which turn a bad outcome into an unmanageable one.

The average is not the matrix. A single ρ cannot distinguish a market that has become uniformly more correlated from one that has split into two blocks. Books hedged on average correlation carry the difference as an unhedged residual, and it is largest in stress, when the correlation structure is changing shape rather than just level.

Liquidity leaves both legs at once. Single-name variance is the product from page 09 chapter 07 whose replication depends on a put wing that gaps and widens in a crash. Twenty of those, against one index position, is a lot of fragility concentrated in one direction.

And the arithmetic check from chapter 02 becomes a live risk control here. Because index volatility can never exceed the weighted average of its components, an index quoted at 39.5% against components averaging 39.10% implies a correlation of 1.0217 — which is impossible, and therefore evidence that a mark is stale, a proxy basket is wrong, or something is genuinely dislocated. It is one line of arithmetic and it is worth running every day.

σI ≤ Σi wiσi    always

Reads as: an index can never be more volatile than the weighted average of its members. Equality needs perfect correlation, so a quote above that line is not a price, it is a broken input.

Try this

Take correlation to 0.95 while leaving the volatility multiplier at 1. Predict whether the long component leg gains anything at all.

Check yourself

Why is a dispersion book's worst day usually also the firm's worst day?

It is short the thing that defines a crash. A crash is a correlation event — that is close to what the word means at portfolio level. So a short-correlation position is not merely unlucky in those episodes, it is structurally aligned with them, and its returns look wonderful right up until the day they explain themselves. Sizing it as though its historical volatility described its risk is the specific mistake.

Question 1 · chapter 01

Twenty names average 39% volatility. What is the highest the index volatility can possibly be?

The weighted average is a hard ceiling. It is also a live arbitrage check — an index quoted above it implies correlation above one, which means an input is broken.
Question 2 · chapter 01

The same twenty names, but correlation is zero. Roughly what is the index volatility?

About 9%. Independence removes the cross terms, not the individual variances. With equal weights that floor falls like one over the square root of the number of names, which is why it is small but never zero.
Question 3 · chapter 02

Why is implied correlation so sensitive to the index mark?

The correlation term carries almost all of index variance. A small residual computed as a difference between two large-ish numbers amplifies any error in either, so stale or mistimed marks produce plausible-looking nonsense.
Question 4 · chapter 03

Why is dispersion traded in variance rather than volatility?

The algebra is linear in variance and not in volatility. This is the practical reason page 09 comes before this one — dispersion is the clearest application of why the square is the tradeable object.
Question 5 · chapter 04

What makes implied correlation persistently rich?

Flow on both legs, pushing the same way. Neither side of that flow has any opinion about correlation, which is the point — the number records a supply-and-demand imbalance in two separate markets, and it is compensation for taking the other side of a crash hedge.
Question 6 · chapter 05

Correlation moves from 0.25 to 0.90 with every single-name volatility unchanged. What happens to index volatility on our basket?

21% to 37%, from correlation alone. This is the single most useful number on the page for sizing: it is what a short-correlation position loses before any volatility has moved at all, and it is why historical P&L volatility badly understates the risk.

Every figure here replaces a full correlation matrix with one average pairwise correlation, and every basket is equally weighted with a smooth spread of volatilities. Real indices are capitalisation-weighted with a long tail of small names, real correlation matrices have sector structure that a single number cannot express, and real dispersion books are traded on a subset of components chosen for liquidity rather than on the whole index. The flow argument in chapter 04 describes a mechanism and a direction; the size of the premium varies with the issuance cycle and is not a constant. Nothing here is a forecast of correlation, and the historical persistence of the premium is not a guarantee of it.
