The price of uncertainty

Black–Scholes is usually taught as a formula to memorize. It is actually a claim about what a hedge can reproduce. This page builds it from random paths and never asks you to take anything on faith.

M

The math, and why each piece is the right tool

Seven ideas. Each is a small thing on its own, and together they are the entire mathematical content of what follows.

Options mathematics has a reputation for difficulty that it doesn't deserve. What it actually has is a lot of notation, most of which is shorthand for something you already understand intuitively. This chapter unpacks each symbol you'll meet, shows what it's for, and — more importantly — shows why it is the natural thing to reach for rather than an arbitrary convention.

If you already know this material, skim the figures and move on. If you don't, everything after this chapter will be readable.

M · 1

Why e, and why logarithms

Money that grows 10% a year becomes 1.10× after a year. Money that grows 5% twice becomes 1.05² = 1.1025×, slightly more, because the second period earns on the first period's gain. Compound more often and you get more, but with rapidly diminishing returns — and the sequence does not run away to infinity. It converges, and the number it converges to is er.

That is the whole reason e appears everywhere in finance. Writing growth as erT is just saying "compounded continuously," which is both the mathematically cleanest convention and, for most purposes, indistinguishable from daily compounding.

Run it backwards and you get discounting: a dollar arriving at time T is worth e−rT today. Every price on this page is a future amount multiplied by that factor.

The natural log is the inverse operation, and it earns its place for one reason: it turns multiplication into addition. Stock returns compound multiplicatively — up 10% then down 10% is ×1.10×0.90 — which is awkward. Take logs and those become +0.0953 and −0.1054, which simply add. Since adding independent random numbers is a well-understood operation and multiplying them isn't, the entire model is built in log space. That single choice is why the terminal distribution comes out lognormal rather than normal.

M · 2

Expectation, variance, and what a standard deviation is for

E[X], the expectation, is the average value of a random quantity weighted by how likely each outcome is. It is not a prediction of what will happen; it's the long-run average of many repetitions. An option's fair value is an expectation, which is why an option can be correctly priced and still expire worthless.

Variance measures spread: the average squared distance from the mean. The squaring looks like an arbitrary choice and isn't — it's what makes variance additive across independent events, which is the property section M·4 depends on entirely.

The catch is that variance is in the wrong units. If prices are in dollars, variance is in dollars-squared, which means nothing. Take the square root and you get the standard deviation, back in dollars and directly comparable to a price. Volatility is a standard deviation — specifically, the standard deviation of returns over one year. That's the entire definition.

M · 3

The bell curve twice: n(x) and N(x)

Two functions appear constantly and are easy to confuse because they describe the same distribution.

n(x) is the density: the height of the bell curve at x. It is not a probability. The probability of landing in an interval is the area under the curve over that interval, which is why the total area is exactly 1.

N(x) is the cumulative version: the probability of landing at or below x, which is the running total of that area. It climbs from 0 to 1 and passes through exactly 0.5 at x = 0.

So when you see N(d₂) in the pricing formula, read it as: the shaded area to the left of nothing in particular — it is the probability that a standard normal draw exceeds the exercise threshold. The two graphs below are the same information; drag the slider and watch the shaded area on top become the height on the bottom.

M · 4

Why time enters as √T and not T

This is the single most useful piece of arithmetic in options, and the reasoning is three lines.

Split a year into independent daily moves. Variances of independent things add. So the variance over 252 days is 252 times the daily variance. But standard deviation is the square root of variance, so the standard deviation over the year is √252 ≈ 15.87 times the daily one — not 252 times.

Var(total) = T × σ²  ⟹  sd(total) = σ√T

Reads as: uncertainty grows with the square root of time. Four times the horizon, only twice the spread.

Randomness partially cancels itself. Over many periods some moves are up and some down, so the total wanders outward far more slowly than it would if every move pushed the same way. That is why a 30% annual volatility corresponds to roughly a 1.9% daily move rather than a 0.12% one, and why every conversion between timeframes carries a square root.

It is also why σ√T — total volatility — is the model's only real ruler. Not σ alone and not T alone, but the product. Two options with the same σ√T are, up to scaling, the same option.

M · 5

Derivatives are sensitivities; second derivatives are curvature

A derivative ∂V/∂S answers one question: if S moves a little, how much does V move? It is a slope. The curly ∂ rather than a straight d only signals that other variables (time, vol) are being held still.

That is exactly what delta is. If delta is 0.6, then a $1 move in the stock moves the option about $0.60. Nothing more mysterious than that.

The second derivative asks: how fast is the slope itself changing? That is gamma, and it measures curvature. A straight line has zero curvature and therefore zero gamma; a sharply bending curve has a lot.

Put them together and you can approximate the curve near a point without repricing anything — a Taylor expansion:

V(S + ΔS) ≈ V(S) + Δ · ΔS + ½ Γ · (ΔS)²

Reads as: new value ≈ old value, plus the slope times the move, plus a correction for the bend.

Two features of that second term matter enormously. It is squared, so it contributes the same whether the move is up or down — long gamma doesn't care about direction. And it carries a ½, which comes from the fact that curvature accumulates gradually across the move rather than applying all at once. Drag the point below and watch the straight-line estimate fail while the parabola holds.

M · 6

Why curvature is worth money

Here is the deepest idea on this page, and it takes one sentence: for a curved function, the average of the outcomes is not the outcome of the average. Formally, E[f(X)] ≠ f(E[X]) — Jensen's inequality.

An option payoff is convex: it bends upward at the strike. So if you average the payoff across all the places the stock might finish, you get more than the payoff at the stock's average finishing place. The excess is pure convexity value, and it is the reason options have any time value at all.

The clean demonstration: take an at-the-money call with no drift. The stock's expected finishing price is exactly the strike, so the payoff at the average outcome is exactly zero. Yet the average payoff is not zero — it's the option's whole premium. At 20% vol over a year, on a $100 stock, the average payoff is about 7.95 while the payoff-at-the-average is 0.00. Every cent of that option's value is Jensen's inequality.

Widen the distribution and the gap widens with it. That is vega. Bend the payoff more sharply and the gap widens too. That is gamma. Convexity and volatility are the two ingredients of option value, and this figure is where they meet.

The same effect running the other direction explains the −σ²/2 in chapter one. The logarithm is concave, bending the opposite way, so averaging works against you: volatility drags the median of a stock price below its mean by exactly σ²T/2.

M · 7

The one strange fact: squared randomness doesn't vanish

Ordinary calculus rests on the idea that if you take a small enough step, the squared step is negligible. Halve dt and (dt)² falls by four — it disappears faster than everything else, so you drop it.

Random increments break this. A Brownian step dW over an interval dt has standard deviation √dt, not dt. So its square is about dt — the same order as the time step itself, not smaller. Squared randomness refuses to vanish. Compactly:

(dW)² = dt

Reads as: the squared size of a random shock is not negligible — it is exactly as significant as the passage of time.

This isn't a formal trick; it's measurable, and the figure below measures it. Take any path, chop it into n pieces, square each increment, and add them up. The total lands on T — the elapsed time — and it does so for every path, not just on average. Individual paths differ wildly; their accumulated squared wiggle does not. Increase n and the scatter across paths collapses.

Two consequences follow, and they are the two that matter. First, when you expand a function of a random process you must keep the second-order term, which is why Itô's lemma carries an extra ½σ²S²(∂²V/∂S²) that ordinary calculus wouldn't. Second — and this is the practical one — volatility is observable from a path in a way that drift is not. You can measure accumulated squared movement precisely over a short window; estimating drift to comparable accuracy takes decades of data. That asymmetry is why a model that depends on σ and not μ is a usable model, and why implied volatility is what gets quoted.

Check yourself

A stock's price is currently 100 and its expected price in one year is also 100. A one-year 100-strike call must therefore be worth about zero — after all, on average it finishes exactly at the money. What's wrong with this reasoning?

Jensen's inequality. The payoff at the average outcome is genuinely zero. But you don't receive the payoff at the average — you receive the average of the payoffs, and because max(S−K,0) floors losses at zero while leaving gains unbounded, that average is strictly positive. At 20 vol over a year it's about 7.95 on a $100 stock. Interest rates matter too, but even at r = 0 the option is worth 7.95, so rates are a second-order correction to an effect that is entirely about curvature.
00

What you actually own

Before pricing anything, be precise about the contract. Almost every confusion downstream is really a confusion about this.

An option is a right without an obligation. A call gives you the right to buy 100 shares at a fixed strike K; a put gives you the right to sell at K. You pay for that right up front, and that payment — the premium — is the only thing you can lose if you bought it.

At expiry the arithmetic is not negotiable. A call is worth max(ST − K, 0): if the stock finished above the strike you exercise and capture the difference, and if it finished below you walk away and the contract is worth exactly zero. A put is worth max(K − ST, 0). That's the entire payoff. Everything else in this page exists to answer one question: what should the premium be today, given that this is what happens at the end?

Two vocabulary items you'll need. Intrinsic value is what the option would pay if expiry were right now — the kink formula applied to today's price. Extrinsic value, sometimes called time value, is everything else in the premium: the amount you're paying for the possibility that things get better before expiry. An option is in the money when it has intrinsic value, out of the money when it has none, and at the money when S is sitting on K.

Notice the asymmetry in the figure below when you switch from long to short. The buyer's loss is capped at the premium and their gain is not capped at all. The seller has the mirror image: a capped gain and, on a call, an uncapped loss. That asymmetry is why option sellers hedge and why the rest of this page is largely about hedging.

Check yourself

You buy a 100-strike call for a premium of 5. At expiry the stock is at 103. What is your profit or loss?

−2. The option is worth 3 at expiry, which is real money you get back — it did not expire worthless. But you paid 5 for it, so you're down 2. This is why "the option finished in the money" and "the trade made money" are different statements, and why the breakeven on a long call is K + premium = 103, not the strike.
01

One path, and the two numbers that shape it

Before there is a price there is a model of how the stock moves. Everything downstream inherits its assumptions.

The model says a stock's log price takes independent normal steps. Not the price — the log of it. That single choice does most of the work: it keeps the price positive, it makes returns rather than dollars the thing that's stable across price levels, and it makes the terminal distribution lognormal, which is what eventually gets integrated to produce the formula.

Two parameters steer it. Drift μ tilts the whole path. Volatility σ sets the width of each step — annualized, so a step over dt years has standard deviation σ√dt. Turn σ down and the path becomes a smooth exponential curve. Turn it up and the destination becomes almost unknowable.

Watch what happens to the drift slider in particular, and remember what it does to this picture. In chapter six it will stop mattering entirely, and that is the whole theorem.

Try this

Set drift to zero and volatility to 90%, then press New path five or six times. The paths wander enormously — but watch the realized vol readout in the panel. It rarely equals the 90% you typed in.

That gap is the entire problem with volatility. σ is a property of the process; realized vol is a property of one sample. You can only ever observe the second and you must trade the first.

Check yourself

You double σ from 20% to 40%, holding the horizon fixed. What happens to the standard deviation of the stock's total log return over that horizon?

It doubles. Variance over the horizon is σ²T, so standard deviation is σ√T — linear in σ and square-root in time. The square root is the part people get wrong: doubling the horizon multiplies the standard deviation by only √2 ≈ 1.41, not 2. This is why a 30-vol annual number implies a daily move of roughly 30%/√252 ≈ 1.9%, not 30%/252.
Derivation — why the log, and where the −σ²/2 comes from

The model starts by assuming the stock's proportional change over an instant is a constant drift plus a random shock:

dS = μS dt + σS dW

Reads as: the change in the stock over an instant is a steady drift plus a random shock, both proportional to the current price. Proportional is the key word — a $1 move means something different on a $10 stock than a $500 one.

The natural move is to take logs to make that additive. But ordinary calculus gives the wrong answer here, because dW has the strange property that (dW)² = dt rather than being negligible. Random shocks don't vanish when you square them — that is what separates stochastic calculus from the ordinary kind.

Applying Itô's lemma to f = ln S, and keeping the second-order term that ordinary calculus would throw away:

d(ln S) = (1/S)dS − (1/2)(1/S²)(dS)² = (μ − σ²/2) dt + σ dW

Reads as: in log space the moves are ordinary and additive — but the drift is reduced by σ²/2, which is the price of having taken a logarithm of something volatile.

So the log price does take ordinary independent normal steps — but with drift μ − σ²/2, not μ. That correction is not a fudge factor. It exists because averaging is not the same operation before and after taking a logarithm: a stock that gains 50% then loses 50% is down 25%, not flat. Volatility drags the median below the mean, and σ²/2 is exactly how much.

Integrating over the life of the option gives the form used in every simulation on this page:

ST = S · exp[ (μ − σ²/2)T + σ√T · Z ],   Z ~ Normal(0,1)

Reads as: take today’s price, apply the drift over T years, then multiply by a random factor whose size is set by σ√T. Z is a single standard normal draw. This one line generates every path on this page.

And the sanity check that the correction is right: E[ST] = S·eμT, with the σ²/2 cancelling exactly. The mean grows at μ; the median grows at μ − σ²/2. Chapter two's histogram shows those two numbers sitting in different places.

02

Ten thousand endings

Run the same process again and again, keep only where each path finished, and a shape appears that nobody designed.

A single path tells you nothing. The distribution of endings tells you everything, because a European option only ever looks at where the stock finished. The path in between is irrelevant to the payoff — though, as chapter six shows, it is very relevant to whether your hedge survived the journey.

Press run. Each path that completes drops its terminal price into the histogram on the right. After a few hundred, the bars stop wobbling and settle onto a curve: the lognormal density, which is what you get when the log of a thing is normal. The smooth curve was never fitted to the bars. It is the limit they were always heading toward.

Try this

Run 400 paths at 30 vol and note where the histogram's peak sits relative to the starting price of 100. Now push volatility to 80% and run again on a cleared chart. The peak slides down even though the drift is unchanged and positive.

Compare the sample mean against the median in the panel. Higher vol pushes them apart without moving the mean at all.

Check yourself

In the simulation the sample mean sits noticeably above the median, and the gap widens as you raise σ. Why?

The skew comes from the exponential. Log returns are symmetric, but the price is e raised to that, and the exponential stretches the upside far more than the downside. The stock can never go below zero, yet it can in principle triple. So the distribution has a long right tail that drags the mean above the median, and the gap is exactly the σ²T/2 term from chapter one's derivation. Positive drift shifts both statistics up together and doesn't explain a gap between them.
03

The price is an average, taken carefully

Apply the payoff to every ending, average the results, discount once. Watch the number walk onto Black–Scholes.

Here is the whole pricing idea in one line: the option is worth the average payoff it produces, discounted back to today — provided you average under the right probability measure. That proviso is doing serious work, and it's where most explanations wave their hands, so here is the honest version.

You cannot average under the real-world drift. If you could, two people who disagreed about a stock's expected return would compute different option prices, and one of them could be arbitraged. Instead you average under the risk-neutral measure, in which every asset drifts at the risk-free rate r − q. This isn't a claim that stocks really return the T-bill rate. It's a bookkeeping change: because the option can be replicated by a stock-and-cash portfolio, the drift cancels out of the replication cost, and setting μ = r − q is simply the arithmetic that makes that cancellation visible.

So: simulate with μ replaced by r − q, take the payoff of each ending, average, multiply by e−rT. The running estimate below converges at the usual Monte Carlo rate — error shrinking like 1/√n, so a hundred times more paths buys you one more decimal place. The horizontal line it's converging to was computed from the closed form.

Check yourself

Your Monte Carlo estimate has a standard error of 0.04. You want it down to 0.01. How many times more paths do you need to run?

16×. Standard error falls like 1/√n, so cutting it to a quarter needs 4² = 16 times the paths. Every extra decimal place of precision costs a hundredfold increase in computation. That relationship is why exotic desks care so much about variance-reduction tricks, and why nobody prices a vanilla European option by simulation in production when a formula exists.
Derivation — why you may replace μ with r − q

This is the step that looks like sleight of hand, so here is the argument in full.

Suppose you can build a portfolio of stock and cash that reproduces the option's payoff in every future state of the world. Then that portfolio and the option must cost the same today — otherwise buy the cheap one, sell the dear one, and you have taken in cash with no residual risk. That's the no-arbitrage principle, and it's the only economic assumption in the entire model.

Chapter six builds exactly that portfolio and shows the construction never once refers to μ. Its cost depends on how far the stock wobbles, because that's what forces you to rebalance, but not on where it's headed. Two traders who violently disagree about a stock's expected return will nonetheless pay the same amount to run the identical hedging program.

Since the answer cannot depend on μ, you may compute it under any drift you find convenient. The convenient choice is the one that makes discounting trivial: pretend every asset drifts at the risk-free rate. Under that fictional measure — call it Q — the discounted stock price is a martingale, meaning today's price is the discounted expectation of tomorrow's:

S = e−(r−q)T · EQ[ ST ]

Reads as: under Q, today’s stock price is just the discounted average of its future price. That is what "drifts at the risk-free rate" means, stated as an equation.

and the same relation prices the option:

V = e−rT · EQ[ payoff(ST) ]

Reads as: average the payoff over every outcome, then discount it back. The superscript Q is a reminder of which probabilities to average with — not a different kind of averaging.

This is the formula chapter three evaluates by brute force.

What this does not say. It is not a claim that investors are indifferent to risk, or that stocks really return the T-bill rate. Q is a computational device, not a forecast. Real-world probabilities and risk-neutral probabilities differ substantially — which is why N(d₂) is not a betting man's estimate of the chance of finishing in the money, and why selling far out-of-the-money puts is not the free money the model's probabilities make it look like.

04

Now the formula, and it should look familiar

Two terms, two probabilities, one ruler. Everything in it appeared already in the simulation.

Doing that integral in closed form gives the expression below. It is not a new idea — it is the same discounted average, evaluated exactly. Read it as two legs: the shares you would receive and the cash you would pay, each present-valued and each weighted by a probability of exercise.

The two probabilities differ, and the reason is worth twenty seconds. N(d₂) is the plain risk-neutral chance of finishing in the money — exactly the fraction of green dots in chapter three. N(d₁) is that same event's probability measured in share units rather than cash units, which weights outcomes by how large the stock got. Bigger endings count for more, so N(d₁) exceeds N(d₂) for a call. And because it is a share-weighted quantity, it is also the number of shares you must hold to hedge. Delta is not a separate concept bolted on afterward; it fell out of the same integral.

The denominator σ√T is the only ruler in the model. It's total volatility over the option's life, and d₂ is just a z-score: how many of those units the strike sits away from the forward. This is why a 20-vol name with thirty-six days left and a 60-vol name with four days behave almost identically: 0.20×√(36/365) and 0.60×√(4/365) are both 0.0628. Same ruler, same distance, same greeks up to scale.

Practical consequence for a corporate-actions desk: when a ratio adjustment changes S and K by the same multiplicative factor, log(S/K) is unchanged, so d₁ and d₂ are unchanged, so delta is unchanged and the option value scales linearly. When an adjustment changes the deliverable's composition instead, nothing that clean is true and you have to reprice the basket.

Check yourself

For a call, N(d₁) is always larger than N(d₂). What is the reason?

They differ by exactly σ√T. Both weight the same event — the stock finishing above K — but N(d₂) counts outcomes equally while N(d₁) weights each by how large the stock got, since the first leg pays you in shares rather than cash. Bigger endings count for more, so the share-weighted probability exceeds the plain one, and it exceeds it by more when there's more uncertainty. Set σ or T near zero and the two collapse onto each other.
Derivation — doing the integral, and where d₁ and d₂ come from

We want the discounted risk-neutral expectation of the call payoff:

C = e−rT EQ[ max(ST − K, 0) ]

Reads as: the call is the discounted average of what it pays, where max(·,0) encodes the right to walk away.

Substitute ST = S·exp[(r − q − σ²/2)T + σ√T·Z]. The payoff is nonzero only when ST > K, which after taking logs and rearranging means:

Z > −[ ln(S/K) + (r − q − σ²/2)T ] / (σ√T) = −d2

Reads as: the option pays only when the random draw beats a threshold, and that threshold, measured in standard deviations, is what we name d₂.

That inequality defines d₂. It is the exercise boundary translated into standard-normal units.

Split the expectation into its two pieces. The second is immediate, because it's just K times the probability of that inequality holding:

E[K · 1{Z > −d₂}] = K · N(d2)

Reads as: a fixed amount K, received only when the threshold is beaten, is worth K times the probability of beating it. The 1 with a subscript is an indicator — it equals 1 when the condition holds and 0 otherwise.

The first piece is harder because ST itself is inside the expectation, multiplying the indicator. Writing it out, you get an integral of eσ√T·z · n(z) over z > −d₂. The trick is to complete the square in the exponent:

eσ√T·z · e−z²/2 = eσ²T/2 · e−(z − σ√T)²/2

Reads as: the same bell curve, slid sideways by σ√T, times a constant. Everything on the right is a normal density again — that is the entire trick.

That is the same bell curve shifted right by σ√T, times a constant. Shifting the curve shifts the integration limit with it, turning −d₂ into −d₂ − σ√T = −d₁. So:

d1 = d2 + σ√T

Reads as: d₁ is just d₂ after the curve was shifted. They are one quantity seen before and after a change of perspective, not two ideas.

and the first term evaluates to S·e(r−q)T·N(d₁). Multiply both pieces by the discount factor e−rT and you have the formula:

S e−qT N(d1) − K e−rT N(d2)

Reads as: what you get, minus what you pay, each discounted and each weighted by its own probability of mattering.

So d₁ is not a second mysterious quantity. It is d₂ after a change of measure, and the shift σ√T is the footprint that change leaves behind. This is also the cleanest way to see why N(d₁) doubles as delta: it arrived attached to the share-denominated term.

The identity that makes the greeks tractable. A small miracle worth knowing, because it is what makes differentiating the formula bearable:

S e−qT n(d1) = K e−rT n(d2)

Reads as: the two legs have exactly equal sensitivity at the margin. When you differentiate, those pieces cancel — which is the only reason the greeks come out as clean as they do.

When you differentiate C with respect to S, the chain-rule terms involving ∂d₁/∂S and ∂d₂/∂S cancel against each other precisely because of this identity, leaving the deceptively simple Δ = e−qTN(d₁). Every greek on this page is verified against numerical differentiation, and they all rely on that cancellation.

05

Six views of one density

The greeks look like six independent risks on a report. They are derivatives of a single function, and they show it.

Every panel below contains the term n(d₁) — the normal density evaluated at d₁ — multiplied by something that scales it. Gamma is that density divided by Sσ√T. Vega is the same density multiplied by S√T. Theta is largely the same density with a minus sign. They peak in the same place because they are the same place.

The instructive move is to drag time to expiry toward zero and watch delta. Far from expiry it's a lazy S-curve; near expiry it becomes a step function. That transition is gamma, and the spike you see in the gamma panel is the same event described from a different angle. Pin risk on expiration Friday is nothing more exotic than this cliff.

5 · 1

What a greek is, and what it is holding still

A greek is a partial derivative of the option's value with respect to one input, with every other input frozen. That last clause is the one that causes trouble in practice, because in a real market nothing is ever frozen. Spot moves and implied vol moves at the same time; a day passes while both happen.

So each greek answers a slightly artificial question — "if only this changed, what would happen?" — and the art is in knowing which artificial questions are close enough to the real one to be useful. First-order greeks answer it well for small changes. The cross-greeks in section 5·6 exist precisely because the freezing assumption fails, and they measure how badly.

Two housekeeping points that prevent most confusion. Units are a convention, not a fact. Vega is mathematically per one unit of vol (σ = 1.00, i.e. 100 vol points), which is a useless number, so every desk quotes it per point by dividing by 100. Theta is per year and quoted per day by dividing by 365. This page shows the traded convention and labels it. And signs follow the position. Every number here is for a long option; flip them all for a short one.

5 · 2

Delta — three things at once

Definition
∂V/∂S — the change in option value per $1 change in the stock.
Range
0 to e−qT for a call, −e−qT to 0 for a put. Never outside.
Largest
Deep in the money. Smallest deep out.

Delta is unusual in wearing three hats that people use interchangeably without noticing. It is the hedge ratio: hold 0.6 delta and you neutralize your first-order risk by shorting 60 shares per contract. It is the share-measure probability of exercise, which is where it came from in the integral. And it is the equivalent stock position — a 0.6-delta call behaves, for small moves, exactly like 60 shares.

The third framing is the one that makes risk reports readable: an options book's total delta tells you how many shares of stock you are effectively holding right now, which is why it's the first line on every risk summary and the first thing that gets flattened.

Delta's own behaviour is the interesting part. It isn't constant, and the rate at which it changes is gamma — so hedging with delta alone is like navigating with a speedometer and no steering. As expiry approaches, delta compresses from a lazy S-curve into a step function, which is why an at-the-money position on expiration morning can swing from 60 shares equivalent to zero and back on a small move.

One relationship worth memorizing: Δcall − Δput = e−qT for the same strike and expiry, which is put-call parity differentiated once. Without dividends that's exactly 1.

5 · 3

Gamma — the thing you are actually buying

Definition
∂²V/∂S² — the change in delta per $1 change in the stock.
Sign
Positive for any long option, call or put. Identical for both.
Largest
At the money, close to expiry. Both effects compound.

Gamma measures curvature, and curvature is what you are paying for when you buy an option. A stock has zero gamma — its payoff is a straight line. An option bends, and that bend is worth money for the reason section M·6 established: averaging a curved function beats evaluating it at the average.

The practical mechanism is worth being concrete about. You are long gamma and delta hedged. The stock rallies, your delta rises, so to stay hedged you must sell stock — at the higher price. It falls back, your delta drops, so you buy — at the lower price. The hedging program mechanically buys low and sells high, and the profit is real. This is gamma scalping, and no forecasting is involved.

Nothing is free, and the price is theta. Gamma and theta are two readings of a single quantity, which chapter seven proves. The right way to hold both facts at once: gamma is the engine, theta is the fuel bill, and the trade works only when realized movement exceeds what you were charged.

Note that gamma is identical for a call and a put at the same strike. So is vega. Only delta differs, by a constant. If you're long a call and short a put at the same strike you have a synthetic stock position — pure delta, zero gamma, zero vega — which is exactly why that structure appears in conversion and reversal trades.

5 · 4

Theta — rent, not decay

Definition
∂V/∂t — value change per unit of calendar time passing, everything else still.
Sign
Negative for almost every long option. Positive is possible: deep in-the-money European puts, where the discounting of the strike dominates.
Largest
At the money, near expiry — the same corner as gamma, and not coincidentally.

The standard framing is that options are "wasting assets" that bleed value as time runs out. That's true but it explains nothing, and it invites the wrong conclusion — that selling options is a way of harvesting an inevitable drip of income.

The better framing: theta is the rent charged for holding gamma. Chapter seven shows θ ≈ −½σ²S²Γ almost exactly. You are not being taxed for the passage of time; you are being charged for owning convexity, and the charge is set by the volatility you paid. Collect theta by selling options and you have not found free money — you have rented out your convexity and taken on the obligation that comes with it.

This also explains theta's shape, which otherwise looks arbitrary. It's largest exactly where gamma is largest, because that's where you own the most convexity. The famous acceleration of decay in the last weeks isn't time behaving strangely; it's gamma rising.

One trap in the arithmetic. Theta is quoted per calendar day, but a Friday close to a Monday close is three days of decay against one day of trading opportunity. Weekend theta is a real, recurring line item, and it's why some desks mark their books on a modified time basis rather than straight calendar days.

5 · 5

Vega — the same curvature, priced by the clock

Definition
∂V/∂σ — value change per one point of implied volatility. Not a Greek letter; the only impostor in the family.
Sign
Positive for any long option. Identical for calls and puts.
Largest
At the money, long dated — the opposite end of the curve from gamma.

Vega measures exposure to the price of volatility rather than to volatility itself. That distinction matters more than almost anything else on this page. Being long vega means you profit if the market re-prices implied vol upward — a mark-to-market event that can happen in seconds and requires the stock to do nothing at all. Being long gamma means you profit if the stock actually moves, realized over time.

You can lose on one while winning on the other. Buy a straddle before an earnings release; the stock gaps exactly as you hoped and you make money on gamma, but implied vol collapses the moment the uncertainty resolves and the vega loss swamps it. That is the classic vol-crush trade and it catches people every quarter.

The relationship to gamma is exact rather than analogical: ν = Γ · S²σT. Same underlying density, but gamma divides by √T and vega multiplies by it. So they are the same object weighted at opposite ends of the term structure — which is the real reason gamma trading and vol trading are different jobs, staffed differently, on the same desk.

5 · 6

The cross-greeks — what happens when two things move at once

The four greeks above each assume everything else holds still. It never does. The cross-greeks are second derivatives that mix two inputs, and each one measures the failure of a hedge you thought you had on.

Vanna
∂Δ/∂σ — your delta hedge changes when implied vol moves. You are flat delta; vol rises three points and you are no longer flat, without the stock having traded. Vanna flips sign across the strike, so a vol move pushes your delta one way below the strike and the other way above. This is most of what a volatility smile does to a book, and it's why skew traders think in vanna terms rather than delta terms.
Charm
∂Δ/∂t — your delta hedge changes just because a day passed. Also called delta bleed. You go home flat on Friday and open Monday with a delta position you never took, purely from time. Largest for near-expiry options close to the strike, which is exactly when you can least afford the surprise.
Volga
∂ν/∂σ — your vega isn't constant in vol either. Vega is largest at the money and falls away in the wings, so as vol changes and effective moneyness shifts, vega changes with it. Long volga means you gain vega as vol rises — convexity in the volatility dimension. It's what makes far out-of-the-money options behave less linearly in vol than a vega number suggests.

Here is the elegant part, and it's a genuine mathematical fact rather than a mnemonic. Mixed partial derivatives don't care about order. So:

vanna = ∂Δ/∂σ = ∂ν/∂S

Reads as: "how much my delta moves when vol changes" and "how much my vega moves when spot changes" are the same number. Not similar — identical, to every decimal place.

charm = ∂Δ/∂t = ∂θ/∂S

Reads as: "how much my delta bleeds per day" and "how my theta changes as the stock moves" are also one number.

This is worth internalizing because it halves what you need to track. A desk worrying about delta drift from vol moves and a desk worrying about vega drift from spot moves are worrying about the same exposure, and hedging one hedges the other.

5 · 8

How they come together — one day, explained

Individually the greeks are sensitivities. Together they are a forecast of your P&L, and the way they combine is the Taylor expansion from section M·5, extended to every variable at once:

ΔV ≈ Δ·ΔS + ½Γ(ΔS)² + ν·Δσ + θ·Δt + vanna·ΔS·Δσ + ½volga·(Δσ)² + charm·ΔS·Δt

Reads as: your day's P&L, broken into what the stock did, how much it bent, what vol did, what time cost, and three corrections for things moving together.

This is what a risk report is. Every line on a desk's daily attribution is one of these terms, and the "unexplained" line at the bottom is everything the expansion missed — third-order effects and beyond. Run the scenarios below and watch which terms dominate in each. The lesson is that no single greek is ever the answer:

On a quiet day theta is nearly the whole story and everything else is noise. On a gap, gamma overtakes delta and the linear estimate becomes actively misleading. On a vol crush, vega dwarfs everything the stock did. Over a weekend, theta and charm arrive together and you find delta you didn't have. The greeks that matter are entirely a function of what kind of day it was.

Check yourself

You are flat delta on an options book. Overnight the stock doesn't move at all, but implied vol rises four points. You arrive to find you are no longer delta neutral. Which greek did this?

Vanna and charm together. Gamma is innocent here — it only moves delta when spot moves, and spot didn't. Vanna is the delta change from the vol move, charm is the delta change from the day passing, and both fire overnight without a single share trading. This is the everyday reason a book that closed flat opens with a position, and it's why cross-greeks are on real risk reports rather than being a textbook curiosity.

Check yourself

You want the largest possible gamma. Which option should you buy?

At the money, about to expire. Gamma is e−qTn(d₁)/(Sσ√T): the numerator is largest when d₁ ≈ 0, meaning at the money, and the denominator shrinks toward zero as T does. Both effects push the same way. Far out of the money and short dated is the opposite corner — n(d₁) collapses because you're far into the tail, and that beats the shrinking denominator. Note this is also the maximum-theta option: you cannot rent that much convexity cheaply.
Derivation — the greeks are one density wearing six hats

Start from Δ = e−qTN(d₁) and differentiate once more with respect to S. Only d₁ depends on S, and ∂d₁/∂S = 1/(Sσ√T), so:

Γ = e−qT n(d1) / (S σ √T)

Reads as: curvature is the bell curve’s height, divided by how much room the stock has to move. Less room — short expiry, low vol — means more curvature.

Differentiate the price with respect to σ instead. The same identity that killed the chain-rule terms for delta kills them here too, leaving:

ν = S e−qT n(d1) √T

Reads as: vol sensitivity is that same height, multiplied by how much time is left. Same numerator as gamma, opposite treatment of time.

Now put them side by side. Both are the same n(d₁), scaled:

ν = Γ · S² σ T

Reads as: vega and gamma are the same quantity in different units. Anything true of one is true of the other after rescaling.

Vega is gamma in different clothing. They peak in the same place and vanish in the same places.

This relationship explains a fact that otherwise has to be memorized: gamma blows up as T → 0 because it divides by √T, while vega dies as T → 0 because it multiplies by √T. Same density, opposite powers. That is why gamma is a front-month concern and vega is a back-month concern — not two separate empirical facts, but one algebraic one.

Theta follows from the pricing equation rather than by grinding through the derivative, which is chapter seven's subject. The remaining two on this page are cross-derivatives:

vanna = ∂Δ/∂σ = −e−qT n(d1) d2 / σ

Reads as: how much your hedge ratio moves when implied vol moves. The d₂ factor out front is what flips its sign across the strike.

volga = ∂ν/∂σ = ν · d1d2 / σ

Reads as: how much your vol sensitivity itself changes when vol changes — convexity in the volatility dimension rather than the price dimension.

Note that vanna carries a factor of d₂ and volga carries d₁d₂. Those factors are why both change sign as you cross the strike, while delta, gamma and vega never do. A greek's sign changes exactly when one of its d-factors passes through zero.

06

The part that makes it true

Sell the option, hedge it mechanically, and see what you're left holding. Then break the drift on purpose.

Everything so far could have been a coincidence of arithmetic. This is the chapter where it stops being one. The claim Black and Scholes actually made is not "here is a fair value" — it's "here is a trading strategy that manufactures the option's payoff out of stock and cash, and its cost is the price." If that's true, the price isn't an opinion. It's a cost of goods.

The simulation sells one option at the model price and then hedges it: hold Δ shares, finance the rest at r, collect dividends at q, and rebalance to the new Δ at every step. No forecasting, no discretion. At expiry, compare what the portfolio is worth to what you owe. The residual is the hedging error.

Now do the thing that matters. Move the real-world drift μ to −40%, or to +60%. The paths change completely; some chapters' pictures would be unrecognizable. The hedging error distribution does not move. It stays centred on zero, because the replication cost never depended on where the stock was going — only on how much it wobbled on the way. That is why implied volatility is the quoted variable and expected return is not, and it is the single idea that separates pricing an option from having a view on it.

What does move the error is rehedging frequency. Hedge continuously and the error vanishes; hedge twenty times and you keep a fat residual whose standard deviation falls roughly like 1/√N. That residual is not a modelling defect you can optimize away. It's discretization, and it is a real, funded line item on every options desk.

Try this

The experiment that proves the theorem. Set rehedges to 4, run 300 trials, and read the error standard deviation. Clear, set rehedges to 64, run 300 again.

Sixteen times the hedging effort should have cut the error by √16 = 4×. Then — the real test — move the drift slider anywhere you like and run once more. The width will not change.

Check yourself

Two traders hedge identical short calls with the same discipline. One is convinced the stock will rally hard; the other is convinced it will collapse. Both are right about their own view — the stock does what they each predicted, in two parallel worlds with the same volatility. Whose hedging P&L is better?

Neither. This is the whole content of the theorem and it is genuinely counterintuitive. The bull's hedge buys stock as it rallies and the bear's sells as it falls; both are transacting at prices that make the running cost of replication identical in expectation. What separates a winning short-vol trade from a losing one is never direction — it's whether realized volatility came in under the implied volatility you sold. You can be completely right about direction and still lose, which is exactly what chapter seven quantifies.
Derivation — building the hedge, and the equation that falls out

Hold one option worth V(S,t) and short Δ shares. The portfolio is Π = V − ΔS. Over an instant, apply Itô to V — remembering (dS)² = σ²S²dt:

dV = (∂V/∂t) dt + (∂V/∂S) dS + ½ σ²S² (∂²V/∂S²) dt

Reads as: the option changes for three reasons — time passed, the stock moved, and the stock wiggled. That third term is the one ordinary calculus would have discarded, and it is where all the interesting behaviour lives.

so the portfolio changes by

dΠ = [ ∂V/∂t + ½σ²S² ∂²V/∂S² ] dt + [ ∂V/∂S − Δ ] dS

Reads as: the portfolio has a predictable part and a random part, and the random part is entirely contained in the second bracket.

The second bracket is the only random term — it's the one multiplying dS. Choose Δ = ∂V/∂S and it vanishes identically. That single choice is what delta hedging is, and it is why delta is defined the way it is rather than by some other convention.

What's left has no dW in it, so over the next instant the portfolio is riskless. A riskless portfolio must earn exactly r, or there's an arbitrage. Setting the two expressions for its growth equal — and adding the dividend the short stock position costs you — gives:

∂V/∂t + ½σ²S²(∂²V/∂S²) + (r−q)S(∂V/∂S) − rV = 0

Reads as: decay, plus the value of the wiggle, plus the carry on the hedge, must exactly equal the financing cost. If it didn’t balance, someone would have a money machine.

In greek notation: θ + ½σ²S²Γ + (r−q)SΔ − rV = 0. These are chapter seven's four bars.

Look at what is missing. μ does not appear. It was in dS, and it left with the dS term the moment you chose Δ. That is the formal version of chapter six's experiment: the drift slider doesn't move the histogram because the drift isn't in the equation.

Black–Scholes is the solution to this equation with the boundary condition V(S,T) = max(S−K, 0). The formula in chapter four and this PDE are the same object; the integral was just an easier route to it.

Where it breaks in practice. The derivation assumed you rebalance continuously. Rebalancing N times a day instead leaves a residual whose standard deviation falls like 1/√N — visible in the histogram above and not removable by being cleverer. It also assumed σ is a known constant and that S moves continuously. Real stocks gap, and real volatility is itself volatile, which is why every desk that uses this model also runs a book of corrections to it.

07

What the gamma costs

The equation the whole model satisfies, read as a rent agreement rather than a partial differential equation.

Every Black–Scholes value satisfies one constraint, and the four bars below are its terms. They cancel to zero for any inputs you can dial in — not approximately, but to machine precision, because the model is defined as the solution to this equation.

Delete the two rate terms for a moment and what's left is θ ≈ −½σ²S²Γ. Theta is not a separate decay charge that the model tacks on. It is the price of the gamma, quoted per day. Long gamma means you are renting convexity, and theta is the rent.

So the daily question on a delta-hedged book has an exact answer. Set the gamma P&L from a move, ½Γ(ΔS)², equal to one day of theta, and solve. The gamma cancels completely and you are left with S·σ/√365 — the one-day implied move. If the stock moves more than that, your scalping out-earns the rent; less, and you're paying for convexity you didn't use. Realized versus implied, in one line, with nothing memorized.

Try this

Set spot 100, 32 vol, 30 days, at the money. Before pressing anything, compute the daily breakeven in your head: 100 × 0.32 / √365. √365 is about 19.1, so you should get roughly 1.67 — call it a 1.7% daily move.

Now check it against the panel. Then raise vol to 64% and confirm the breakeven doubles, because the relationship is linear in σ.

Check yourself

You are long a delta-hedged at-the-money option on a $200 stock at 25 implied vol. The stock has been moving about 1% a day. Are you making money?

You're losing. Long gamma does profit from movement in either direction — but you are paying theta every single day for the privilege, and the rent is set by the vol you bought at. At 25 vol you need about 1.31% of daily movement to break even and you're getting 1%, so you bleed the difference. This is the entire long-vol trade compressed into one comparison: realized versus implied, not up versus down. Sell that same option and the arithmetic flips in your favour.
Derivation — the breakeven move, in four lines

Take the pricing equation and drop the two rate terms, which are small for a near-the-money option:

θ ≈ −½ σ² S² Γ

Reads as: your daily decay is set by how much curvature you own and how much the stock moves. More gamma, more rent.

Theta here is annual, so one calendar day of decay is that divided by 365. You are delta hedged, so your P&L from a move ΔS over one day is the gamma term, ½Γ(ΔS)². Set the gain equal to the rent:

½ Γ (ΔS)² = ½ σ² S² Γ / 365

Reads as: set what you earn from today’s move equal to what you pay for today. Solving this is the whole calculation.

Γ appears on both sides and cancels completely — which is the surprising part, because it means the answer does not depend on how much gamma you own:

(ΔS)² = σ²S²/365  ⟹  ΔS = S σ / √365

Reads as: the stock has to move by one day’s worth of implied volatility. Gamma vanished from both sides — the answer is the same whatever size you trade.

That is just the one-day implied move. Buy an option at σ and you have agreed to a daily rent that the stock must move σ/√365 to cover. Move more, you win; move less, you lose. Nothing about the strike, the expiry, or the size of your position enters — they scale both sides equally.

The correction. The panel shows √(2θ/Γ) computed exactly, including the rate terms we dropped, and reports the gap. It's a percent or two for at-the-money options in normal rate environments, and it grows for deep in-the-money strikes where rV is large relative to the gamma term. The rule of thumb is excellent where you'll use it and misleading exactly where you shouldn't.

Check yourself

You are long 40 contracts with a gamma of 0.05 per share, on a $80 stock. You have hedged your delta to flat with stock. The stock rallies 2%. Roughly how far off your hedge are you now?

About 320 shares long. A 2% move on an $80 stock is $1.60. Gamma of 0.05 means delta rises 0.05 per $1, so 0.08 per share over that move, times 100 times 40 contracts is 320 shares. Question 1 · chapter 00

You sell a 50-strike put for 4. The stock finishes at 41. What is your P&L?

−5. The put is worth max(50 − 41, 0) = 9 against you, offset by the 4 you took in. Your breakeven as a put seller is K minus the premium — here 46 — and below that you lose dollar for dollar. Uncapped downside all the way to zero is what you sold.
Question 2 · chapter 01

A stock has σ = 40%. Roughly what is its expected one-day move, using 252 trading days?

About 2.5%. Volatility scales with the square root of time, so to convert an annual number to a daily one you divide by √252 ≈ 15.87, giving 2.52%. Dividing by 252 instead is the single most common units error in options and it's wrong by a factor of sixteen.
Question 3 · chapter 02

Under the model, which statement about the terminal price distribution is true?

Lognormal. The log of the terminal price is normal, which makes the price itself right-skewed with a hard floor at zero. Symmetry in log space emphatically does not survive exponentiation — that's precisely what creates the gap between mean and median you watched open up in chapter two.
Question 4 · chapter 03

Why is an option priced under the risk-neutral measure rather than with the stock's real expected return?

Replication. The hedging strategy's cost is driven by how much the stock wobbles, never by where it's going. Since the price must equal that cost, μ cannot appear in the answer — so you are free to compute under whatever drift is most convenient, and r − q makes the discounting trivial. Q is arithmetic, not a statement about anyone's risk appetite.
Question 5 · chapter 04

A 20-vol option has 36 days to expiry. Roughly what volatility on a 4-day option gives the same σ√T, and therefore near-identical greeks?

60%. To hold σ√T fixed while cutting T by a factor of 9, you multiply σ by √9 = 3. Both sides equal 0.0628. The square root is what trips people up — the nine-times-shorter option needs three times the vol, not nine times. σ√T is the model's only ruler, and two options that measure the same on it behave the same.
Question 6 · chapter 05

As expiry approaches for an at-the-money option, what happens to gamma and vega?

Gamma up, vega down. Gamma is n(d₁)/(Sσ√T) and vega is Sn(d₁)√T. The identical density sits in both, but √T is in the denominator of one and the numerator of the other. That's why gamma trading lives in the front month and vol trading lives in the back, and why the two are different jobs on a desk.
Question 7 · chapter 06

You hedge a short option 25 times over its life instead of 100 times. What happens to the standard deviation of your terminal hedging error?

It roughly doubles. Cutting rehedges by a factor of four multiplies the error's standard deviation by √4 = 2 — you can see this directly in chapter six's histogram. Note this is a spread around zero, not a bias: less frequent hedging doesn't cost you money on average, it makes your outcome noisier. In reality you'd trade that noise off against transaction costs, which is a real optimization every desk performs.
Question 8 · chapter 07

A $400 stock, 20 implied vol, and you're long a delta-hedged straddle. The stock has realized about 1.5% daily. Roughly how are you doing?

Winning. The breakeven daily move is σ/√365 ≈ 1.05% of spot, about $4.19, and the stock is delivering 1.5%. Your gamma scalping is out-earning the theta rent. The direction is irrelevant — chop and trend both count, because the gamma term is ½Γ(ΔS)² and squaring destroys the sign. What matters is only that realized exceeded implied.
Question 9 · chapter 08

You are short 30 contracts with a vega of 0.18 per point. Implied vol rises 3 points overnight; the stock is unchanged. What is your P&L?

−$1,620. 0.18 × 3 points × 100 multiplier × 30 contracts = $1,620, and you are short vega so a vol rally hurts. The distractors are the two errors that actually happen: forgetting the 100 multiplier, and getting the sign backwards. Note this is a mark-to-market loss delivered with zero trading and zero movement in the underlying.
Question 10 · chapter 08

You are long a straddle, delta hedged flat with stock. Your risk manager asks you to reduce vega. Can you do it by trading stock?

No — stock only moves delta. A share has a delta of exactly 1 and a gamma, vega and theta of exactly zero, because its payoff is a straight line with no time dimension. That is precisely why delta is the only greek you can hedge with the underlying, and why every other exposure has to be traded away in the options market itself. It's also the reason a delta-hedged book still has a P&L: you neutralized the one risk stock can touch and kept all the others on purpose.

Price a 3-month at-the-money call by hand: S = 100, K = 100, T = 0.25 years, σ = 30%, r = 5%, q = 0. Every intermediate value is shown so you can check your own arithmetic at each step rather than only at the end. You'll need a normal CDF — most spreadsheets have it as NORM.S.DIST(x, TRUE).

A last observation on that example, because it's the kind of thing that makes the formula feel less alien. The call costs 6.58 on a 100 stock for three months. As a rule of thumb an at-the-money option costs roughly 0.4 × S × σ × √T, which here gives 0.4 × 100 × 0.30 × 0.5 = 6.00. Close, and it stays close across a wide range of inputs. If you ever need to sanity-check a quoted premium without a screen, that's the number to have in your head.

European exercise, continuous dividend yield, constant volatility, frictionless rebalancing, no jumps. Every one of those is false in a real market, and each failure has a name and a desk that trades it: American early exercise, discrete dividends, the volatility surface, transaction costs, gap risk. The model's value is that it makes the disagreements precise enough to price.
