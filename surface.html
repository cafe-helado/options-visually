The shape of what we don't believe

Black–Scholes takes one volatility. The market quotes a different one for every strike and every expiry. That disagreement is not a defect in the model — it is a picture of the distribution the market actually has in mind, and you can read it directly.

01

One volatility per option

The model takes a single σ. The market refuses to supply one. Start by taking the refusal seriously.

Take every listed option on one underlying, invert Black–Scholes on each quoted price, and write down the volatility that makes the formula reproduce the market. If the model were literally true you would get the same number every time. You never do. You get a systematically curved, systematically tilted sheet of numbers — low strikes trading at higher volatility than high strikes, front months behaving differently from back.

There are two ways to react and only one of them is useful. The first is to call the model broken. The second — the right one — is to notice that implied volatility is not a forecast, it is a price in different units. Black–Scholes is a monotonic function from vol to price, so quoting an option in vol is just quoting it in a currency everybody agrees to convert with. The surface is the market disagreeing with the model's distribution, and expressing that disagreement in the only variable the model gives it.

That reframing is what makes the rest of the page possible. A smile is not evidence that traders are confused about volatility. It is a set of prices that, decoded, tells you exactly what probability distribution they are using instead.

02

The smile is a probability distribution

Not analogous to one. Not a summary of one. The same object, and there is an exact formula that converts between them.

Here is the result that makes the surface legible. Consider a call spread: long a call at K, short a call at K + ΔK. It pays ΔK if the stock finishes above both strikes, zero if below both, and something in between otherwise. Make ΔK small and it becomes a bet on finishing above K, so its price is proportional to the probability of that happening.

Now take a butterfly: long K − ΔK, short two at K, long K + ΔK. It pays only if the stock lands near K. Its price is therefore the probability of landing near K — which, as ΔK shrinks, is the probability density at K. A butterfly is a second difference in strike, so:

∂²C/∂K² = e−rT · f(K)

Reads as: the curvature of call price in strike is the risk-neutral density, discounted. This is the Breeden–Litzenberger result, and it is exact.

So the surface is not merely related to a distribution. Differentiate it twice and the distribution falls out. Below, the recovered density is computed from the smile above by numerical second difference — no model, no fitting — and drawn against the lognormal that a flat volatility would have implied.

Flatten the skew slider and the two curves lie on top of each other, because a flat surface is the lognormal assumption. Tilt it and watch the left tail thicken and the right tail thin. At a −0.35 skew the probability of finishing below 70 goes from 5.0% to 8.6% — nearly double — while the chance of finishing above 130 falls from 10.2% to 6.6%. That is what the skew has been saying all along, and it is a statement about crashes rather than about volatility.

Check yourself

A trader says the put skew means "the market expects volatility to be higher if we sell off." Is that the best reading?

The distributional reading is the primary one. Breeden–Litzenberger says the surface is a density, so the most direct translation of downside strikes trading rich is "more probability mass in the left tail than lognormal." The conditional-volatility story is a plausible mechanism for why that mass is there — and so is crash risk, and so is hedging demand — but those are explanations for the shape, whereas the fat left tail is simply what the shape says. Keeping the observation separate from its explanations is what lets you evaluate the explanations.
Derivation — from call spreads to the density in three steps

Write the call price as the discounted expected payoff under the risk-neutral density f:

C(K) = e−rT ∫K∞ (x − K) f(x) dx

Reads as: average what the call pays over every outcome above the strike, then discount.

Differentiate once with respect to K. The integrand's boundary term vanishes because the payoff is zero at x = K, leaving only the derivative of −K inside:

∂C/∂K = −e−rT ∫K∞ f(x) dx = −e−rT · P(ST > K)

Reads as: the slope of call price in strike is the probability of finishing in the money. That is a digital option, and it is also why N(d₂) showed up in page 01.

Differentiate again. The derivative of a tail probability is minus the density:

∂²C/∂K² = e−rT f(K)

Reads as: differentiate the tail probability and you get the density. Two derivatives take you from prices to probabilities with nothing assumed in between.

Each derivative has a trading interpretation, which is worth carrying: the first is a call spread, so a tight call spread prices a digital. The second is a butterfly, so a tight butterfly prices the density. When somebody says the wings are expensive, they are making a claim about tail probabilities whether they mean to or not.

Where it breaks. Strikes are discrete, so you get a finite difference rather than a derivative and the estimate is noisy where strikes are sparse. The tails are worse: the density beyond the lowest and highest listed strike is unobserved and has to be extrapolated, and chapter 06 shows how badly that can go.

03

Two ways to make a smile

Jumps and stochastic volatility both bend the surface. At a single expiry they are indistinguishable. Across expiries they are not.

Black–Scholes produces a flat surface because it assumes a stock diffuses continuously at a constant volatility. Break either assumption and a smile appears.

Jumps. Let the stock occasionally gap by a random amount, as it does through an earnings release or a headline. Merton's model adds exactly that — a Poisson arrival of lognormal jumps — and it has a closed form, so no simulation is needed. Give the jumps a negative mean and the resulting density has a fat left tail, which the surface reports as skew.

Stochastic volatility. Let the volatility itself be random. If vol moves independently of spot, the terminal distribution becomes a mixture of lognormals with different widths, which is fat-tailed on both sides. The surface reports that as a symmetric smile with no skew. To get skew you need vol to move against spot — the leverage effect — which is why real stochastic-vol models carry a correlation parameter and why it is always negative for equities.

Here is the problem: at one expiry, calibrated to the same data, both produce curves you cannot tell apart by eye. The panels below start with settings that look nearly identical at three months.

The test is the term structure. A jump is a fixed-size shock. Over a short window it dominates the diffusion and bends the surface violently; over a long window the diffusion accumulates while the jump does not, so the skew washes out. Stochastic volatility has no such decay — the vol-of-vol keeps working at every horizon. The numbers are stark: jump-driven skew falls from 8.46 vol points at one month to 0.40 at three years, while the mixture's stays essentially flat at 0.3 to 0.6 throughout.

So when you see a surface whose skew collapses as you go out in time, that is a market pricing event risk. When the skew persists into the long end, that is a market pricing a regime.

Check yourself

You observe a name whose 90–110 skew is 9 vol points in the front month and under 1 point at two years. What does that most suggest?

Event risk. Fixed-size jump risk contributes a constant amount of variance regardless of horizon, while diffusion accumulates with time — so the jump's share of total variance, and therefore its effect on the shape, shrinks as maturity grows. Persistent skew into the long end points at a regime property instead. This distinction has a direct trade behind it, which is page 03's subject: if the front-month skew is event-driven you can strip out the event and price what is left.
04

The other axis

A surface has two dimensions and most explanations only draw one. Term structure is where the tradeable information usually is.

Fix the strike at the money and look across expiries. The resulting curve is usually upward sloping in calm markets — the front is cheap because nothing is scheduled — and inverts sharply in stress, because whatever people are afraid of has a date on it and the date is soon.

The mechanism is the same additivity that page 01 used for √T. Variance adds, so total variance to expiry is the sum of each day's variance, and the implied vol you see quoted is a time-average of forward variance rather than a forecast of any particular day. That has an immediate consequence: you can back out the forward volatility between two expiries from the two quoted vols, exactly the way you back a forward rate out of two spot rates.

σfwd² · (T₂ − T₁) = σ₂²T₂ − σ₁²T₁

Reads as: the variance between two dates is the difference of the total variances. Subtract, don't average — averaging vols instead of variances is one of the most common errors on a vol desk.

The forward vol is where the term structure becomes tradeable. A calendar spread is a position in it, and an inverted curve does not automatically mean the front is expensive — it may mean the forward vol implied between the two dates is absurd, which is a sharper and more testable statement.

One warning the figure makes vivid: if the front vol is high enough relative to the back, the implied forward variance goes negative, which is impossible. That is a calendar arbitrage, and it is chapter 06's subject.

05

What your delta actually is

Black–Scholes delta assumes vol does not move when spot does. On a skewed surface that assumption is a choice, and it is worth real money.

Ask what happens to the surface when spot moves and you will find the question has no model-provided answer. It has conventions, and the convention you pick changes your hedge.

Sticky strike: the vol attached to each strike stays put as spot moves. Under this rule the Black–Scholes delta is the whole story, since σ does not change.

Sticky delta (or sticky moneyness): the smile is a function of K/F and travels with the underlying. A fixed strike therefore sees its vol change as spot moves, and your total delta picks up a second term:

Δtotal = ΔBS + vega × ∂σ/∂S

Reads as: the direct effect, plus what the moving smile does to you through vega. That second term is vanna's territory — page 01 built it, and this is where it earns its keep.

The sign is not obvious and matters. With a downward-sloping equity skew, a rise in spot means a fixed strike now sits at lower moneyness on the shifted curve, so its implied vol goes up. Higher vol means a higher call price, which is extra positive delta. On a 100-strike, six-month call at 30 vol with a −0.35 skew, the difference is about 0.097 of delta — roughly 10 shares per contract, or a thousand shares on a hundred lots, from nothing but a choice of convention.

Neither rule is correct. They are descriptions of surface behaviour that hold in different regimes — sticky strike in quiet drifting markets, sticky delta in trending ones — and desks pick one, mark against it, and monitor the error. The one indefensible position is not knowing which one your risk system assumes.

Check yourself

Under which condition do sticky-strike and sticky-delta give the same hedge?

When the surface is flat. The correction is vega × ∂σ/∂S, and ∂σ/∂S is proportional to the slope of the smile, so no slope means no correction and both rules return the Black–Scholes delta. It also means the disagreement is worst exactly where you have the most vega — at the money, in high-skew names — which is the least convenient place for a convention to matter.
06

Surfaces that cannot be right

Not every set of numbers is a surface. Two constraints, both with a trade attached, and both checkable in seconds.

Because the surface is a probability distribution, it inherits the rules that distributions obey. Break one and you have not merely mispriced something — you have written down a set of quotes that lets somebody take money out of you with certainty.

Butterfly constraint. A density cannot be negative, and by Breeden–Litzenberger the density is the butterfly price. So ∂²C/∂K² ≥ 0 everywhere. Steepen the skew past a point and the recovered density dips below zero — at which point a butterfly is trading for a negative premium, and you buy it, and you can only make money. The figure highlights the region in red when it happens; in these settings it appears somewhere around a −1.0 skew.

Calendar constraint. Total variance cannot decrease with maturity, since variance accumulates. So σ²T must be non-decreasing in T at a fixed moneyness. Violate it and the forward variance between two expiries is negative — you would be selling the near-dated option and buying the far-dated one for a certain profit.

There is a third and subtler one worth knowing about. The wings cannot grow arbitrarily fast: if implied vol rises faster than roughly √|k| in the far strikes, the implied density stops integrating to one. This page's own machinery demonstrates it — set a large positive wing curvature and the "density" integrates to 1.19 instead of 1, which is not a rounding error but a signal that the parameterization is not describing a probability distribution at all.

The practical version: any smile you fit needs its wings constrained, and a parameterization that looks great through the listed strikes can be nonsense just outside them. This is why surface fitting in production uses forms that are arbitrage-free by construction rather than whatever curve minimises the error.

Check yourself

A vendor surface prices the 95, 100 and 105 strikes such that the 95/100/105 butterfly costs −0.02. What do you do?

Buy it, or distrust the surface. A butterfly is a bet on landing near the middle strike and cannot be worth less than zero, so a negative price is either free money or, far more often, a fitting artifact in a vendor surface. In practice the second is overwhelmingly more likely — which makes this a good automated check to run across every surface you consume, because it flags exactly the strikes where the fit has come apart.

Question 1 · chapter 01

What does a smile tell you the flat Black–Scholes model got wrong?

The distribution, not the arithmetic. The surface is not a failure of the formula so much as the market's correction to the assumption underneath it — which is exactly why the second derivative across strikes recovers a density.
Question 2 · chapter 02

Breeden–Litzenberger recovers what from option prices?

The implied density. Under a flat surface it reproduces the lognormal to 0.04%; put a −0.35 skew in and the probability of finishing below 70 goes from 5.0% to 8.6% while the chance of finishing above 130 falls from 10.2% to 6.6%.
Question 3 · chapter 03

Jumps and stochastic volatility both make a smile. How do you tell them apart?

Term structure, not shape. At one expiry the two are close to indistinguishable, which is precisely why fitting a single smile tells you much less about the generating mechanism than people assume.
Question 4 · chapter 05

Sticky strike or sticky delta: how much does the choice change your delta?

About 0.097, or ten shares a contract. Two desks with identical prices and identical models can hold measurably different hedges purely because of a convention nobody prints on the ticket.
Question 5 · chapter 06

What does vanna do in a skewed market?

Vanna is the bridge. On a skewed surface a spot move is a volatility move for the strike you hold, so your delta is model-dependent in a way vanna is the natural language for.
Question 6 · chapter 07

A quoted butterfly across three strikes has a negative price. What does that mean?

The surface cannot be right. Butterfly and calendar constraints are not modelling preferences — they are the conditions under which the prices correspond to any probability distribution at all, and a violation is a broken mark far more often than it is a trade.

Everything here treats the surface as a static object read at one instant. Real surfaces move, and how they move is the harder question — sticky rules are crude descriptions of that motion, and the difference between them is a live P&L item rather than a modelling nicety. The models in chapter 03 are also deliberately simple: Merton has constant jump parameters, and the mixture has no correlation between spot and volatility. Real calibration uses both mechanisms together, because neither alone reproduces both ends of the term structure.
