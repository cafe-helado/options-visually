A deal is a price on two futures

A target stock after an announcement is not a company any more. It is a claim on the deal price if the deal closes and on the standalone value if it breaks, financed until somebody decides which.

01

The spread is a rate, not a profit

A dollar of spread on a four-dollar stock is a return only after you say how long it takes and what the money costs.

Suppose an acquirer agrees to buy a company for $5 a share in cash. The target was $2 yesterday; it opens at $4. The gross spread is $1 — a 25% return if the deal closes.

Twenty-five percent over what? That is the whole first lesson. If the deal closes in three months the annualized return is about 100%; in a year it is 25%; in two years 12.5%. The same dollar is a completely different trade depending on the calendar, which is why arbitrageurs quote spreads annualized and why the legal timeline is as important as the legal outcome.

And the money is not free. You fund the position at some rate — fed funds plus a spread for most institutions. The net spread is what is left after paying it, and it is the real risk premium: the compensation for the chance that the deal breaks and the stock falls back toward $2.

That framing answers a question that sounds like a riddle. A fund that has returned 8% a year for decades, while fed funds averaged 4%, has not earned 8% of skill. It has earned the funding rate plus about 4% of premium. If you borrow at fed funds plus 50 basis points when fed funds is 5.25%, the honest forecast for that fund is 5.25% plus the same 4% premium — about 9.25% — and your carry after paying 5.75% on the loan is about 3.5% a year, or roughly $350,000 on a million over ten years without compounding. Compound both sides and it is $673,169. The 8% headline was never the number; the premium over funding was.

net spread = (D − S)/S × 12/months − (r + funding spread)

Reads as: the spread you collect, stretched to a year, minus what the year's money costs you. Positive means you are being paid to take the break risk; negative means you are paying for it.

Try this

The deal is unchanged but the regulator signals a long review. Predict the month at which the spread stops covering a 5.75% cost of money, then stretch the timeline.

Check yourself

A merger-arb fund returned 8% a year for fifty years while fed funds averaged 4%. Fed funds is now 5.25%. What should you expect it to return?

Funding plus the premium. Spreads are set so that arbitrageurs earn their cost of capital plus compensation for risk, so when the cost of capital rises spreads widen with it. The last answer names a real second-order effect — expensive financing can deter leveraged buyers — but it is not what sets the arbitrageur's return on deals already announced.
02

The price is a probability, once you pay for the wait

Two outcomes, one price, and the odds fall out. The common shortcut charges interest in only one of the outcomes.

If the deal closes the stock is worth $5. If it breaks, it is worth roughly what it was before anyone heard of the deal — $2, on the assumption that the holders who were happy at $2 would buy it there again. Today it is $4. With no time and no interest, the price is a weighted average of the two outcomes and the weight is the probability:

S = p · D + (1 − p) · SV ⟹ p = (S − SV)/(D − SV) = (4 − 2)/(5 − 2) = 66.7%

Reads as: the downside you are risking, as a share of the whole range between break and close. The market is paying as though there is a two-in-three chance the deal goes through.

Now put the wait back in. The deal will resolve in exactly a year and you fund at 10%. You pay $4 today, but what you must earn is $4.40 at resolution, because the money cost you 40 cents either way — whether the deal closes or breaks. Price that honestly and the odds are 80.0%: p·5 + (1 − p)·2 = 4.40.

The shortcut most primers teach, downside ÷ (upside − interest + downside), gives 76.9%. It subtracts the interest from the upside only — as though you paid for your money in the world where the deal closes and got it free in the world where it breaks. You do not. The gap is three points of probability here and it grows with the rate and the time.

Notice the direction: carry raises the implied odds. A stock trading at the same price under higher rates is telling you the deal is more likely, because the market is accepting a lower return for the same risk only if there is less risk.

Try this

The question as it is usually asked: one year, 10% funding, $4 stock, $2 standalone. Predict both odds before pressing, then take the rate to zero.

Check yourself

A target trades at $4 against a $5 cash deal and a $2 standalone. Rates go from zero to 10% with the price unchanged. What happened to the implied odds?

They rose, to 80%. The fourth answer is the shortcut, and it is the tempting one because it is printed in primers: it charges the 40 cents of interest against the upside only. Your money costs the same in the break state, so the right equation grows the whole $4, not just the part you hope to collect.
Derivation — the odds formula, done once properly

Fund the purchase at r for time T. At resolution you must have S(1 + rT) to break even, and the position is worth D with probability p and SV otherwise.

p · D + (1 − p) · SV = S · (1 + rT)

Reads as: the expected payout equals what the purchase cost, grown at your funding rate. That is what "fairly priced to a financed buyer" means.

Solve for p.

p = ( S(1 + rT) − SV ) / ( D − SV )

Reads as: the downside, grown by the carry, as a share of the range. With r = 0 it is the simple ratio from the start of the chapter.

The shortcut instead writes p(D − S − SrT) = (1 − p)(S − SV), which is the same equation with the carry deleted from the break side. Expanding both shows the gap is (1 − p)·SrT in the numerator — interest on the break state, exactly the thing the shortcut forgot.

Where it breaks. Standalone value is not a constant. The market moves while the deal is pending, the target's business changes, and a broken deal often leaves the target worth less than before (it may have stopped investing) or more (somebody else now knows it is for sale). The odds you back out are only as good as the SV you assumed, and the SV is a view.

03

A stock deal borrows nothing and hedges imperfectly

Pay in shares instead of cash and the financing almost disappears. What replaces it is basis risk, and a short that does not close itself if the deal breaks.

Change one term: the acquirer pays $5 of its own stock per share instead of $5 cash. To lock in the spread you buy the target at $4 and short $5 of the acquirer. If the deal closes, your target shares turn into exactly the acquirer shares you are short, and the position closes itself for a $1 profit regardless of where the acquirer trades.

Notice what happened to the financing. You are long $4 and short $5, so you are net short a dollar and earn a rebate on the proceeds. The capital cost that dominated chapter 02 is gone, which is why stock deals are sometimes called costless capital — and why the timeline matters much less. If the deal takes longer, you keep earning the rebate and keep waiting.

Ignoring the rebate, the odds are the plain ratio: 2 / 3 = 66.7%. The maximum loss is the interesting number. If the deal breaks with the acquirer unchanged, the target falls to $2 and you lose $2. But the short does not close itself in that world. If the acquirer rallied 30% before the break you lose another $1.50 on the short — $3.50 in total — and in principle the loss on the short leg is unbounded.

And before anything is decided there is basis risk. The target only tracks the acquirer in proportion to the odds of closing — with a two-in-three chance, a 10% acquirer rally lifts the target by about two-thirds of that on the deal leg. Short the full $5 and you are over-hedged by a third: a rally hurts you even while the deal is on track.

mark-to-market ≈ (p − 1) · $5 · (acquirer move)

Reads as: the target moves with the acquirer only by the probability of closing, so a full short leaves you short the remainder. Hedging p of the consideration rather than all of it removes the drift.

Try this

Rally the acquirer 30% and predict the loss if the deal then breaks. Then raise the odds to 95% and watch the daily basis shrink.

Check yourself

You are long the target and short the full stock consideration. The acquirer rallies 10% with no deal news. What happened?

You lost a little. At two-in-three odds the target picks up about two-thirds of the acquirer's move on its deal leg, and the short loses all of it. The third answer is half right: the consideration is worth more, but only in the world where the deal closes, and the price weights that world by p.
04

Rates move an arb book twice, in opposite directions

A rate cut marks cash deals up today and lowers what they earn from then on. Stock deals barely notice.

Chapter 01 said arb returns are funding plus a premium. Chapter 02 said the price of a cash target is its expected payout discounted at the funding rate. Put them together and a rate change does two things at once.

Take a book that is 75% cash deals and 25% stock deals, every deal a year from closing, funded at 5.25%. Rates fall 100 basis points and stay there. Each cash target is priced off its payout divided by 1 + r, so the cut marks them up immediately: by about 0.96% each, or +0.72% on the book. Standalone values are assumed not to move.

From then on the book earns less. Cash deals now return a point less a year. Stock deals lose only the rebate on their net short — a quarter of their capital — so they give up a quarter point. Together the book's yearly return falls by 0.81%. Over the first year the mark-up and the lower carry nearly cancel, leaving −0.09%; every year after, the book simply earns about 0.8% less.

That is the relationship people get backwards. Lower rates feel good for arbitrage because the first thing you see is a mark-up. But merger arbitrage is a spread over funding, and spreads compress when funding gets cheaper. In the long run arb returns move with short rates, one-for-one on the cash part of the book.

Try this

Make the book all cash and cut rates 300 basis points. Predict whether year one is up, down or flat.

Check yourself

Rates fall 100 basis points and are expected to stay there. What happens to the expected return of a cash-heavy merger-arb portfolio?

Lower going forward, after a one-off gain. The mark-up is the market re-pricing the spreads to the new, lower required return; what you collect afterwards is that lower return. The fourth answer forgets that stock deals are funded by their own short and barely care.
05

A second request costs odds and time, and the tree says how much of each

The antitrust process is a sequence of decision points. Price each one as a node and the stock's reaction to news becomes arithmetic.

A US merger review runs through a few predictable gates. The companies file under HSR, and within thirty days the FTC or DoJ either lets the deal proceed or issues a second request. A second request means months of document production. At the end the regulator either clears the deal or sues to block it, and a judge decides the lawsuit.

A desk turns that into a tree. Each gate is a node; each branch has a probability; each node's value is the probability-weighted value of its children, discounted for the time that stage adds. The tree below is the one from a desk's own model of a real cash deal: a $11.50 takeout, a $7.51 break price, a 32.2% chance of clearing at HSR, 73.7% of clearing after a second request, and a 33.1% chance of winning a lawsuit. Multiply the branches through and the tree's own odds of closing are 88.1%.

With a 5% funding rate and the second request and lawsuit each adding about six months, the tree reproduces the desk's node prices to the cent: $10.79 today, $10.47 after a second request, $8.63 after a lawsuit.

Now the question desks actually ask: why does a second request knock 32 cents off the stock? Two separate reasons. The odds fall — once you are in the second-request branch, the chance of closing drops from 88.1% to 82.4%, because the quick-clearance path is gone and the lawsuit path is now live. And the wait lengthens — six more months of carry on an $11.50 claim. The tree splits the drop: about 23 cents from the odds and 9 cents from the time.

V(node) = [ p · V(close) + (1 − p) · V(next node) ] / (1 + r · t)

Reads as: each node is worth the average of where it can go next, discounted for how long getting there takes. Work backwards from the leaves and the whole tree prices itself.

Try this

Make the second request instant — zero added months — and predict how much of the 32-cent drop survives. Then make the lawsuit a coin flip.

Check yourself

A cash deal gets a second request and the target falls from $10.79 to $10.47. Name the two components.

Probability and time. The third answer is the trap, and chapter 01 is why: time is the denominator of every spread. The fourth answer can be true of a real deal, but it is a change in a leaf of the tree, not in the second request itself.
Derivation — splitting the drop into odds and time

Price the tree twice: once with the real stage lengths and once with every stage instantaneous. The instantaneous tree has no carry at all, so the change in its value when you move from today's node to the second-request node is the probability effect alone.

odds effect = V₀(2nd request) − V₀(today) · time effect = total drop − odds effect

Reads as: remove time, measure what is left, and call the remainder time. It is the same split as a variance attribution, done on a tree.

Where it breaks. The branch probabilities are the desk's opinion, and a tree whose odds multiply to 88% while the market trades the stock at an implied 80% is a disagreement, not a fact — that gap is either the desk's edge or the desk's blind spot. Timelines are worse than probabilities: courts and regulators do not publish schedules, and the length of a lawsuit is one of the least predictable numbers in the whole calculation. Foreign regulators — the CMA, SAMR, the European Commission — add their own trees, and the deal closes only if every one of them ends at the green box.

06

A special dividend is worth the odds times the dividend

The cash leaves the company whether or not the deal closes, so it only adds value in the world where somebody pays the full price for what is left.

A week after the GoPro announcement — $5 cash, $4 stock, $2 standalone, zero rates — the target declares a 30-cent special dividend, to be paid before the deal resolves. The acquirer says the $5 still stands. Where does the stock trade?

The tempting answer is $4.30: the old price plus the dividend. It double-counts. If the deal breaks, the company is worth $2 in total, but $0.30 of that has just been paid out — so the shares fall to $1.70 and you hold 30 cents of cash. Still $2.00. The dividend only adds value in the close state, where the acquirer pays $5 for a company that has already handed you 30 cents. So the stock moves by the odds times the dividend: 2/3 × 0.30 = 20 cents, to $4.20.

The same arithmetic on the VZIO deal: an $11.50 takeout trading at $10.79 on 80% implied odds announces a 20-cent special, and the takeout is unchanged. The stock should trade at about $10.95, up 16 cents — not 20.

Once the dividend goes ex, the stock drops by the full dividend, because now everybody's claim is lower by exactly that cash. Before the ex-date, the dividend is a bet on the deal like everything else on this page.

S′ = p · (D + q) + (1 − p) · SV ⟹ S′ − S = p · q

Reads as: in the close state you get the deal price and the dividend; in the break state the dividend came out of the standalone value, so nothing changes. The price moves by the odds times the dividend.

Check yourself

GoPro trades at $4 on a $5 cash deal and a $2 standalone, at zero rates. It declares a $0.30 special paid regardless of the outcome, and the deal price is unchanged. Where should it trade?

$4.20. The fourth answer is what happens on the ex-date, not on the announcement. The first answer is the common one, and the error is treating the standalone value as if it did not pay for the dividend.
07

Options on a binary outcome price the shape of the bad hump

A deal or a drug trial turns one distribution into two. The cheapest options on the chain are a claim on where the second hump sits.

Before a binary event resolves, the stock's distribution is a mixture: a hump around the success price and another around the failure price, weighted by the odds. Every option on the chain is the probability-weighted average of its two conditional prices, and nothing in Black–Scholes' single bell curve can describe that.

Here is the case that makes it tradeable. A biotech at $20 has a trial readout coming. The market thinks failure takes it to $10; assume success takes it to $26, which together with the $20 price implies 62.5% odds. Your research team thinks a failure is far worse — $5, not $10. You do not need to disagree with the odds to have a trade. You need an option that only pays in the failure hump, below where the market thinks that hump sits.

The 10/5 put spread is that option. On the market's distribution it is worth $0.19; on yours, $1.78 — about nine times as much. The 7.5 put is the extreme version: about a fifth of a cent on the market's view and about 94 cents on yours. Strikes below the market's failure price are cheap precisely because the market puts almost no probability there, which is what makes them the right instrument when you disagree about the floor rather than about the odds.

The same shape governs options on deal stocks. A target in a cash deal is pinned near the takeout and its day-to-day volatility collapses — chapter 08 of page 08 shows the certain-deal limit, where the calls above the deal price are worth nothing. In a stock deal the target inherits the acquirer's volatility, scaled by the exchange ratio, so its non-catalyst vol stays high. A collar deal sits between: inside the collar band the consideration is fixed in value and the target behaves like a cash deal, outside it the target moves like stock. So the ranking of non-catalyst volatility is stock, then collar, then cash.

And it answers a question that sounds like a riddle: you are short the January 50 calls on a $30 stock — what is the best headline you could wake up to? Not a crash, if you are hedged long the shares. The best headline is an all-cash takeover below $50: the stock jumps, your long-delta hedge makes money, and the calls you are short lose their volatility and can never reach the strike, so they decay toward the value of a topping bid. Every option you are short gets cheaper and every share you hold gets dearer.

option = p · BS(success price) + (1 − p) · BS(failure price)

Reads as: price the option in each world and weight by the odds. The smile you would see on such a chain is not a statement about volatility at all; it is the two humps, flattened into one number per strike.

Check yourself

The market prices a failed trial at $10 from $20. You believe $5. What trade expresses that view most efficiently?

Puts below the market's floor. You have no view on the odds, only on the failure price, so you want an instrument that is insensitive to the odds and sensitive to the floor. The at-the-money put pays mostly on whether the trial fails, which is the market's question, not yours. The straddle bets on movement the market has already priced. The short sale makes money on any failure and loses on success, which is a bet on the odds.

Question 1 · chapter 02

GoPro is at $2 when a $5 all-cash take-private is announced. Rates are zero and the stock goes to $4. What odds is the market pricing?

Two-thirds. S = pD + (1 − p)SV gives p = (4 − 2)/(5 − 2). The first answer divides by the wrong range: it treats a broken deal as worthless rather than as a return to $2.
Question 2 · chapter 02

Same deal, resolving in a year, funded at 10%. What are the odds?

80%. The shortcut charges interest only in the close state; your money costs the same if the deal breaks. Chapter 02 has the derivation.
Question 3 · chapter 03

Instead of cash, the acquirer pays $5 in its own stock. Ignoring the rebate, what are the odds, and what is the most you can lose?

Two-thirds, and more than $2. The first answer is what most people say and it is the number that holds only if the acquirer does not move. Chapter 03's red line is the rest of it.
Question 4 · chapter 04

Rates fall 100 basis points for a decade. Your arb book is 75% cash deals closing in a year. What do you expect?

Up today, lower forever. The mark-up is the spread compressing to the new required return; what remains to be earned is smaller by about the cut on the cash share. Chapter 04.
Question 5 · chapter 06

VZIO trades at $10.79 on an $11.50 cash takeout, implying 80% odds. It announces a $0.20 special; the takeout is unchanged and the odds do not move. Where does it trade?

Up 16 cents. If the deal breaks the company is worth the same in total after paying the cash out. Chapter 06.
Question 6 · chapter 07

Which target has the highest non-catalyst volatility: one in a cash deal, a stock deal, or a collar deal?

Stock, then collar, then cash. Non-catalyst volatility is what the target does while nothing happens to the deal, and that is set by what the consideration is made of.

Every number on this page uses simple interest, a single funding rate and a fixed standalone value, which is how spreads are quoted rather than how they behave. Real standalone values move with the market and with what the target did while it waited; real timelines slip; real deals have financing conditions, material-adverse-change clauses, competing bids and votes that are not drawn as branches here. The VZIO tree's branch probabilities and timings are a desk's model, reproduced to show the mechanics, and the biotech success price is an assumption. Antitrust doctrine and regulatory practice change with administrations — the Chicago school's focus on consumer prices and the newer emphasis on market power produce different trees for the same deal — and nothing here is a forecast of how any regulator will act.
