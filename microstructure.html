Every price has someone behind it

Five pages of this series have produced prices from models. A real option has a bid, an ask, a queue, and a counterparty who may know something you don't — and the difference between the model price and the one you get is where a desk actually lives.

01

What a tick is worth

Options are quoted in price and traded in volatility. The exchange rate between them varies by a factor of three hundred across a single chain.

A market maker thinks in volatility. The screen shows price. The conversion between them is vega, and because vega varies enormously across a chain while the tick size does not, the same one-cent increment means completely different things at different strikes.

On a six-month at-the-money option, vega is large — a cent is about 0.04 vol points, an amount too small to care about. On a seven-day five-delta option, vega is tiny, and that same cent is 11.57 vol points. A nickel-wide market is 0.2 vol wide in the first case and 58 vol wide in the second.

This single table explains a set of things that otherwise look like separate facts. Why far out-of-the-money short-dated markets appear absurdly wide in percentage terms. Why you cannot quote them tightly even if you want to — the tick will not let you. Why a market maker will happily show a penny market in one option and refuse to show anything reasonable in another on the same underlying. And why an "edge" of two cents is meaningless until you know which option it was in.

The practical version: before reacting to a quoted spread, convert it to vol. A market that looks scandalous in cents is often unremarkable in vol, and occasionally the reverse.

Derivation — why one tick is worth 300 times more on one option than another

A quoted price and a quoted volatility are the same information in different units. The exchange rate between them is vega.

Δσ = Δprice / vega

Reads as: to convert a price move into a volatility move, divide by how much the option's price changes per volatility point. Everything about this chapter is that one division.

So the volatility value of a fixed tick is not a property of the tick. It is a property of the denominator, and vega collapses in exactly the situations where the tick does not.

vega = S·e−qT·n(d1)·√T

Reads as: vega is large when there is time left and the option is near the money, and vanishes otherwise. The √T kills short expiries; the n(d₁) kills far strikes.

Both factors shrink together on a short-dated out-of-the-money option, so the denominator becomes tiny while the numerator stays fixed at one cent. That is the whole explanation for the 0.04-versus-11.57 figure in the table: the numerator never changed.

Where it breaks. The conversion assumes there is an implied volatility to move — that the quoted price sits inside the no-arbitrage bounds and the option has enough value to invert. On the deepest, shortest options the price is essentially all intrinsic and the implied volatility is barely identified, so a one-tick move can imply an enormous volatility change or none at all depending on rounding. At that point the vol quote is an artefact of the price grid rather than a measurement, which is why desks stop quoting those series in volatility and start quoting them in cents.

Try this

Set the expiry to seven days and drag the strike far out of the money. Predict roughly how many volatility points one cent is worth before you read it — most people are out by a factor of ten.

Check yourself

A weekly 5-delta call is quoted 0.05 at 0.10. Is that a wide market?

Convert it to vol first. Five cents on an option with a vega of roughly 0.001 per point is dozens of vol points, which sounds appalling and is close to the tightest anyone can show — the next tick down is 0.04, a whole increment away. The percentage-of-premium framing is the least useful one available, because the denominator is tiny by construction. The last answer is not wrong that delta matters, but it is vega, not delta, that sets the conversion.
02

What is actually inside a spread

Four components, and only one of them is profit.

A quoted market is not a fee. It is the sum of several distinct costs the market maker faces, plus whatever is left over.

Hedging cost. Take the other side of an option and you immediately need delta in the underlying, which means crossing another spread. That cost scales with the option's delta and with how liquid the stock is.

Inventory risk. Having taken the position you now hold vega and gamma you did not choose, possibly for days. The wider the position's risk, the more you need paying to warehouse it.

Adverse selection. Some of the people trading with you know more than you do, and they systematically trade when your quote is wrong. This is not a possibility to be insured against but a certainty to be priced, and it is chapter 03's subject.

Fees. Exchange, clearing and regulatory charges, small per contract and not small in aggregate.

What remains is edge. Move the sliders and notice how rarely it is the largest component — and that in the wings, where markets look widest, hedging and inventory dominate while edge is often thinner than in the tight at-the-money market next to it.

03

Quote a market yourself

Set a width. Get filled. Then look at who filled you and what happened next.

Adverse selection is usually defined and rarely felt. Here it is a number you can move.

You quote a two-sided market some number of vol points wide around your fair value. Flow arrives. Uninformed traders hit your quote because they want the option — the tighter you are, the more of them you win. Informed traders only trade when your quote is wrong by more than their edge, which means every fill from them is a fill you did not want.

Quote too tight and the arithmetic is brutal. At a 0.20 vol-point half-width you get filled on 321 of 400 arrivals and lose 31.3 — you are winning enormous volume and most of the volume that matters is picking you off. Widen and volume collapses while P&L per fill climbs; at 3.50 wide you make 2.41 per fill and only trade six times.

The optimum sits in between, around 1.2 vol points in these settings, and it is the same shape as page 06's execution problem viewed from the other side of the trade — a quantity you want more of, against a cost that grows with getting it.

Watch the informed percentage column as you widen. It rises: informed traders are the last to be deterred, because they only trade when they have an edge big enough to cross your spread. You cannot widen your way out of adverse selection, only price it.

Try this

Quote absurdly tight — a tenth of a volatility point — and predict whether you make money on volume. Then widen until you barely trade.

Check yourself

You widen your quotes to reduce adverse selection. What happens to the share of your fills that are informed?

It rises. Widening deters the uninformed first — they are trading for reasons unrelated to your quote's accuracy, so a worse price simply sends them elsewhere. The informed only ever traded when they had an edge, and a wider spread merely raises the bar on how large that edge must be. So the flow you retain gets more toxic even as it gets less harmful in aggregate. The right conclusion is that widening manages the size of the problem, never its composition.
04

Where the liquidity actually is

Volume and open interest answer different questions, and the chain is far more concentrated than it looks.

A chain shows hundreds of lines. A small fraction of them carry nearly all the risk.

Volume is what traded today — a flow measure, and it resets every session. Open interest is what remains outstanding — a stock measure, and it tells you where positions actually sit. A line with heavy volume and no open interest is day-trading churn; a line with low volume and enormous open interest is where somebody's risk lives, and it is the one that matters on expiration.

Liquidity concentrates in front-month, near-the-money strikes, and falls away fast in both directions. The practical consequences: anything you want to trade in size should be checked against open interest before you assume you can get out, a "cheap" far-dated wing option may be cheap because nobody will ever bid for it again, and the greeks you compute on an illiquid line are theoretical in a way the greeks on the ATM line are not.

The one that catches people: you can put on a position you cannot take off. Establishing a wing position by paying up is easy; exiting requires somebody to want the other side, and in the wings there frequently isn't one at any price you would accept.

Concentrate the open interest hard into the front month and predict what happens to everything outside it.

05

Dealer gamma, with the condition attached

The mechanism is real. It is almost always stated without the assumption it depends on.

The story goes: dealers are long gamma, so they sell rallies and buy dips to stay hedged, which dampens the market. Or they are short gamma, so they buy rallies and sell dips, which amplifies it. Both halves of that are correct as descriptions of hedging behavior.

What is usually left out is that hedging flow only moves the market if it is large relative to everything else trading. That is a condition, it is quantitative, and it is frequently not met.

The simulation makes the dependence explicit by putting price impact on a slider. At low impact the three cases — no hedging, long-gamma dealers, short-gamma dealers — are statistically indistinguishable. My first attempt at this figure used an impact coefficient small enough that the effect vanished entirely into the noise, and that failure is worth more than the eventual success: the mechanism is real and its magnitude is an empirical question with a frequently unimpressive answer.

Raise impact enough and the textbook result appears cleanly. Long-gamma dealers pull spot toward the strike — mean distance falls from 2.46 to 1.50, and the share finishing within fifty cents rises from 12.2% to 19.4%. Short-gamma dealers push it away: mean distance 4.41, with only 0.8% finishing near the strike.

Two honest caveats to carry alongside any dealer-positioning argument. Positioning is inferred, not observed — nobody publishes net dealer gamma, and the estimates rest on assumptions about who initiated each trade. And the flows compete with everything else in the market, so the same nominal gamma matters enormously on a quiet expiration Friday in a single name and not at all on a heavy day in an index.

Try this

Drop the price impact toward zero, keeping dealer gamma large. Predict whether pinning survives on positioning alone.

Check yourself

Someone argues that large dealer long gamma will suppress volatility this week. What is the missing piece of the claim?

The flow has to be big enough to matter. The hedging behavior is real and its direction is correctly described, but the price effect is the product of that flow and the market's price impact — and if the gamma is small relative to a day's volume, the product is indistinguishable from zero. That is exactly what the simulation shows at low impact. Add that dealer positioning is estimated from trade-side inference and you have two places for a confident-sounding claim to be quietly wrong.
06

Expiration

The one day when open interest, gamma and a fixed deadline all point at the same price.

Everything in this page compounds on expiration day. Gamma is at its maximum, so hedging flows per unit of price move are at their largest. Open interest is concentrated at round strikes. And the deadline is absolute — whatever is unhedged at the close is a decision, not a position.

Pinning is the observable result, and the mechanism is chapter 05's with the dial turned up. But two other things happen that are worth knowing separately.

Delta discontinuity. An option's delta at expiry is a step function, so a stock oscillating around a heavily-owned strike hands hedgers alternating large buy and sell orders. That is what makes the last hour at a pinned strike look strange.

The exercise decision. Near the money at the close, whether a contract is exercised stops being automatic and becomes a judgement about where the stock will open. This is where page 07's assignment mechanics and page 08's early-exercise arithmetic meet the microstructure — and it is the reason expiration is an operations problem as much as a trading one.

The practical stance: treat a large open-interest strike as a place where the price is more likely to spend the last session than a random walk would suggest, and treat that tendency as a weak statistical regularity rather than a reliable trade. The effect is real, measurable, and much smaller than the confidence with which it is usually discussed.

07

What a market maker's day actually optimizes

Not the direction of the stock. Inventory, and the price of being wrong about it.

The popular picture of a market maker is someone with a view who trades on it. The real job is closer to the opposite: quote both sides, try to end the day with as little position as possible, and get paid the spread for the service. The optimisation is over inventory, not over price.

That changes what the quotes are for. A market maker who is long too much does not simply hedge and carry on — they skew: they move both bid and offer down, so that selling becomes more likely and buying less likely, and the position drifts back toward flat on its own. The skew is the inventory control, and it is why the mid-price you observe is not the market maker's estimate of fair value. It is fair value plus a position adjustment you cannot see.

Three consequences follow, and each explains something that looks irrational from outside.

The quote moves when nothing happened. A market can reprice by a tick with no trade in the underlying and no news, because someone's inventory changed. Reading that as information is a mistake, and it is a common one.

Size is priced separately from price. The quote you see is for the quoted size. Asking for ten times that is a different trade with a different price, because it moves the maker's inventory ten times as far and the skew has to pay for it.

Widening is a position statement. A maker who has been picked off repeatedly on one side widens that side specifically. An asymmetric market is usually a maker telling you what they already own.

The figure runs a day. Orders arrive, the maker quotes with a skew proportional to inventory, and the position wanders. Turn the inventory aversion up and the position hugs zero while the spread capture falls, because skewing away from the market costs you fills. Turn it down and you capture more spread and end the day with a position you did not choose — and the P&L standard deviation, which is the number a risk manager cares about, grows with it.

The result is the same shape as page 06's U-curve and page 05's own quoting simulation: an interior optimum between two costs that run in opposite directions. It is worth noticing how often that shape has turned up in this series, because it is what almost every real trading decision looks like once both sides of it are priced.

quote mid = fair value − k · inventory

Reads as: the price you are shown is the maker's estimate of value, shifted by how much they already own. k is how aggressively they want the position gone.

Try this

Set inventory aversion to zero and predict whether the day's spread capture or the end-of-day position risk grows faster as you turn it back up.

Check yourself

A market maker's quote drops a tick with no trade in the underlying and no news. What is the most likely explanation?

Their inventory changed. The observed mid is fair value plus a position adjustment, and the adjustment is invisible from outside. Treating every quote move as information is how people end up chasing a market that is only trying to go home flat — and it is the reason a single maker's mid is a worse estimate of fair value than the consensus across several.

Question 1 · chapter 01

One cent on a 180-day at-the-money option is 0.04 vol points. Roughly what is it on a 7-day 5-delta?

About 11.6 points — nearly 300 times more. The tick is fixed in price and wildly variable in volatility, because the conversion divides by vega and vega vanishes for short-dated far strikes.
Question 2 · chapter 02

What is left in a bid–offer spread if no trader is informed?

Most of it survives. Adverse selection is the component people name because it is the interesting one, but a maker still has to hedge, carry inventory, pay fees and earn something.
Question 3 · chapter 03

You widen your quoted market. What happens to the share of your fills that are informed?

It rises. This is the counterintuitive result from the quoting simulation: widening filters out uninformed flow first, because the informed trader's edge still clears the wider spread. You trade less and a larger fraction of what remains is against you.
Question 4 · chapter 05

Under what condition does dealer gamma actually pin a stock?

Only above a flow-size threshold. The narrative is usually stated without this condition, and the condition is what makes it a claim rather than a story. Below it the simulated effect disappears completely.
Question 5 · chapter 06

A stock settles one cent in the money against a strike you are short. What is the risk?

A position, not a cent. Page 08 puts the numbers on it: $20 of intrinsic against roughly $3,800 of weekend risk on ten contracts. Expiry closes the option, not the stock position it delivers.
Question 6 · chapter 07

Why does a market maker skew their quotes?

Inventory management. The mid you observe is fair value plus a position adjustment, which is why a maker's quote can move with no news and why asking for size is a different trade from the one on the screen.
