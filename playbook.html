01

Every strategy is paid for a shape of risk

Sell insurance and you earn steady premiums with a rare large loss. Buy it and you bleed steadily for a rare large gain. Same market, opposite shapes.

Options strategies earn money for a small number of reasons, and the most important one is that somebody is paying to get rid of a risk. Pensions buy puts because a crash would breach their mandate; corporates buy collars because their board demands a floor; investors buy structured notes for a coupon and sell crash risk to get it. Whoever takes the other side collects a premium — and inherits the risk.

That makes the shape of the returns the real product. The figure runs one simulated 30-year market through three strategies. Ordinary months have 15% volatility; crash months arrive about once a decade and cost a quarter of the index. Options are priced at implied volatility 25% above the ordinary months' realized — a variance risk premium in line with what index options have historically carried — and the far put is marked up again for skew.

In the default history, systematic put-writing wins in 93% of months and has a skewness of −9.2: hundreds of small gains and a few losses as large as 19% in a month. The index grows 13.0% a year with a worst drawdown of 26%. The index with a rolled tail hedge — 15%-out-of-the-money puts bought every month — grows 13.6% with a worst drawdown of 19%: in this history the hedge paid for itself in the crash months. Press "New history" a few times and watch how much that last result depends on when the crashes land. That dependence is the whole argument about tail hedging, in one button.

premium earned ≈ (implied variance − realized variance) × exposure, paid in small pieces, lost in large ones

Reads as: the seller of insurance earns the gap between what the market charges for volatility and what it delivers — but collects it steadily and pays it back in lumps. The buyer has the mirror image.

Take the variance risk premium to zero and predict what happens to put-writing. Then make crashes three times as frequent.

Check yourself

A put-writing fund shows 93% winning months and a steady equity curve for six years. What is the most important number you do not yet have?

The tail. Negative-skew strategies look best right before they look worst. Sharpe ratios computed over calm periods flatter them, and the win rate is high by construction (page 18). Ask what the fund lost in the worst month it could plausibly face, and whether it was leveraged into it.
02

The playbook

Fifteen strategies people and institutions actually run. Each pays for a reason, and each has a way it fails that the people running it know about and sometimes forget.

Read each strategy in the same order: why it gets paid, how it is run, how it loses, and what the people who survive it do differently. The pattern repeats. Strategies that sell insurance — put-writing, overwriting, short volatility, dispersion, structured-product issuance — earn steadily and lose in crises, all at once. Strategies that buy it — tail hedging, long volatility — bleed and pay off rarely. Strategies that sell a service — market making, financing — earn a spread and lose to informed flow or plumbing. The edge in all of them comes from the five sources in page 04's field guide; the strategy is just how it is packaged.

Check yourself

Why do dispersion trades, put-writing and short-VIX products tend to lose money in the same weeks?

Same risk, different wrappers. Each is paid a premium for bearing crash risk, so a crash collects on all of them together. A book that holds several of them is more concentrated than its strategy list suggests — page 11's stress limits exist for exactly this.
03

Choosing a strategy is choosing which risk you can carry

Not which one has the best backtest. Which tail you can survive, which bleed you can tolerate, and which edge you can actually measure.

Four questions sort the playbook for any individual or institution.

Which way does your tail point? If a crash would end your business, you should be buying insurance, not selling it, even though insurance loses money on average. If you can survive a crash and hold through it — a pension with a long horizon, a fund with locked-up capital — you may be the natural seller, and you are paid for it.

Can you measure the edge? Every strategy here has a measurable premium: implied versus realized, implied versus realized correlation, the event move versus history, the roll yield, the spread net of markouts. If you cannot state yours as a number and track it, you are running the risk without knowing whether you are being paid for it.

What does it cost to run? Execution, hedging frequency, financing and borrow can consume a premium that looked generous on paper (page 06). The strategies that look best in backtests are often the ones with the most hidden trading.

How big? The same strategy is prudent at one size and ruinous at three times it. Size to the worst plausible month, and use a fraction of Kelly when the edge is an estimate (toolkit). Most famous options blow-ups were sound strategies at the wrong size.

Check yourself

A long-horizon pension and a leveraged hedge fund both consider systematic index put-writing. Who is the more natural seller?

The one that can carry the tail. The premium is the same for both; the ability to survive the bad month is not. Leverage converts a drawdown into a forced exit at the worst price, which is why the same strategy can be sensible for one owner and fatal for another.

The simulation in chapter 01 is a deliberately simple world — two regimes, a fixed premium, monthly options priced at a single implied vol with a crude skew adjustment — chosen to show the shape of each strategy's returns, not to forecast them. Real premiums vary with the volatility regime, real crashes cluster and arrive with partial warning, and real strategies pay transaction costs this one ignores. The strategy descriptions summarize widely documented practice; specific products, mandates and rules change, and none of this is a recommendation to run any of them.
