The straddle measures something else

A standard deviation and an average absolute move are two different rulers laid against the same distribution. An option pays for one of them and the screen quotes the other, and the gap between the two is twenty-five percent of a number people compare to two decimal places.

Scroll ↓

Start here

Page 03 established the identity in one line: a straddle costs about 0.7979·S·σ√T, so the implied move it quotes is about 80% of a one-sigma move. This page is about why that number is 0.7979, what happens when it is not, and what the difference does to a position.

  1. Chapter 02 is the conversion to have automatic. Divide a straddle-implied move by 0.7979 — equivalently multiply by 1.2533 — to get a standard deviation. Reading the implied move as one sigma understates volatility by 20.2% of itself.
  2. Chapter 03 is the part page 03 does not cover. That 0.7979 is the normal distribution's ratio and nothing more. Under fatter tails it falls — to exactly 3/4 for a t with six degrees of freedom, and to 1/√2 for a t with four.
  3. Chapter 04 is the one that surprises people. Fat tails make the stock exceed the straddle-implied move less often, not more. The straddle buyer wins less frequently and wins bigger.

01

Two rulers, one distribution

Squaring the deviations before averaging them is a choice, and it produces a different number from not squaring them.

Ask how spread out a distribution is and there are two honest answers. Average the absolute deviations, or average the squared deviations and take the root. Both are legitimate; neither is an approximation to the other.

The second is the standard deviation, and it dominates finance for reasons that are mostly about convenience: variances add, so a two-day variance is twice a one-day variance and the √T scaling of page 01 follows immediately. Absolute deviations do not add like that, which is why nobody builds a model on them.

The first is the mean absolute deviation. It has no such algebra, but it has something else: it is what an option pays. A straddle held to expiry pays |ST − K|, an absolute value with no square in sight.

Because squaring weights the far observations more heavily, the standard deviation always comes out the larger of the two. For a normal distribution the ratio is √(2/π) = 0.797885, and it is worth seeing where that comes from — it is the average of |z| under a standard normal, which is a one-line integral rather than a fitted constant.

MAD = E|X − μ| σ = √( E[(X − μ)²] ) for a normal, MAD = √(2/π)·σ ≈ 0.7979·σ

Reads as: average the distances, or average the squared distances and take the root. The squaring is what makes the second larger, because it pays disproportionate attention to the observations far from the middle.

Check yourself

Why is the standard deviation always at least as large as the mean absolute deviation?

The squaring reweights before the averaging. This is Jensen's inequality doing the work — the root of a mean of squares is never below the mean of the absolute values, with equality only when every deviation is identical. It is also why the standard deviation is the more fragile of the two under fat tails, which is chapter 05.
02

What a straddle actually buys

The payoff is an absolute value, so the price is an expected absolute move. The screen then quotes it next to a volatility, which is a standard deviation.

A straddle struck at the forward pays |ST − K|. Its price is therefore the expected absolute move, discounted — and under Black–Scholes that expectation has a closed form which, for realistic vol-times, is almost exactly 0.7979·S·σ√T.

Take a concrete one. A 30-day straddle on a $100 stock at 30 vol costs $6.860. Quoted as an implied move that is 6.86% of spot. The one-standard-deviation move over the same period is 0.30 × √(30/365) = 8.60%. The ratio of the two is 0.7976, which is the theoretical 0.797885 to three decimals.

So the conversions, both worth having automatic:

Going from a quoted implied move to a volatility, divide by 0.7979 or multiply by 1.2533. Going the other way, multiply by 0.7979. And treating the implied move as though it were one sigma understates the volatility by 20.2% of itself — or, from the other end, the true sigma is 25.3% larger than the number being quoted.

The practical consequence is about frequency. If you think the implied move is one sigma you expect the stock to breach it 31.7% of the time. It actually breaches it 42.5% of the time, because the breakeven sits at 0.80 sigma rather than 1.00. That is a gap of nearly eleven percentage points in how often a straddle you bought finishes in the money, and it is the single most common miscalibration in reading an implied move.

σ = implied move ÷ 0.7979 = implied move × 1.2533 implied move = 0.7979 × σ

Reads as: the quoted move and the standard deviation differ by a fixed factor of about a quarter. Which one a sentence means is almost never stated, and the two get compared to each other constantly.

Check yourself

A screen shows an implied move of 5%. What is the one-standard-deviation move?

6.27%. And the difference matters for more than pedantry: at 5% you expect a breach 31.7% of the time and get 42.5%. Any comparison between a straddle-implied move and a historical standard deviation has to convert one to the other first, or it is off by a quarter before it starts.
03

0.7979 is the normal's number, and nothing else's

Change the shape of the tails and the ratio moves. For a t with six degrees of freedom it is exactly three quarters.

Everything in chapter 02 assumed a normal distribution. The straddle price is an expectation of an absolute value and does not care what shape produced it — but the conversion to a standard deviation does, entirely.

Under fatter tails the standard deviation is inflated by the extremes far more than the mean absolute deviation is, so the ratio between them falls. The exact values are unusually clean:

A uniform distribution, with no tails at all, gives √3/2 = 0.8660. The normal gives 0.7979. A Student t with 6 degrees of freedom gives exactly 3/4. A t with 4 gives exactly 1/√2 = 0.7071, which is also the Laplace value. A t with 3 gives exactly 2/π = 0.6366.

Now put that to work. A straddle quotes a 5% expected absolute move. Under a normal that implies a volatility of 6.27%. If the true distribution is a t with 4 degrees of freedom — fat, but not absurd for a single name over a month — the same straddle implies 7.07%, which is 12.8% higher. The option price did not change. The number you wrote down next to it did.

This is a real and underappreciated wedge. Every implied volatility on a screen was produced by inverting a model that assumes a normal distribution of log returns. To the extent the truth has fatter tails, the ATM implied vol is a statement about the expected absolute move dressed up as a statement about a standard deviation — and the two drift apart in exactly the situations, like a single name into a binary event, where you care most.

Where this comes from

For a standardized Student t with ν degrees of freedom, the mean absolute deviation and the standard deviation both have closed forms:

E|T| = 2√ν · Γ((ν+1)/2) ÷ ( √π · (ν−1) · Γ(ν/2) ) and σ = √( ν/(ν−2) ), so the ratio is the first divided by the second. The gamma functions collapse at small integer ν, which is why the values come out as recognizable constants — 2/π at ν = 3, 1/√2 at ν = 4, exactly 3/4 at ν = 6 — and why the limit as ν grows is √(2/π), the normal's value, approached from below.

Where it breaks: the standard deviation only exists for ν > 2, and the ratio is undefined below that — for a t with 2 degrees of freedom the variance is infinite while the mean absolute deviation is perfectly finite, so the straddle has a price and the volatility does not exist. That is not a pathology invented for a textbook; it is the formal version of the complaint that a single implied volatility cannot describe a distribution whose second moment is dominated by events that have not happened yet, which page 15 takes up directly. The Student t is also only a stand-in — real return distributions are skewed, and skew breaks the symmetry every formula here assumes.

Check yourself

Two markets quote the same 5% implied move, but one has visibly fatter tails. Which has the higher standard deviation?

The fat-tailed one, by 12.8%. The straddle price pins the expected absolute move, and that is all it pins. Converting it to a standard deviation requires a distributional assumption, and the standard one — normality — is the assumption that produces the smallest answer of any realistic candidate.
04

Fat tails mean fewer wins, not more

The straddle buyer's hit rate falls as the tails fatten. The payouts, when they come, get larger.

Here is a result that runs against most people's instinct. If the distribution has fatter tails, does the stock exceed the straddle-implied move more often or less often?

Less often. Under a normal, the breach rate is 42.49%. Under a t with 30 degrees of freedom it is 41.9%; with 10, 40.7%; with 6, 39.4%; with 4, 37.4%. Under a Laplace distribution the answer is exactly e⁻¹ = 36.79%.

The reason is that a fat-tailed distribution is not simply "more spread out." It is more concentrated in the middle and more spread in the extremes — that is what excess kurtosis means. The straddle prices the average absolute move, and the average is pulled upward by the rare large outcomes. So the breakeven sits further out relative to the bulk of the distribution, and the many quiet days fall short of it.

For a straddle buyer this is the whole character of the trade in one number. Fatter tails do not make the position win more often; they make it lose small more often and win big less often. The expected value can be identical while the path is completely different, which is page 16's distinction between an edge and a distribution of outcomes arriving in the most concrete possible form.

And it explains something that otherwise looks like a puzzle: sellers of options report high hit rates in exactly the markets where their tail risk is worst. The hit rate is high because the tail is fat. Those are the same fact.

Check yourself

You buy a straddle in a market with fat tails. Compared to a normal market at the same price, what changes?

Less often, and larger. Fat tails put more mass near zero as well as far out, and the near-zero mass is what determines the hit rate. This is also why a seller's high hit rate is not evidence of an edge — in the fattest-tailed markets it is a mechanical consequence of the shape, and the losses are waiting in the part of the distribution the hit rate does not describe.
05

Measuring volatility with absolute values

The same choice, made on realized data rather than implied. One estimator is steadier and one is honest, and they are not the same one.

The distinction is not only about pricing. Estimating realized volatility offers the identical choice: average the squared returns and take a root, or average the absolute returns and scale by √(π/2) = 1.2533.

Under a genuinely normal distribution the squared-return estimator wins, though not by much. Its sampling variance is σ²/2n against the absolute estimator's σ²(π/2 − 1)/n = 0.5708σ²/n, a relative efficiency of 87.6% — the absolute version needs 14.2% more data for the same precision. Its spread of estimates is wider by √((π/2 − 1) ÷ ½) = 1.068, and the figure below resamples enough times to land there.

Now contaminate it. Let 6% of days come from a distribution with four times the volatility — a mild version of what a real return series looks like. The true sigma is 1.3784. The squared estimator still converges to it. The absolute estimator converges to 1.1800 — which is √(π/2) times the mixture's own expected absolute value, and works out to 85.6% of the truth. Meanwhile the spreads swap places: the squared estimator's roughly triples while the absolute one's barely moves, leaving it about half as wide.

That is the trade, and it is not a free lunch in either direction. The absolute estimator is roughly twice as stable under contamination and biased low for the standard deviation, because it is faithfully measuring a different quantity — the mean absolute deviation — whose relationship to sigma just moved. The squared estimator remains unbiased and becomes wildly noisy.

Neither is wrong. The question is which quantity you actually need. If you are sizing against a tail, you want the noisy unbiased one and you should expect it to jump around. If you are tracking whether today is busier than last week, the stable one is telling you what you asked, and its bias is a constant you can ignore.

Check yourself

A desk switches from squared returns to absolute returns for its realized-vol number and finds it reads consistently low. Is the new estimator broken?

It is measuring the right thing and converting with the wrong constant. Exactly chapter 03's problem on realized data instead of implied. The estimator is faithful; the 1.2533 that turns it into a volatility assumes a shape the data does not have, and the shortfall is a rough measure of how far from normal the returns are.

Question 6 · chapter 05

Which realized-volatility estimator is preferable?

Neither dominates. Under a clean normal the RMS estimator is 87.6% efficient — better, but only just. Under contamination the absolute estimator is far steadier and biased low, because it is faithfully measuring a mean absolute deviation whose conversion to sigma has changed underneath it.

The straddle identity here assumes a strike at the forward and no rates or dividends; away from the forward the price is no longer a pure expected absolute move and the 0.7979 stops applying cleanly, which is why the number belongs to the at-the-money straddle specifically and not to option prices in general. The approximation is also always high — it truncates an expansion — by 0.03% at thirty vol and thirty days and by 0.22% at eighty vol, so it is a quoting convenience rather than a pricing tool. Every distribution compared in chapters 03 and 04 is symmetric, and real equity returns are not; skew breaks the symmetry that lets a single ratio describe both sides of the move, and a put-side implied move and a call-side one are genuinely different numbers on a skewed surface. The Student t is a convenient stand-in for fat tails rather than a claim about what returns are, and its degrees-of-freedom parameter is not something anyone can estimate reliably — page 15 is about that gap. The estimator comparison in chapter 05 uses a two-regime contamination, which is a mild caricature of a real return series: actual volatility clusters, so the extreme days arrive together rather than independently, and that makes both estimators noisier than shown here.
