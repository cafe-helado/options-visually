A decision worth exactly nothing

An American option carries a right its European twin does not: exercise it whenever you like. Priced across six hundred cases, that right is worth 0.0000000000002. Knowing the handful of situations where it suddenly isn't is most of what this subject is.

Scroll ↓

Start here

Almost everything on this page falls out of one equation, and that equation is put–call parity wearing a different hat. If you have read page 07 you already have it; if not, chapter 02 derives what you need in four lines.

  1. Chapters 01 and 02 are the whole argument. Exercising early costs you the option's time value, always, exactly. Everything after that is a catalogue of the things that can be worth more than time value, and there are only three.
  2. The call rule is exact; the put rule is not. Chapter 03's criterion is arithmetic you can do on a phone. Chapter 04's needs a tree, and it shows how badly the phone version misses.
  3. Chapters 06 and 07 are where the money actually goes. The theory says which options should be exercised. Assignment says which of yours were, and that is a lottery with a standard deviation.
  4. Market plumbing is dated and provisional. Auto-exercise thresholds, allocation methods and tender rules are conventions, not mathematics. They are labelled where they appear.
Notation — every symbol used on this page
KStrike. The price you pay (call) or receive (put) on exercise.
τTime remaining after the decision point, in years. Not time to the decision — time from it to expiry.
DThe cash dividend going ex, per share. The only ordinary thing that can pay for exercising a call early.
p(S)The European put struck at K with τ left. On this page it is not a trade, it is a price tag — the value of the insurance you throw away by exercising.
K(1−e−rτ)The interest floor. What it costs to hand over the strike today instead of at expiry.
S*The critical spot. Above it, exercising into the ex-date wins; below it, holding wins.
BoundaryFor a put, the curve of spot prices below which immediate exercise is optimal. It moves with time remaining.
Exercise by exceptionThe clearing house exercising in-the-money options automatically unless told not to. Currently at one cent in the money.
DeliverableWhat one contract actually delivers. Normally 100 shares. After a corporate action, whatever the adjustment memo says.
01

The right to exercise early is worth zero

Not small. Not usually negligible. Zero, to the last bit a double can hold — for a call on a stock that pays no dividend.

An American option can be exercised at any moment up to expiry; a European one only at expiry. The American clearly has more rights, so it should cost more. Price both in the same binomial tree, on a stock that pays no dividend, and the difference is not merely small — it is zero at every spot, every volatility, every level of rates.

Six hundred cases, spanning spot from 60 to 180 against a 100 strike, expiries from a month to two years, volatility from 15% to 60%, and rates from zero to ten percent. The largest gap anywhere in that grid is 1.85 × 10−13, which is not a small number so much as a rounding artefact of arithmetic on floating point. The extra right is genuinely worthless.

The reason is one line, and it is worth having before the figure. If you exercise a call you receive S − K. If instead you sell the call to somebody else, you receive at least S − Ke−rτ, because no call can trade below that without opening an arbitrage. And since Ke−rτ < K whenever rates are positive, that is strictly more than S − K. Selling beats exercising. Always.

So the right is never used, and a right that is never used is worth nothing. Notice the argument never mentioned volatility, moneyness or time — which is why the residual panel below stays pinned to zero no matter which slider you drag.

c ≥ S − Ke−rτ > S − K

Reads as: a call is always worth more alive than dead. The market will pay you the strike's discount for it; exercising throws that discount away.

Try this

Set rates to zero. The inequality above collapses from strict to weak — exercise stops being strictly worse and becomes merely never better. Predict: does the residual panel finally lift off zero?

Check yourself

You are long a deep in-the-money call on a non-dividend payer, delta 0.99, with three months left. You want the stock. What is the cheapest way to get it?

Sell the call and buy the stock. "The time value is gone" is the misconception this whole page exists to kill. At delta 0.99 the insurance component is gone — the put is worth essentially nothing — but the other component is not. You still hold the right to pay K three months from now instead of today, and on a 100 strike at 4.5% that is worth about $1.10 per share. Exercising hands it to whoever is short. The one genuine argument on the other side is financing, and it points the same way: a long call ties up less capital than long stock, which is a reason to keep the call, not to exercise it.
Derivation — why the bound holds, and the assumptions in it

Consider two portfolios. The first is one call plus Ke−rτ in cash. The second is one share.

at expiry:   max(ST − K, 0) + K   vs   ST

Reads as: the call-plus-cash portfolio is never worth less than the share. If the stock finishes above the strike they are equal; if below, the cash alone beats it.

A portfolio that is never worth less at expiry cannot be worth less today, or you would buy it, sell the share, and bank the difference. So c + Ke−rτ ≥ S, which rearranges to the bound. Since a call is also never negative, we can write it as c ≥ max(S − Ke−rτ, 0).

Now compare with what exercise pays, S − K. Because Ke−rτ < K for any positive rate and any positive time, the bound sits strictly above the exercise value. Not approximately above — strictly, everywhere, with a gap of exactly K(1 − e−rτ), which is the number chapter 02 names.

Where it breaks. Three places, and each is a later chapter. The argument assumed the share pays nothing over the option's life, which chapter 03 removes. It assumed rates are positive, and at exactly zero the strict inequality becomes weak. And it assumed the only thing you can do with a share is hold it, which chapter 09 removes when a tender offer makes shares temporarily worth more than their own market price. Outside those three, this is not a heuristic. It is a theorem, and the residual panel is what a theorem looks like when you plot it.

02

Exercising early costs exactly the time value

And time value has exactly two ingredients. Naming them turns every question on this page into subtraction.

Chapter 01 proved that exercising a call early is a mistake, but it did not price the mistake. It should, because the moment a dividend appears the question becomes quantitative: is what I gain by owning the share today larger than what I give up? The answer is clean enough to be surprising. What you give up is the option's time value, precisely, with no residual and no approximation.

Time value is the part of the price that is not intrinsic: c − (S − K) for a call that is in the money. Take put–call parity, c − p = S − Ke−rτ, and rearrange it into that expression. Two terms fall out, and they are worth naming, because they are the two reasons anybody ever hesitates.

The first is the interest floor, K(1 − e−rτ): exercising means paying the strike now rather than later, and money now is worth more than money later. The second is the put, p(S): holding the call means keeping a floor under your downside, and exercising converts you into a shareholder with no floor at all. Insurance and interest. That is the whole of time value.

The identity below is checked live against the Black–Scholes price. Drag any slider and the residual column stays at the tenth decimal — across 1,728 test cases the worst it ever got was 4.4 × 10−14. This matters more than it looks. It means you never need an option pricer to decide whether to exercise. You need a put quote, which is already on the screen in front of you.

time value  =  K(1 − e−rτ)  +  p(S)

Reads as: what you throw away by exercising is the interest on the strike plus the insurance you were carrying. Everything else on this page is a question about whether something is bigger than this.

Try this

Push spot to 180 on a 100 strike. The blue band vanishes and total time value collapses onto the ochre line. Predict the number it lands on before you look — then triple the volatility and see whether it moves.

Check yourself

Two 100-strike calls, same 45 days to expiry, same stock. One is quoted on a 20 volatility, the other on a 60. Whose interest floor is bigger?

Identical, and that is the useful part. The floor is pure discounting: strike, rate, time. Volatility and spot enter time value only through the put. This is what makes the night-before screen in chapter 05 a two-column sort rather than a modelling exercise — you can rule out most of a chain using a number that needs no volatility input at all, and only then price the survivors.
Derivation — four lines from parity

Start with put–call parity for European options on a non-dividend payer.

c − p = S − Ke−rτ

Reads as: a call minus a put is stock financed to expiry. Page 07 builds this from scratch; here we only rearrange it.

Solve for the call, then subtract the intrinsic value S − K, which is what exercise would pay.

c − (S − K) = p + S − Ke−rτ − S + K

Reads as: time value equals the put plus whatever is left of the strike terms. The two spot terms cancel, which is why the answer contains no spot except through the put.

time value = p + K(1 − e−rτ)

Reads as: insurance plus interest, and nothing else.

Where it breaks. The parity relation used here is the European one, so p must be a European put value — not the American put quoted on the screen beside it, which is worth more, and chapter 04 shows by how much. For short-dated options on a liquid name the difference is pennies and the screen quote is fine. For a long-dated put on a high-rate, low-volatility name it is not, and substituting the quote will make you exercise calls you should have kept. The second failure is the dividend the derivation assumed away: once the stock pays one before expiry, parity itself picks up a term — which is precisely what makes the next chapter possible.

03

Only a dividend can pay for it

And most dividends can't. There is a floor a dividend must clear before exercise is possible at any spot, on any stock, at any volatility.

A dividend is the one ordinary thing that makes owning the share today better than owning the right to it. If you hold the stock through the ex-date you collect D; if you hold the call you do not, and the stock drops by roughly D underneath you. So on exactly one day in the option's life — the last moment before the stock goes ex — the calculation of chapter 02 has something on the other side of it.

Exercise then and you gain D. You give up the time value, which chapter 02 priced. Set gain against cost and the rule is one line of arithmetic with no pricer in it.

exercise into the ex-date  ⟺  D > K(1 − e−rτ) + p(S − D)

Reads as: take the dividend only if it beats the interest you give up plus the insurance you give up. τ is the time from the ex-date to expiry, and the put is valued at the ex-dividend price because that is what the stock will be worth the moment after.

This rule is exact, and the reason is chapter 01. After the ex-date there are no more dividends, so from that instant the American call is a European call and the only comparison that matters is the one above. This is a genuine closed form, not an approximation — which is unusual enough in American option pricing to be worth saying out loud, and it is why the call side of this subject is arithmetic while the put side needs a tree.

Two consequences fall straight out. The first is a hard filter. Because p is never negative, no dividend below the interest floor can ever be worth taking — not on the deepest call in the chain, not on the least volatile stock in the market. On a 100 strike at 4.5% rates, that floor is 8.6 cents with a week to run, 36.9 cents with a month, and $1.10 with three months. A quarterly dividend of twenty cents against a three-month expiry is not a close call; it is not a call at all.

The second is what the boundary curve does. Solve the rule for the spot at which it just balances and you get S*, the critical price: exercise above it, hold below it. As the ex-date moves closer to expiry, τ shrinks, both terms on the right shrink, and S* falls toward the strike. As the ex-date moves earlier, S* runs away upward and then simply ceases to exist — there is no spot high enough, because the insurance term dies but the interest floor does not.

Drag the dividend in the figure and watch the region appear. At D = 0.20 with 28 volatility there is a sliver of exercise territory inside two weeks of expiry and nothing beyond it. At D = 2.40 the region has swallowed most of the panel and a call only 2.4% in the money should be exercised with a month to run.

Check yourself

A stock at 118 pays a 55-cent dividend tomorrow. You are long the 100-strike calls, and there are four months to expiry. Rates are 4.5%. Do you exercise?

No, and the floor settles it before you look at anything else. K(1−e−rτ) with K = 100, r = 4.5% and τ = 4/12 is $1.4888. The dividend is 55 cents. Since the put can only add to the hurdle, no volatility assumption and no put quote can rescue this. The third answer is the tempting one because it uses the right equation — it just forgets that the equation has two terms on the right, and here the term people ignore is three times the size of the one they check.
Derivation — the rule, and why it is exact rather than approximate

Stand at the instant before the stock goes ex. Spot is S. You have two choices.

Exercise. You pay K, receive the share worth S, and hold it through the ex-date, so you also collect D. But collecting D is not a gain relative to the share price — the share drops to S − D and you hold D in cash, so your position is worth S − K. That is the exercise value, as usual.

Hold. A moment later the stock is at S − D and you own a call with τ to run on a stock that will pay nothing more. By chapter 01 that call is European, so parity prices it exactly.

c(S − D) = (S − D) − Ke−rτ + p(S − D)

Reads as: the surviving call is worth the ex-dividend stock, financed, plus the put. No inequality and no approximation — the option really is European from here.

Exercising is better exactly when its value exceeds the surviving call's.

S − K > S − D − Ke−rτ + p(S − D)

Reads as: compare the two numbers directly. The spot terms cancel, leaving only strike, dividend and put.

D > K(1 − e−rτ) + p(S − D)

Reads as: the dividend has to beat interest plus insurance. The same two ingredients as chapter 02, now with something on the other side of the scale.

Where it breaks. The exactness rests on there being no further dividend after this one, which is false for most of the year on most dividend payers — with two ex-dates left the option is not European after the first, and the honest version is a tree. In practice the rule stays close because the second dividend's effect on the surviving call is small compared with the first decision, but "close" is not "exact" and the distinction matters at the boundary. The other soft spot is D itself: you are using an announced dividend for a date that has not happened, and the stock does not always drop by the full amount on the ex-date. Tax treatment, index rebalancing and simple noise all move it, and the drop is only on average the dividend.

04

The put runs on interest instead

Same equation, roles swapped. The put's early-exercise value is made of rates, and at zero rates it does not exist.

Everything so far has been about calls, where the thing you gain by exercising early is a dividend and the thing you lose is interest. For puts both halves swap. Exercising a put means receiving K today rather than at expiry — you gain the interest — and it means giving up a call and any dividend you would have collected by not being short the share.

Write it in the same shape as chapter 03 and the symmetry is exact.

exercise the put  ⟸  K(1 − e−rτ) > D + c(S)

Reads as: take the strike early only if the interest on it beats the dividends and the upside you are handing back. Note the single arrow: unlike chapter 03 this is a necessary condition, not an exact one.

Two things follow immediately. First, set r = 0 and the left side is zero while the right side cannot be negative — so a put on a non-dividend payer is never exercised early in a zero-rate world, and the American put collapses onto the European one. The figure shows this as a boundary that simply is not drawn: at zero rates there is no spot, at any time remaining, where exercise wins.

Second, turn rates up and the premium becomes large fast. On a 100-strike one-year put at 30 volatility with spot at 80, the early-exercise right is worth nothing at zero rates, 1.44 at 4.5% — 7.2% of the option's value — and 5.09 at 12%, which is a third of the European price again. This is not a rounding term. A firm marking American puts with a European model in a high-rate regime is marking them wrong by a visible amount.

Now the part that separates this chapter from the last one. The arrow above points one way on purpose. The condition compares exercising now against holding to expiry, but you have a third option — hold for a while and exercise later — and that option is worth something. So passing the test is necessary for exercise to be optimal but not sufficient, and the boundary it produces sits above the true one. Measured against a 900-step tree, the shortcut overshoots by 6.9 to 9.5 points of spot at three to six months, consistently, across every rate tested.

That is a large error and it has a usable shape: the shortcut over-exercises. Use it to rule options out — if the quick test fails, exercise is definitely wrong — and never to rule one in. Ruling in needs the tree, and the figure draws the tree's answer.

Check yourself

Volatility on your name doubles overnight. You are long a deep in-the-money American put that you had been planning to exercise. What happens to the exercise boundary?

It falls, and by a lot. At 4.5% rates with nine months left, the boundary is 87 at 15 volatility and 42 at 60 volatility — the same option, the same rates, a boundary in a completely different place. The term you are giving up is c(S), the upside you still own, and volatility is what makes that upside valuable. The first answer confuses the reason you own the put with the reason you would kill it: more volatility is exactly why you should keep the optionality rather than convert it into cash.
Derivation — the swap, and why the put has no closed form

Exercising a put pays K − S. Holding it, if we pretend for a moment it is European with a dividend D to come, is worth p, and parity with a dividend gives:

c − p = S − De−rtd − Ke−rτ

Reads as: a call minus a put is the stock less the dividends you don't get, financed to expiry. The dividend term is what the call holder forgoes and the put holder is spared.

Rearranging for the put's time value, p − (K − S), gives the mirror of chapter 02.

p − (K − S) = c(S) + De−rtd − K(1 − e−rτ)

Reads as: the put's time value is the upside you keep, plus the dividends you avoid, minus the interest you are not yet earning on the strike. That last term is negative, which is the whole reason early exercise is ever possible.

Exercise beats holding-to-expiry exactly when that time value is negative, which is the condition in the chapter. But the American put's continuation value is not the European price — it is the European price plus the value of the exercise decisions still ahead of you, and that quantity has no closed form. The free boundary and the option value have to be solved for together, which is what the tree does by working backwards from expiry.

Where it breaks. The tree's boundary is only as good as its assumptions, and two matter here. It assumes constant volatility, while the real boundary depends on the volatility you will face after the decision, which nobody knows. And it assumes you can hold the position — a put you are long is your decision, but the boundary is also being used to guess whether the person long the put against your short will exercise, and they may be margin-constrained, taxed differently, or simply not paying attention. Chapter 06 is about the gap between what should be exercised and what is.

05

The screen you run the night before

Two columns, one sort, and a number at the bottom that is usually larger than people expect.

The theory in chapters 02 and 03 becomes a job the afternoon before an ex-date. You have long calls across a chain; some should be exercised by five o'clock and the rest should not; and the cost of getting it wrong in either direction is real money that nobody will ever tell you about, because an unexercised call does not generate a trade confirmation saying what you lost.

The screen is the rule from chapter 03, applied strike by strike, with the put quote taken off the market rather than modelled. Three columns: the dividend on the left, the hurdle in the middle, the difference on the right. Sort by the difference. Everything positive is an exercise notice.

The table below is live. It builds the hurdle from the interest floor plus a put valued on a skewed surface, so the low strikes carry the higher volatilities the way a real chain does. What to watch is where the line falls: at a stock of 112.40 with a $1.42 dividend and 38 days to run, the 100 strike clears by 21 cents and the 105 strike misses by 79 cents. The boundary between "exercise" and "hold" runs between two adjacent strikes, and it moves several strikes if you change the dividend by a few cents.

Which is the practical warning. The rule of thumb people carry — "exercise the ones where the dividend is bigger than the put" — is the right equation with the interest floor deleted from it, and on that chain it moves the line by a full strike. The floor is small in absolute terms and it is decisive precisely because the decision is decided near zero.

The bottom line adds up what is at stake. Across the strikes that clear, at a hundred contracts each, this chain is worth $28,407 to get right, on positions whose total value is a small multiple of that. This is the highest ratio of dollars-to-effort anywhere in options operations, which is why it is a checklist item rather than a judgement call.

Try this

Set the dividend to 1.42 and count the green rows. Now switch the hurdle to the shortcut that ignores interest. Predict how many extra rows go green before you press it.

Check yourself

You run the screen and the 105 strike misses by two cents. The dividend is confirmed, but the stock rallies 3% in the last hour of trading. What happens to that row?

It flips to exercise. The hurdle is interest plus insurance, and only the insurance moves with spot. A rally pushes the put further out of the money, the hurdle drops, and a row that missed by two cents clears. This is why the screen is run late rather than at lunchtime: the dividend side is fixed by announcement but the hurdle side is live until the close, and the marginal strike can change hands in the last twenty minutes.
06

Assignment is a lottery you are already in

The theory says which contracts should be exercised. Which of yours were is drawn at random, and the draw has a standard deviation.

Everything up to here has been written from the long side, where exercise is a decision. From the short side it is not a decision, it is weather. Someone somewhere exercised, and a chain of allocations decides whether it lands on you.

The mechanism has two stages. Exercise notices arrive at the clearing house, which allocates them across the clearing members who are short that contract — historically at random, in proportion to short open interest. Each member then allocates its share across its own customers, by a method it must disclose and apply consistently: usually random, sometimes first-in-first-out. Neither stage looks at you specifically, and neither can be predicted or appealed.

The practical consequence is that your assignment is a binomial draw, not a number. Short ten contracts into an event where 40% of the open interest exercises, and you expect four. You get exactly four about a quarter of the time. You get nothing 0.6% of the time and you get seven or more 5.5% of the time.

Scale it up and the proportional spread narrows but the absolute one does not. Short fifty contracts at the same 40%, and you expect 2,000 shares delivered against you with a standard deviation of 346 shares. The chance of landing within a single contract of your expectation is 33.5% — so two times in three, the hedge you put on the night before is wrong by more than a hundred shares, in a direction you find out about the next morning.

This is the part that turns a clean theory chapter into an operational one. You cannot hedge the assignment, because you learn it after the market has moved. What you can do is size the residual: know the distribution, know the overnight volatility of the name, and know what the tail of that convolution costs. That product is the real risk of being short options into an ex-date, and it is not on any greek report.

P(k of N assigned) = C(N,k) · pk · (1−p)N−k

Reads as: each contract you are short is an independent coin, and p is the fraction of the open interest that exercised. The mean is Np and the standard deviation is √(Np(1−p)) — the second number is the one nobody quotes.

Try this

Set the exercise fraction to 100% — everyone who could exercise did. Predict the standard deviation of your assignment before you press it.

Check yourself

You are short 100 calls into an ex-date and you calculate that exercising is clearly correct for the holders. How should you hedge?

Hedge for the expectation and size for the spread. The correct exercise fraction is never 100% in practice — some holders are asleep, some are constrained, some have tax reasons, and some simply do not run the screen from chapter 05. That is the same fact from the other side: the money left on the table by non-exercisers is money that lands on somebody short, at random. The second answer is the dangerous one, because "it averages out" is true across many ex-dates and irrelevant on this one, which is the only one you are exposed to tonight.
07

Twenty dollars of intrinsic, two thousand of risk

Pin risk is not about the option expiring at the strike. It is about not knowing your stock position until Monday.

An option that finishes a cent in the money is exercised automatically. One that finishes a cent out of the money is not. The clearing house applies the threshold mechanically — exercise by exception, currently at one cent — so the difference between a two-cent-in and a two-cent-out settlement is the difference between being short a thousand shares over the weekend and being flat.

Take the arithmetic literally. You are short ten 100-strike calls and the stock settles at 100.02. Those calls are exercised against you, and you are short a thousand shares from Friday's close. The intrinsic value that changed hands is two cents a share: $20. The overnight risk you have just been handed, on a 35-volatility name, has a standard deviation of $2,205 for one trading day and $3,820 across a three-day weekend.

That ratio — 110 to 1 for a day, 191 to 1 for a weekend — is what pin risk actually is. Not the chance of a strange settlement, but the fact that a rounding-level economic difference determines a position-level exposure, and you learn which one you got after the market has closed.

It gets worse where it should get better. At a settlement of exactly the strike, holders' behaviour is genuinely unpredictable: the option is worth nothing either way, so some file exercise notices and some do not, and your assignment is a draw from chapter 06 with an exercise fraction nobody can estimate. Your Monday position is somewhere between flat and short a thousand shares, and there is no calculation that narrows it.

The defences are all operational rather than clever. Trade out of the position before the close instead of letting it settle — pay the spread and know your position. File a do-not-exercise instruction on longs you do not want, which is the one lever you actually control. Or close the offsetting stock so that either outcome leaves you flat rather than one of them leaving you long and the other short. What you cannot do is model your way out, because the uncertainty is in other people's paperwork.

Try this

Put the settlement exactly on the strike and set the exercise fraction to something you cannot know — say 50%. Predict whether the outcome distribution is one hump or two.

Check yourself

You are long 10 calls struck at 100 and the stock is settling around 100.01. You do not want the shares. What is the cheapest correct action?

File the instruction, or trade out. Exercise by exception means the default is exercise, and the default does not care that the option is worth a cent. The first answer is the expensive one and it is the most commonly held: "worthless" describes the option's value, not its deliverable, and the deliverable is $100,000 of stock. The fourth answer looks prudent and is actively bad — if the stock ticks down a cent in the closing auction you are not assigned, and you are now short a thousand shares you chose deliberately.
08

A ratio change survives; a composition change does not

Splits rescale everything and break nothing. A cash merger leaves the contract intact and every greek in it dead.

Corporate actions adjust option contracts, and the adjustments fall into two categories that behave completely differently. The first kind changes the ratio: how many shares one contract delivers and at what strike. The second changes the composition: what the deliverable is made of. The first is invisible to your risk. The second annihilates it.

Take a two-for-one split. The strike halves, the deliverable stays at 100 shares, and you hold twice as many contracts. Every per-share number changes and every position-level number does not. A 100-strike call on a $120 stock worth 25.2795 becomes two 50-strike calls on a $60 stock worth 12.6398 each. Position value, dollar delta, gamma cash and vega all come through with a residual of exactly zero. Share delta doubles, which is correct — twice as many shares at half the price.

Odd ratios work identically, they just look worse. A three-for-two split leaves you one contract with a 150-share deliverable struck at 66.667, and the position value comes through with a residual of −9 × 10−13. The identity table below computes both sides live; drag anything and the residual column stays at the floor. The reason is that Black–Scholes is homogeneous of degree one in spot and strike — scale both by the same factor and the price scales with them — so a ratio adjustment is a change of units and nothing more.

Now the other kind. A cash merger at $85 replaces the deliverable with $8,500 in cash. The contract survives, the expiry survives, and the option becomes a claim on a fixed amount of money. Volatility no longer enters, because there is nothing left to be volatile.

The consequence is a table people find hard to believe. Announce an all-cash deal at $85 on a stock trading $84 with six months and a 40 volatility, and the stock goes up — while every call on it goes down. The 90-strike call, worth 7.80, becomes worthless: the deliverable will never exceed $85, so the strike will never be reached, regardless of the five and a half months remaining. The at-the-money 85 call goes from 9.85 to zero on the same logic. Even the deep 70-strike falls from 18.48 to 14.67, because its price contained time value and time value requires uncertainty. Vega is zero on every line.

Nothing was mispriced and nothing was adjusted incorrectly. The contracts do exactly what their terms say. The lesson is that greeks describe a deliverable, not a contract, and when the deliverable stops being a random variable the greeks stop existing — which is a different and much more abrupt failure than the ones the rest of this series deals in.

c(λS, λK, τ, σ, r) = λ · c(S, K, τ, σ, r)

Reads as: double the stock and the strike together and the option price doubles. A ratio adjustment picks λ so that value is preserved, which is why it can be done mechanically and why no greek notices.

Check yourself

Your name announces an all-cash acquisition at a large premium. You are short the far out-of-the-money calls. What happened to your risk?

It went to zero — assuming the deal is all cash and your strikes sit above the deal price. This is the one situation in the whole series where a short option position becomes riskless rather than merely cheap. The trap in the first answer is real, though: it is the right answer for a stock-for-stock deal, where the deliverable becomes shares of the acquirer and remains perfectly volatile. "Cash or stock" is the question to ask before anything else, and in an election deal you may not know for weeks.
Derivation — why scaling preserves the greeks, and what the adjustment cannot fix

Black–Scholes depends on spot and strike only through their ratio and through a single multiplicative factor. Scale both by λ and every d term is unchanged, because ln(λS/λK) = ln(S/K).

d1(λS, λK) = d1(S, K)

Reads as: moneyness is a ratio, and a split does not change a ratio. Everything downstream of d₁ and d₂ therefore survives untouched.

The price is a sum of terms each carrying one factor of spot or strike, so it scales linearly. Delta, being a derivative of a linear-homogeneous function, is unchanged; gamma picks up 1/λ per share and the deliverable picks up λ, so gamma cash is preserved; vega scales like price.

Multiply through by the new deliverable and contract count, which the adjustment sets to 1/λ of the old exposure per contract, and every position-level number returns to where it started. That is the whole content of a ratio adjustment: it is a change of units chosen to make λ cancel.

Where it breaks. Three ways, and none of them is arithmetic. Rounding: strikes are published to a fixed number of decimals, so a three-for-two adjustment produces a strike that is not exactly two-thirds, and on a large position the difference is real money. Non-standard deliverables: an adjusted contract may deliver cash plus shares plus a stub of something else, and it no longer nets against the standard contract on the same name — which quietly destroys the spread you thought you had on. And liquidity: adjusted series stop being quoted properly, because market makers price the standard deliverable and the adjusted one is a different instrument wearing a familiar ticker. The greeks survive; the ability to trade out of the position frequently does not.

09

The third reason, and the one nobody writes down

A tender offer makes shares temporarily worth more than their own market price. Options are not shares, and that gap is the only bridge.

Chapter 01 said a call is never exercised early on a non-dividend payer, and chapter 03 gave the one exception. There is a second, and it does not appear in textbooks because it is a rule of securities regulation rather than a result in mathematics.

When a company or a bidder makes a tender offer — an offer to buy shares directly from holders at a stated price — the tendering rules require the person tendering to be net long the shares at the time of tender. This is the short tender rule, and its point is to prevent the same shares being offered up more than once. A long call is not a long share. It is a right to buy one, and rights do not tender.

So a holder of in-the-money calls who wants to participate has exactly one route: exercise, take delivery, and tender the stock — and the deadline for that decision is the tender's expiry, which is typically weeks away and has nothing to do with when the options expire. The result is early exercise driven by neither dividends nor rates.

The economics use the same equation as everything else on this page. What you gain is the tender premium: the deal price less what the stock trades at, which is a gap that exists only because the market prices in the chance the deal fails. What you give up is, as always, the time value.

exercise and tender  ⟺  (tender price − S) > K(1 − e−rτ) + p(S)

Reads as: the same test as the dividend chapter, with the tender premium in the dividend's place. The right-hand side has not changed at all, which is the point — there is only ever one hurdle.

Put numbers on it. A stock was trading 88; an all-cash tender at 100 is announced and closes in three weeks; the stock now trades 98.20, the discount reflecting deal risk and the wait. You hold 70-strike calls with four months left. Implied volatility has collapsed toward 9, because the stock is now a bet on a binary event rather than a business.

The hurdle is the time value, which at those parameters is 1.028 per share — and note that it is almost entirely the interest floor, since the put on a deal stock at a 30-point premium is worth essentially nothing. The gain is 1.80, the gap between the tender price and the market. Exercise and tender wins by 77 cents a share, which on a hundred contracts is $7,700 for filing paperwork on time.

The general shape is worth carrying: deal stocks have crushed volatility, and crushed volatility means the insurance half of the hurdle disappears, which leaves the interest floor as almost the entire cost of exercising. That is the smallest the hurdle ever gets, and it is why tender situations produce early exercise so reliably.

Two warnings, both important. The mechanism above is the durable part; the specifics are not. Tender rules, the treatment of options in a tender, proration in partial offers, and what happens to unexercised contracts after a deal closes are all governed by the offer document and the prevailing rulebook, and they change. And in a partial tender only a fraction of the shares you tender are accepted, so the gain must be multiplied by an expected proration factor you will not know until after the deadline — which turns a clean inequality into an estimate. Read the offer, not the formula.

Check yourself

Why can't you simply tender against your long calls and exercise afterwards, once you know how many shares were accepted?

The net-long requirement. A call gives you a claim on shares, not shares, and tendering what you do not own is exactly what the rule exists to prevent. This is why the decision has to be made before the tender deadline and therefore before you know the proration outcome — you convert first and find out second, which is what makes the arithmetic an expected value rather than a certainty. The fourth answer invents a tax rule; the real constraint is a securities one, and it is about the sequence rather than the treatment.

Question 1 · chapter 01

Rates are exactly zero and the stock pays no dividend. Is an American call worth more than a European one?

Equal. The bound c ≥ S − Ke−rτ becomes c ≥ S − K, so exercising captures exactly the bound rather than falling short of it. There is still no gain, so the extra right is still worth nothing. The last answer is a factual error worth naming: listed equity options are American, and that is exactly why this page exists.
Question 2 · chapter 02

What are the two components of a call's time value?

Interest plus insurance. It comes straight out of parity, holds to 4 × 10−14, and it is what makes the exercise decision a subtraction you can do with a put quote. The last answer is the common muddle: extrinsic value is time value, so that option says time value equals time value plus intrinsic.
Question 3 · chapter 03

A stock pays a 30-cent dividend. Rates are 4.5%, the strike is 100, and there are 90 days from the ex-date to expiry. At what spot does exercising become correct?

No spot at all. This is the filter that clears most of a screen in seconds: compare the dividend with K(1−e−rτ) before doing anything else, because the put can only add to the hurdle. Chapter 03's figure shows the boundary curve ceasing to exist rather than running off the top of the chart, and this is that.
Question 4 · chapter 04

What drives early exercise of an American put on a non-dividend payer?

Rates. At 4.5% the early-exercise right on a one-year 100-strike put with spot at 80 is worth 1.44; at 12% it is 5.09, a third of the European price. At zero it is worth nothing. Volatility works in the opposite direction to the first answer — it raises the call you give up, which pushes the boundary down.
Question 5 · chapter 04

The one-line test says exercise your put below 85. A binomial tree says 78. Which do you follow?

The tree. The shortcut compares exercising now against holding to expiry, which is not your only alternative — you can also hold and exercise later, and that right is worth something. So the test is necessary but not sufficient, and the boundary it draws sits above the true one by 6.9 to 9.5 points across every rate tested. Use it to rule out, never to rule in.
Question 6 · chapter 06

You are short 50 contracts and 40% of the open interest exercises. How confident should you be about your resulting share position?

2,000 ± 346 shares. Allocation is random across the short open interest, so your assignment is a binomial draw rather than a proportion. The mean is the number you hedge to; the standard deviation is the risk you carry overnight, and it is the number that never appears on a greek report.
Question 7 · chapter 07

Short 10 calls struck at 100, stock settles at 100.02, name trades on a 35 volatility. Roughly what have you taken on over a three-day weekend?

Roughly $3,800 against $20. A ratio of 191 to 1. Pin risk is not the risk of a strange settlement — it is that a rounding-level economic difference hands you a position-level exposure that you learn about after the close. The third answer is the one that costs people money: expiry closes the option, not the stock position it delivers.
Question 8 · chapter 08

An all-cash acquisition at $85 is announced on a stock trading $84. What happens to the six-month 90-strike calls?

Zero. The stock goes up and every call on it goes down, including the deep ones, because their prices contained time value and time value requires uncertainty. Nothing was mispriced — greeks describe a deliverable, and this deliverable stopped being a random variable. Note that the answer flips entirely for a stock-for-stock deal, where the deliverable becomes acquirer shares and stays volatile.

Two kinds of statement appear on this page and they should not be trusted equally. The mathematics — the zero American premium, the time-value identity, the exact ex-date criterion, the scaling result — is checked numerically and holds to the precision of the arithmetic. The market plumbing is convention: exercise-by-exception thresholds, random allocation, contract adjustment policy, and tender rules are set by clearing houses, exchanges and regulators, they differ between products and jurisdictions, and they change. Everything here is dated to the time of writing and stated as mechanism rather than rulebook. Before acting on the operational chapters, check the current contract specifications and the offer document. The exercise fractions and price-impact assumptions in the simulations are illustrative parameters on sliders, not measurements.
