The same trade, read twice

A pension buys a payoff. A volatility fund buys an exposure. They can be on opposite sides of one ticket, describing it in vocabularies that share no words — and both be right.

01

Two readings of one ticket

The payoff diagram and the greek profile describe the same object and answer different questions. Most confusion between desks is a vocabulary mismatch.

Ask a corporate treasurer why they put on a collar and you will hear about a board mandate, a budget of zero premium, and a worst case they can live with. Ask a volatility fund why they took the other side and you will hear that they were short skew at a level they liked and needed the vanna. Neither is being evasive. They are describing the same trade along different axes.

The institutional reading asks: what is my worst case, what does it cost, how does it book, and does it fit the mandate. The payoff diagram answers all four, which is why it is the diagram that ends up in the pitch.

The volatility reading asks: what am I long and short on the surface, what does it cost to carry, what happens when vol moves, and what will it take to hedge. The payoff diagram answers none of those, because it only describes expiry — and a vol fund rarely holds anything to expiry.

This page draws both, always together. Pick a structure below and you get the shape on the left and the exposure on the right. The claim the page is building toward is that the second view is the one that reveals when two differently-named trades are the same trade.

02

The fingerprint

Five bars. Every structure on this page reduces to them, and structures that share a fingerprint are the same bet wearing different names.

A payoff diagram has one axis and cannot show what a position is long. Reduce a structure instead to what it holds along the five dimensions a surface actually has, and comparison becomes trivial.

Direction is delta. Realized is gamma — does this profit from movement happening. Vol level is vega — does it profit from movement being repriced. Skew is vanna, the axis that page 02's smile acts along. Vol convexity is volga, whether your vega grows as vol grows.

Each bar below is normalized against the same greek on a one-lot at-the-money straddle, so the units are comparable across structures and across strikes. A straddle is by construction 1.0 on realized and 1.0 on vol level, which makes it a natural yardstick.

Two habits worth building from this figure. First, read the sign before the size — most structures are obvious once you know whether they are long or short each axis. Second, notice what is near zero. A butterfly is barely directional and barely skewed; almost all of it is a short-realized, short-vol bet concentrated in one place. That is a much more useful description than "long one, short two, long one."

03

The same trade, from both sides

A corporate's zero-cost collar and a volatility fund's risk reversal are one instrument with the sign flipped. Not similar. Identical, to every decimal.

A concentrated shareholder wants downside protection and cannot pay for it, so they buy a put and sell a call to fund it. On the pitch it is a collar: a floor, a cap, zero premium, and a worst case the board can approve.

A volatility fund sees the other side and calls it a risk reversal: long the call, short the put, which on an equity surface means short the expensive downside and long the cheap upside. They are not thinking about floors. They are thinking that skew is too steep and they want to be paid to own that view.

Put both through the fingerprint and the numbers are exactly negatives of each other — delta, gamma, vega, theta, vanna and volga all sum to zero to machine precision. There is no sense in which these are two trades. There is one trade, and two descriptions of it that share almost no vocabulary.

Why this matters beyond being tidy: it tells you where the price of a collar comes from. The treasurer thinks they are buying insurance and paying for it with an upside cap. What they are actually doing is selling skew, and the level they receive is set by how steep the surface is that day. A collar that is zero-cost in a high-skew environment requires giving up far less upside than the same collar when skew is flat — a fact that is invisible in the payoff diagram and obvious in the fingerprint.

Check yourself

A treasurer says their zero-cost collar "cost nothing." What did it actually cost?

They sold skew, and the price was upside. Zero premium is an accounting statement, not an economic one — the put was paid for with a call, and the exchange rate between them is the skew. In a steep-skew environment the downside put is expensive relative to the upside call, so funding it requires selling a call further out and you keep more upside. The identical structure in a flat-skew name forces the cap much closer. This is exactly why the fund on the other side describes the trade as a skew position: that is the variable being traded, even though nobody in the meeting said the word.

05

The lizards, and the condition nobody states

A jade lizard is not a set of legs. It is a set of legs plus an inequality, and the inequality fails more often than the name suggests.

The lizard family is usually introduced as leg recipes. A jade lizard is a short put plus a short call spread. A big lizard is a short straddle plus a long call. A twisted sister is the jade lizard's mirror — a short call plus a short put spread.

That description omits the entire point. What makes a jade lizard interesting is that it can have no upside risk at all, and it has that property only when:

total credit received ≥ width of the call spread

Reads as: you must be paid at least as much as the most the call spread can ever lose. If so, the worst upside outcome is a wash and everything above the short call is flat.

Whether that holds depends entirely on strikes and volatility, and it is easy to get wrong. A short 92 put against a 105/110 call spread collects 2.68 against a width of 5 — that is not a jade lizard, it is just a short strangle with a capped call side and real upside risk. Tighten to a 98 put against a 102/104 spread and the credit is 3.80 against a width of 2, and the property holds at every volatility level from 25 to 70.

The general pattern: narrow spreads and higher volatility make the condition hold; wide spreads break it. Which is worth internalizing because it runs against instinct — the wider call spread feels safer, and it is precisely the version that reintroduces the risk the structure exists to remove.

The figure checks the condition live. Move the strikes and the verdict flips, and the payoff's right-hand tail lifts off the flat line the moment the inequality fails.

Check yourself

You widen a jade lizard's call spread from 102/104 to 102/112, keeping the same short put. What happens to the upside risk?

Widening breaks it. Going from 2 wide to 10 wide adds eight points of potential loss and only a fraction of a point of credit, because the extra long call you are no longer buying was far out of the money and nearly worthless. The condition credit ≥ width fails and the upside tail reappears, capped at width minus credit. Not unlimited — the long call still caps it — but no longer zero, which is the property you were paying for. The instinct that wider is safer is exactly backwards here.
06

Where the surface comes from

Page 02 showed the surface is skewed. This chapter is the argument that structural flow is a large part of why.

Chapter 04's two shelves are not symmetric. Run down the institutional list and nearly every entry does the same two things: sells upside volatility and buys downside volatility. Overwriting programmes sell calls, month after month, mechanically. Pension and insurance mandates buy puts. Collars do both at once. Structured note issuance leaves dealers with exposures they must hedge in the same direction.

None of these are opportunistic. They are mandates, and they recur regardless of where implied volatility is trading — an overwriting programme sells calls in January because it is January, not because calls look rich.

Persistent one-way flow has to be absorbed by somebody, and the price of absorbing it is the surface's shape. Downside strikes bid, upside strikes offered, is exactly what a persistent buyer of puts and seller of calls produces in a market where the other side must be paid to warehouse the risk.

How much of the skew this explains is genuinely contested, and it would be dishonest to present flow as the whole story. Page 02 showed that jumps and correlated stochastic volatility each generate skew from pure modelling assumptions with no flow at all, and index skew persisted through decades with very different flow regimes. The honest position is that skew has several sufficient causes and the observed surface is their sum, with the mix varying by underlying and by tenor.

What is not contested is the trading implication, and it is the one worth taking from this page: when you buy an option you are usually buying it from somebody whose reason has nothing to do with your reason. The overwriter selling you a call has no view on volatility. The pension buying puts is not forecasting a crash. Treating every price as though it encodes a view is a good way to find edge that is not there — and treating persistent flow as though it were information is a good way to be on the wrong side of a mandate that will still be running next quarter.

Every structure here is priced on a flat surface at a single volatility, which understates the case: on a real skewed surface the put legs of these structures price off higher implied vols than the call legs, and several of the credit conditions in chapter 05 become easier to satisfy as a result. Structures are also shown at one expiry, so calendars and diagonals — which are term-structure trades — get a mention and not a fingerprint. The flow argument in chapter 06 is a mechanism, not a measurement.
