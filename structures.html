The same trade, read twice

A pension buys a payoff. A volatility fund buys an exposure. They can be on opposite sides of one ticket, describing it in vocabularies that share no words — and both be right.

01

Two readings of one ticket

The payoff diagram and the greek profile describe the same object and answer different questions. Most confusion between desks is a vocabulary mismatch.

Ask a corporate treasurer why they put on a collar and you will hear about a board mandate, a budget of zero premium, and a worst case they can live with. Ask a volatility fund why they took the other side and you will hear that they were short skew at a level they liked and needed the vanna. Neither is being evasive. They are describing the same trade along different axes.

The institutional reading asks: what is my worst case, what does it cost, how does it book, and does it fit the mandate. The payoff diagram answers all four, which is why it is the diagram that ends up in the pitch.

The volatility reading asks: what am I long and short on the surface, what does it cost to carry, what happens when vol moves, and what will it take to hedge. The payoff diagram answers none of those, because it only describes expiry — and a vol fund rarely holds anything to expiry.

This page draws both, always together. Pick a structure below and you get the shape on the left and the exposure on the right. The claim the page is building toward is that the second view is the one that reveals when two differently-named trades are the same trade.

Read the two panels against each other and one rule does most of the work: a peak in the payoff is short gamma, a valley is long gamma. Curvature is gamma, and a peak curves down. The butterfly's tent at 100 reads −0.0080 of gamma at the money even though it is a long position bought for a debit — you paid for the wings, but the body you sold is closer and dominates. The straddle's valley reads +0.0754. Who paid whom does not tell you the sign; the shape does.

The second rule is that time changes the volatility view far more than the payoff view. The grey payoff line never moves when you drag the expiry, because it is the payoff at expiry. The greek panel moves completely: shorten a butterfly to three days and its short gamma concentrates into a spike around the body; push it out to a year and it all but vanishes. Same ticket, very different risk, depending only on the calendar.

greek(structure) = Σ qᵢ · greek(legᵢ)

Reads as: every greek of a structure is the quantity-weighted sum of its legs' greeks. Price is linear in the legs, and the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives — which is why the fingerprint in the next chapter is just the legs' fingerprints added up.

Check yourself

You buy a 95/100/105 call butterfly for a debit. At the money, what sign is its gamma?

Negative, about −0.008 here. Paying a debit tells you about cash, not curvature. The butterfly is short gamma in the body and long it in the wings — page 19 finds the spot where it changes sign — which is why it behaves like a short straddle with insurance bolted on.
Derivation — why greeks add across legs, and when the sum misleads

A structure's value is the sum of its legs' values, each scaled by its quantity.

V = Σ qᵢ · Vᵢ(S, σ, t)

Reads as: the package is worth what its pieces are worth. No interaction terms, because each option pays independently of the others.

Differentiate with respect to anything and the sum passes straight through.

∂V/∂x = Σ qᵢ · ∂Vᵢ/∂x

Reads as: the sensitivity of the whole is the sum of the sensitivities of the parts.

Where it breaks. The algebra never breaks; the interpretation does. Summing vega across legs assumes every leg's volatility moves by the same amount, and on a skewed surface or across expiries they do not — page 11 shows a book whose net vega of 417 produces anything from $743 to $1,238 on moves that all look like two points.

02

The fingerprint

Five bars. Every structure on this page reduces to them, and structures that share a fingerprint are the same bet wearing different names.

A payoff diagram has one axis and cannot show what a position is long. Reduce a structure instead to what it holds along the five dimensions a surface actually has, and comparison becomes trivial.

Direction is delta. Realized is gamma — does this profit from movement happening. Vol level is vega — does it profit from movement being repriced. Skew is vanna, the axis that page 02's smile acts along. Vol convexity is volga, whether your vega grows as vol grows.

Each bar below is normalized against the same greek on a one-lot at-the-money straddle, so the units are comparable across structures and across strikes. A straddle is by construction 1.0 on realized and 1.0 on vol level, which makes it a natural yardstick.

Two habits worth building from this figure. First, read the sign before the size — most structures are obvious once you know whether they are long or short each axis. Second, notice what is near zero. A butterfly is barely directional and barely skewed; almost all of it is a short-realized, short-vol bet concentrated in one place. That is a much more useful description than "long one, short two, long one."

Look at two of the bars and they always agree. Realized and Vol level read the same number for every structure on this page — the straddle +1.00 on both, the iron condor −0.53 on both, the butterfly −0.11 on both. That is not a coincidence of the examples. At a single expiry vega is gamma times S²σT, the same constant for every leg, so once both are divided by the straddle's they cannot differ. A single-expiry structure can never be long movement and short its price; page 19 proves it and shows the calendar that breaks the tie.

So the honest count is four axes, not five, and the interesting differences live in the last two. A strangle carries 0.64 of a straddle's gamma and vega plus a convexity bar of +0.47 that the straddle does not have at all. A risk reversal reads +0.35 direction and +0.46 skew with almost nothing elsewhere: a skew position with a tilt. A call spread reads +0.33 direction like the risk reversal, but −0.25 skew — the same directional bet with the opposite exposure to the smile.

One caveat about scale. Direction, Realized and Vol level are measured against the straddle directly. Skew and Vol convexity are vanna and volga against a straddle-sized yardstick scaled for display, so read their signs and their sizes relative to each other, not as numbers to put in a risk limit.

bar = greek(structure) / |greek(one-lot ATM straddle)|

Reads as: each axis is how much of that exposure you hold, measured in straddles. A −0.53 on Realized means the structure is short about half a straddle's worth of gamma.

03

The same trade, from both sides

A corporate's zero-cost collar and a volatility fund's risk reversal are one instrument with the sign flipped. Not similar. Identical, to every decimal.

A concentrated shareholder wants downside protection and cannot pay for it, so they buy a put and sell a call to fund it. On the pitch it is a collar: a floor, a cap, zero premium, and a worst case the board can approve.

A volatility fund sees the other side and calls it a risk reversal: long the call, short the put, which on an equity surface means short the expensive downside and long the cheap upside. They are not thinking about floors. They are thinking that skew is too steep and they want to be paid to own that view.

Put both through the fingerprint and the numbers are exactly negatives of each other — delta, gamma, vega, theta, vanna and volga all sum to zero to machine precision. There is no sense in which these are two trades. There is one trade, and two descriptions of it that share almost no vocabulary.

Why this matters beyond being tidy: it tells you where the price of a collar comes from. The treasurer thinks they are buying insurance and paying for it with an upside cap. What they are actually doing is buying skew — paying for the expensive downside with the cheap upside — and the exchange rate is set by how steep the surface is that day. A collar that is zero-cost in a high-skew environment requires giving up more upside than the same collar when skew is flat: on a 90 put at 30 vol and 45 days, the zero-cost call strike falls from 113 on a flat surface to 106.5 on a steep one. That is invisible in the payoff diagram and obvious in the fingerprint.

Derivation — why a collar and a risk reversal annihilate exactly

Write both positions in terms of the same three instruments and the result is arithmetic rather than insight.

collar = +stock − call(Kc) + put(Kp)

Reads as: own the shares, sell the upside, buy the downside. The corporate's description, in the corporate's units.

risk reversal = +call(Kc) − put(Kp)

Reads as: buy the upside, sell the downside. The fund's description, with no stock in it at all.

Add them and every option term cancels, leaving the stock.

collar + risk reversal = +stock

Reads as: the two structures are the same instrument with the sign flipped, plus a share. So every option greek — delta contribution, gamma, vega, vanna, volga, charm, theta — must sum to zero between them, because stock has none of them.

That is exactly what the figure shows: the largest absolute sum across all seven greeks is 0.00e+0. It is not an approximation that holds near the money, and it does not decay with time or volatility. It is an identity.

Where it breaks. Nowhere in the mathematics, and everywhere in the institution. The two sides face different margin treatment, different accounting, different tax and different mandates, so the identical exposure is not identically ownable. A corporate treasurer who is permitted to hold a collar against a shareholding is frequently not permitted to hold the risk reversal alone, even though the difference is one line of stock — and that regulatory asymmetry, rather than any disagreement about value, is a large part of why the flow is one-directional and why the surface ends up shaped the way chapter 06 describes.

Check yourself

A treasurer says their zero-cost collar "cost nothing." What did it actually cost?

They bought skew, and the price was upside. Zero premium is an accounting statement, not an economic one — the put was paid for with a call, and the exchange rate between them is the skew. In a steep-skew environment the downside put is expensive relative to the upside call, so funding it requires selling a call closer to the money and you keep less upside: the zero-cost call for a 90 put moves from 113 on a flat surface to 106.5 on a steep one. The identical structure in a flat-skew name leaves the cap much further out. This is exactly why the fund on the other side describes the trade as a skew position: that is the variable being traded, even though nobody in the meeting said the word.
04

The two shelves

What each side reaches for, and what the other side sees when they do.

Sorted by who typically initiates them, with the translation attached. Load any of them into the figure above to see both views.

One structural asymmetry runs down both lists. The institutional shelf is dominated by selling upside and buying downside — overwriting programs, collars, protective puts. The volatility shelf is where the other side of that accumulates. That imbalance is not a coincidence and it is chapter 06's subject.

The translation column is the useful one, and the fingerprint puts numbers on it. The collar and the risk reversal are the cleanest pair: −0.35 against +0.35 on direction and −0.46 against +0.46 on skew, every axis an exact mirror — chapter 03's identity read off the bars. The iron condor and the butterfly look like relatives on the payoff diagram, but per lot at these strikes the condor carries about five times the butterfly's short gamma and vega, −0.53 against −0.11: its short strikes sit near the money with nothing close by to offset them, while the butterfly's long wings sit right beside its body. The strangle is not a cheap straddle: it is 0.64 of a straddle plus a convexity bet the straddle does not make.

Two habits follow. When someone on the institutional shelf describes a trade, translate it into the axes before deciding whether you want the other side. And when two structures look interchangeable on the volatility shelf, compare their skew and convexity bars — that is where they stop being the same trade. Check yourself

A pension runs a monthly overwriting programme — selling calls against stock it holds. In greek terms, what is the dealer on the other side buying?

Upside volatility, sold on a schedule. Long stock and short calls is exactly a short put (page 19), so what changes hands is gamma and vega. The fourth answer confuses the delta the stock covers with the volatility exposure the stock cannot touch. And because the programme sells regardless of price, it is a persistent supply of upside vol — chapter 06's reason upside strikes are offered.
05

The lizards, and the condition nobody states

A jade lizard is not a set of legs. It is a set of legs plus an inequality, and the inequality fails more often than the name suggests.

The lizard family is usually introduced as leg recipes. A jade lizard is a short put plus a short call spread. A big lizard is a short straddle plus a long call. A twisted sister is the jade lizard's mirror — a short call plus a short put spread.

That description omits the entire point. What makes a jade lizard interesting is that it can have no upside risk at all, and it has that property only when:

total credit received ≥ width of the call spread

Reads as: you must be paid at least as much as the most the call spread can ever lose. If so, the worst upside outcome is a wash and everything above the short call is flat.

Whether that holds depends entirely on strikes and volatility, and it is easy to get wrong. A short 92 put against a 105/110 call spread collects 2.36 against a width of 5 — that is not a jade lizard, it is just a short strangle with a capped call side and real upside risk. Tighten to a 98 put against a 102/104 spread and the credit is 3.80 against a width of 2, and the property holds at every volatility level from 25 to 70.

The general pattern: narrow spreads and higher volatility make the condition hold; wide spreads break it. Which is worth internalizing because it runs against instinct — the wider call spread feels safer, and it is precisely the version that reintroduces the risk the structure exists to remove.

The figure checks the condition live. Move the strikes and the verdict flips, and the payoff's right-hand tail lifts off the flat line the moment the inequality fails.

Check yourself

You widen a jade lizard's call spread from 102/104 to 102/112, keeping the same short put. What happens to the upside risk?

Widening breaks it. Going from 2 wide to 10 wide adds eight points of potential loss and only 1.88 of credit — 3.80 becomes 5.67 — because moving the long call from 104 to 112 saves you the difference between two out-of-the-money calls, which is far less than the eight points of width it adds. The condition credit ≥ width fails and the upside tail reappears, capped at width minus credit. Not unlimited — the long call still caps it — but no longer zero, which is the property you were paying for. The instinct that wider is safer is exactly backwards here.

The same test applies to the whole family, each with its own width. A big lizard — short the 100 straddle, long the 105 call — collects 5.97 against a width of 5, so it holds by 97 cents. A twisted sister — short the 105 call, short the 95 put, long the 92 put — collects 3.21 against a width of 3 on the downside, and holds by only 21 cents. One shrug of implied vol and it does not.

Notice where the risk went. A jade lizard that holds has no upside risk, but it has not become safe; it has moved all of its risk to the other side. It is a short put with a call spread sold on top, so a fall to 85 at expiry costs 98 − 85 − 3.80 = 9.20. The name describes the side that was made safe and stays quiet about the side that was not.

And the flat surface on this page understates how often the condition holds. On a real equity skew the short put is priced off a higher implied vol than the calls, which raises the credit. With a skew of −0.8 vol per unit of log-moneyness, the 98/102/104 lizard collects 4.21 instead of 3.80, and the 92/105/110 version collects 3.21 instead of 2.36 — closer, but still short of its width of 5. Skew helps lizards; it does not rescue a spread that is too wide.

Derivation — where the condition comes from, and what it does not protect

Take a jade lizard: short a put at K₁, short a call at K₂, long a call at K₃ above it, for a total credit c. Above K₃ every leg's payoff is fixed: the put is worthless and the call spread owes its full width.

P&L above K₃ = c − (K₃ − K₂)

Reads as: keep the credit, pay the full width of the call spread. That is the worst the upside can ever do, because nothing above K₃ changes.

Between K₂ and K₃ the loss on the call spread is smaller than the width, so the P&L is higher than at K₃. The worst upside outcome is therefore the one above K₃, and it is non-negative exactly when c ≥ K₃ − K₂. The big lizard and twisted sister are the same argument with the width measured from the straddle strike and on the put side.

Where it breaks. The condition is a statement about expiry. Before then the short call can be assigned early — into a dividend, say, from page 08 — which removes one leg and leaves the others. A settlement right at a short strike brings page 08's pin risk. Margin is charged on the position, not on the inequality, so a lizard that has no upside risk on paper can still tie up capital against it. And the condition says nothing at all about the downside, which is where the position's real risk lives.

06

Where the surface comes from

Page 02 showed the surface is skewed. This chapter is the argument that structural flow is a large part of why.

Chapter 04's two shelves are not symmetric. Run down the institutional list and nearly every entry does the same two things: sells upside volatility and buys downside volatility. Overwriting programs sell calls, month after month, mechanically. Pension and insurance mandates buy puts. Collars do both at once. Structured note issuance leaves dealers with exposures they must hedge in the same direction.

None of these are opportunistic. They are mandates, and they recur regardless of where implied volatility is trading — an overwriting program sells calls in January because it is January, not because calls look rich.

Persistent one-way flow has to be absorbed by somebody, and the price of absorbing it is the surface's shape. Downside strikes bid, upside strikes offered, is exactly what a persistent buyer of puts and seller of calls produces in a market where the other side must be paid to warehouse the risk.

How much of the skew this explains is genuinely contested, and it would be dishonest to present flow as the whole story. Page 02 showed that jumps and correlated stochastic volatility each generate skew from pure modeling assumptions with no flow at all, and index skew persisted through decades with very different flow regimes. The honest position is that skew has several sufficient causes and the observed surface is their sum, with the mix varying by underlying and by tenor.

What is not contested is the trading implication, and it is the one worth taking from this page: when you buy an option you are usually buying it from somebody whose reason has nothing to do with your reason. The overwriter selling you a call has no view on volatility. The pension buying puts is not forecasting a crash. Treating every price as though it encodes a view is a good way to find edge that is not there — and treating persistent flow as though it were information is a good way to be on the wrong side of a mandate that will still be running next quarter.

Try this

Turn overwriting off entirely and leave put buying alone. Predict which side of the surface flattens.

The figure puts numbers on the mechanism, with the caveat that the mapping from flow to vol is an assumption on a slider. With both flows at their default size and an impact of 0.35, the 90 strike trades at 31.39% and the 110 at 24.52%: a 90–110 skew of 6.87 points on a surface that started flat at 28. Switch overwriting off and the skew falls to 3.40; switch put buying off instead and it falls to 3.47. Each flow supplies about half the slope, from opposite ends — the put buyers lift the left wing and the overwriters press down the right.

That split matters for trading, because the two flows move on different calendars. Overwriting runs monthly and steadily; put buying spikes when portfolio managers get nervous. A surface whose slope comes mostly from overwriting is held down at the upside and tends to stay there; one whose slope comes from put demand can steepen sharply and relax just as fast. Same skew number, different behavior.

σ(K) ≈ σ₀ + κ · [ demand(K) − supply(K) ]

Reads as: each strike's implied vol is the no-flow level, pushed up by persistent demand and down by persistent supply, in proportion to how much the market charges to absorb an imbalance. κ is that charge, and it is the one number here nobody can measure directly.

Check yourself

A large wave of new overwriting programmes launches and keeps selling calls every month. All else equal, which part of the surface moves?

The upside wing comes down. The third answer is right that overwriters have no view and wrong about the consequence: flow with no information still has to be warehoused by somebody who charges for it, and the charge is the price. That is the whole point of this chapter — and the reason "the market is telling us upside vol is cheap" is often only telling you that a mandate is running.
Derivation — why an imbalance with no information still becomes a price

A dealer who absorbs a one-way flow ends up holding inventory it did not choose. Page 05's market-making chapter shows what it does about that: it skews its quotes away from the side it already owns.

quote mid = fair value − k · inventory

Reads as: the price shown is the dealer's estimate of value, shifted by how much it already holds. Long calls from overwriters, it marks calls down to discourage more selling and encourage buying.

When the flow is persistent the inventory never clears, so the shift never goes away. Across many strikes and many dealers, that permanent shift is the slope of the surface — the overwriting term at the upside, the put-buying term at the downside.

Where it breaks. Flow is not the only generator of skew. Page 02 showed jumps and correlated stochastic volatility each produce one from pure modeling assumptions, and index skew persisted through very different flow regimes. Positioning is inferred rather than observed, so the size of κ in any real market is a matter of estimation and argument. Read the figure as which way each flow pushes, not as a decomposition of any real surface.

07

The field guide: every common structure, and what it takes to win with it

Why each one is traded, who trades it, how it loses, and the conditions under which it actually makes money. No structure has an edge on its own.

Every structure below is a way of buying or selling some mix of direction, movement, the price of movement, skew and time. None of them is good or bad. A structure makes money when the market has mispriced the thing it is long or short, and loses when it has not — so the question is never "is this a good strategy" but "what is this structure betting on, and is that thing mispriced right now?"

There are only five honest sources of edge in options, and every "how to win" below is one of them. Price: implied volatility is wrong relative to what will be realized — the variance risk premium, measured with the vol cone in the toolkit. Shape: one part of the surface is rich or cheap relative to another — skew and term structure, page 02. Events: a catalyst is priced wrong, page 03. Flow: you are paid for warehousing someone else's mandated trade, chapter 06. Execution and sizing: you pay less to trade and you size so a bad outcome does not end the game, page 06 and the toolkit's Kelly tool. A structure is the vehicle; the edge has to come from one of those five.

The live numbers on each card use chapter 01's market — change spot, vol or expiry there and every card reprices. Filter by what you are trying to do.

expected P&L = Σ probability(state) × payoff(state) − price paid

Reads as: a structure only makes money if your distribution of outcomes is better than the one the market charged you for. Win rate, credit received and "defined risk" all leave this equation unchanged; they describe the shape of the bet, not its value.

Check yourself

An iron condor seller wins 85% of the time. What does that tell you about the strategy's edge?

Nothing by itself. Win rate is a property of the payoff shape, not of the price. Page 18 shows a seller's hit rate rising as tails fatten — exactly when the strategy is most dangerous. The fourth answer over-corrects: short options earn the variance risk premium on average, which is why so many institutions run them, but the premium comes with a tail you must size for.

Question 1 · chapter 01

An institution and a vol fund describe the same ticket completely differently. Who is right?

Both, and that is the page's whole point. The two readings are complete and non-overlapping — which is why the same structure can be simultaneously a hedge and a carry trade, and why the flow between them is so persistent.
Question 2 · chapter 03

What is a collar plus a risk reversal on the same strikes?

A share of stock. Since stock has no gamma, vega, vanna, volga or charm, every one of those must net to exactly zero between the two structures — measured at 0.00e+0 in the figure, not approximately.
Question 3 · chapter 05

When does a jade lizard actually have no upside risk?

Only when the credit covers the width. This is the condition the name usually omits: at 98 put with a 102–104 spread the credit is 3.796 against a width of 2 and it holds at every volatility from 25% to 70%. At 92 put with a 105–110 spread the credit is 2.357 against a width of 5 and it does not — that position is a short strangle with a lid.
Question 4 · chapter 05

Which change makes a lizard more likely to hold?

Narrow it, and hope for high volatility. Widening the spread raises the credit but raises the width faster, so the condition fails. This is the finding that became a whole chapter, because the instinct runs the wrong way.
Question 5 · chapter 06

Why does persistent institutional flow show up in the surface?

Someone has to warehouse the imbalance. This is the same mechanism page 10 uses to explain persistently rich implied correlation, and the same one page 05 uses for the spread: durable one-way flow becomes a price.
Question 6 · chapter 02

What does the exposure fingerprint normalize against?

A one-lot at-the-money straddle. Without a common denominator the axes are in incomparable units and the chart is decorative; with one, a collar and a risk reversal can be shown to be mirror images at a glance.

Every structure here is priced on a flat surface at a single volatility, which understates the case: on a real skewed surface the put legs of these structures price off higher implied vols than the call legs, and several of the credit conditions in chapter 05 become easier to satisfy as a result. Structures are also shown at one expiry, so calendars and diagonals — which are term-structure trades — get a mention and not a fingerprint. The flow argument in chapter 06 is a mechanism, not a measurement.
