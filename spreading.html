Nobody trades one option

Five structures, all bullish, all sized to exactly the same delta. Which one makes the most money changes hands four times as the stock moves — and there is no version of "the right one" that survives contact with the outcome.

Page 04 catalogued structures and showed what each one is. This page is about choosing between them — the question a trader actually faces, which is not "what does a call spread look like" but "I am bullish; now what do I put on".

01

An outright is three opinions in a trench coat

Buy a call and you have bet on direction, on volatility and on time. You probably only meant one of them.

Suppose you think a 100 stock is going to 110 over the next three months. The obvious trade is to buy a call. The obvious trade also commits you to two other views you never expressed.

Buy the 105 call at 3.9341. You are now long 0.194 of vega per point — so if implied volatility drops five points overnight with the stock unchanged, the option is worth 2.9419 and you have lost 25.2% of your money having been exactly right about nothing happening yet.

Now sell the 115 call against it. The package costs 2.5151 and carries 0.0568 of vega — 71% less. The same five-point volatility drop costs 13.9% instead of 25.2%. You have kept most of the directional exposure and thrown away most of the exposure you did not want.

That is the entire argument for spreading, and it generalises. Every additional leg is an opportunity to cancel a sensitivity you are not being paid to hold. A calendar cancels direction and keeps term structure. A butterfly cancels direction and most of the vega and keeps the distribution's shape. A risk reversal cancels most of the volatility level and keeps skew.

Two things you give up, and they are real. The spread caps the upside — above 115 the short call gives back everything the long call earns. And it doubles the transaction costs, which page 05 priced: two spreads crossed instead of one, on options whose spreads are wide in volatility terms.

So spreading is not free and it is not automatically better. It is a way of paying, in upside and in commission, to stop being exposed to things you have no view on. Whether that is worth it is the subject of the rest of the page.

Check yourself

You are bullish and buy a call. The stock does nothing for a week and you lose 12%. What happened?

Theta and vega, and it is usually the vega that surprises people. A five-point volatility drop costs 25.2% on this outright and a week of theta costs a fraction of that. The whole reason to spread is that you can keep the view you have and stop paying for the ones you do not.
02

The vertical, and the credit-spread illusion

A bull call spread and a bull put spread on the same strikes are the same trade. The difference is eleven cents of interest.

The vertical is the basic directional spread: two options, same type, same expiry, different strikes. Buy the 100 call and sell the 110 call and you have paid 3.6607 for a position that can make at most 6.3393, loses at most what you paid, and breaks even at 103.6607.

Now do it the other way. Sell the 100 put and buy the 110 put — a "credit spread" — and you receive 6.2290. Different options, different cash flow, and a whole vocabulary of trading advice built on the distinction.

It is the same trade. Line the two P&L profiles up across every terminal price and they differ by a constant 0.110346 at every single point — at 80, at 95, at 110, at 120, identically. That constant is not a strategic difference. It is interest: the width of the spread is 10, the debit plus the credit is 9.889654, and the gap is exactly 10 × (1 − e−rT) — the financing on the width over ninety days, which the credit version collects because the cash arrives today.

So "I prefer credit spreads because I collect premium" is a statement about cash flow and margin treatment, not about risk. The two positions have the same payoff diagram, the same maximum profit, the same maximum loss and the same breakeven. Choosing between them is a financing and operational decision — which is a real decision, and a completely different one from the one people think they are making.

The other trap in this chapter is reward-to-risk. The 100/110 spread offers 1.73 to 1; the 100/105 offers 1.33 to 1. That ranking says nothing about which is better, because it says nothing about how likely each outcome is. A structure with a 20:1 reward-to-risk that needs a 30% move is not generous; it is a lottery ticket with the odds printed on the other side.

debit + credit = (K2 − K1) · e−rT

Reads as: what you pay for the call spread plus what you receive for the put spread is the width of the spread, discounted. That identity is why the two are the same position — it is put–call parity applied to both legs.

Check yourself

A trader says they prefer selling credit spreads because "you collect premium and time decay works for you". What is wrong?

They are describing the same position twice. The bull put spread and the bull call spread on the same strikes are one trade — if one has favourable decay so does the other, because they are identical. What genuinely differs is margin treatment, assignment risk on the short leg (page 08) and the eleven cents of interest. Those are worth choosing on; the payoff diagram is not.
Derivation — why the credit and the debit are one trade

Put–call parity holds at every strike separately. Write it at K₁ and again at K₂, for the same expiry and the same underlying:

C(K) − P(K) = S − K·e−rT

Reads as: at any strike, the call minus the put is the discounted forward. It is an identity about payoffs, so it holds whatever the volatility surface is doing.

Now build the call spread and substitute each call for its put using that identity. The stock terms cancel, because there is one of them on each side:

C(K₁) − C(K₂) = [P(K₁) − P(K₂)] + (K₂ − K₁)·e−rT

Reads as: the call spread you buy equals the put spread you sell, plus the discounted width. Rearranged: debit paid plus credit received equals the width discounted, always.

That is the whole result. The two positions are not similar and they are not close relatives — they differ by a fixed amount of money that does not depend on the stock, on the volatility, or on anything you have a view about. Which is why their P&L profiles in the figure above sit parallel across every terminal price rather than crossing anywhere.

It also explains the number. The gap is the width minus the discounted width, W·(1 − e−rT), which is simply the interest on the width over the life of the trade. At three months and four-and-a-half percent on a ten-wide spread that is about eleven cents. Nobody chooses a strategy for eleven cents; they choose it because one version arrives as a credit, and a credit feels like income.

Where it breaks. Parity is a European statement. On American options the short leg can be assigned before expiry, and the two versions are then assigned in different circumstances — the put spread on a sell-off, the call spread on a rally into a dividend — so their operational risk genuinely differs even though their theoretical value does not (page 08). A hard-to-borrow underlying adds a q term to parity and the gap stops being plain interest (page 07). And margin treatment often differs between the two, which is the one real reason to prefer a particular version — and it has nothing to do with premium selling.

03

Five ways to be bullish, and none of them wins

Identical starting delta. Five structures lead in five different regions, and the lead changes hands four times.

Here is the comparison the textbooks describe and rarely draw. You are bullish on a 100 stock over ninety days. Five structures express that, and to compare them honestly each is sized so they all start with exactly 100 share-deltas — identical directional exposure, so the only differences are structural.

The sizes are already revealing. A hundred deltas costs $10,000 as stock, $936 as long 105 calls, $1,122 as a 105/115 call spread, and −$954 as short 95 puts — you are paid to take the position. The 1×2 call ratio needs a size of 35.6 spreads to reach a hundred deltas, because each one starts with almost none.

Then the outcomes, and this is the point of the page.

If the stock falls to 80, the long call is the best of the five, losing $936 — its whole cost — while the short put loses $4,011. At 95 to 105, where nothing much happens, the short put is best at +$954 and the long call is the worst, losing its entire premium. At 110 to 120 the ratio spread dominates everything, making $31,734 at 115. And at 130 the ratio has collapsed to a $21,727 loss while the plain long call — worst-in-class over most of the range — is the best of the five at +$5,014.

Five structures, one view, identical initial delta, and the lead changes hands four times across five distinct regions. The long call is simultaneously the best trade if you are wrong, the worst trade if you are slightly right, and the best trade again if you are extremely right.

Which means "what is the best bullish structure" is not a question with an answer. The question with an answer is how right do you expect to be, and how wrong can you afford to be — and each of these five encodes a different reply.

Check yourself

All five structures start with 100 deltas. What does that tell you about their risk?

Almost nothing. Matching delta matches the first derivative at one point — which is exactly page 11's lesson about how fast a local approximation stops describing a position. These five agree on the first penny of movement and disagree by tens of thousands of dollars on the outcome.
04

What you actually signed up for

Same delta, opposite everything else. The greek table is the contract you did not read.

Chapter 03 showed the outcomes. This is the mechanism, and it fits in one table. Every structure carries the same hundred deltas and completely different second-order exposure.

The long 105 call runs gamma-cash of +669, vega of +46.20 and theta of −8.30 a day. You are long movement, long volatility, and paying rent for both.

The short 95 put runs gamma-cash of −830, vega of −57.33 and theta of +7.57. Same direction, opposite everything else: you are short movement, short volatility, and collecting rent.

The 105/115 call spread is the moderate version — +367 of gamma, +25.33 of vega, −5.04 of theta — roughly half the long call's second-order exposure for the same first-order exposure, which is chapter 01's argument in numbers.

The 1×2 call ratio is in a different category: −4,160 of gamma-cash and −287 of vega on a position that is nominally just "bullish". That is six times the short put's gamma and five times its vega. The ratio is a short-volatility trade wearing a directional label, which is why chapter 03 showed it making $31,734 in one place and losing $21,727 in another.

So the greek table is where the disguise comes off. Two structures with the same delta and opposite gamma are not two versions of the same view — they are a directional view bundled with a volatility view, and the second one is frequently larger.

The practical habit that follows: before putting on any spread, look at its gamma, vega and theta per unit of delta. If those numbers are large relative to the delta you wanted, the trade is mostly not about direction, whatever you called it when you decided to do it.

Check yourself

Two structures both have 100 deltas. One has +669 of gamma cash and one has −4,160. What are you really choosing between?

You are choosing a volatility view. The shared delta is the smallest thing these two have in common. This is the same trap page 04 describes from the other end: a structure has a payoff description and an exposure description, and the exposure description is the one that determines what happens to you between now and expiry.
05

Ratios: the spread that turns on you

Sell more than you buy and the payoff has a peak. Past that peak it goes down forever.

A ratio spread buys one option and sells more than one further out. Our 1×2 — long one 105 call, short two 115 calls — is a net debit of 1.0961, and its payoff is a tent: nothing much below 105, rising to a peak of 8.904 at 115, then falling away.

The number that matters is where it crosses back through zero on the way down: 123.904. Above that the position loses money, and it keeps losing without limit, because you are net short one call.

That single fact makes the ratio a fundamentally different animal from everything else on this page. A vertical's worst case is its debit. A ratio's worst case is unbounded and is realised precisely when your directional view was most right. It is the only structure here that punishes you for being too correct.

Turn it around and you get a backspread: sell one near option, buy two further out. Now the payoff is a valley — you lose in the middle and win big at the edges, with limited risk. Backspreads are long volatility with a directional tilt; ratios are short volatility with a directional tilt.

The reason ratios get traded despite the tail is that the tent covers the region where the stock usually ends up, and the premium collected pays for the middle. That is a real edge and it is also the exact shape of a strategy that works repeatedly and then removes a year of profit in a week. Page 03's earnings-straddle chapter is the same distributional shape seen from the other side: high win rate, negative tail, and a mean that hides both.

The figure lets you drag the ratio and the strikes so you can watch the upper breakeven move. Two things to notice: widening the gap between strikes raises the peak and moves the breakeven further away, and raising the ratio from 1×2 to 1×3 does the opposite in a hurry.

Check yourself

Your 1×2 call ratio is at its maximum profit. The stock keeps rallying. What happens?

It becomes a naked short call. The peak is not a cap, it is a turning point — which is the single most important thing to know about ratios and the thing their payoff diagram makes look benign. Being spectacularly right about direction is the scenario that hurts, and no other structure on this page has that property.
06

The same view, capped and uncapped

A short straddle and a long butterfly both want the stock to sit still. One of them can end your year.

Suppose the view is not directional at all: you think the stock stays near 100 and implied volatility is too high. Two structures express it, and comparing them is the cleanest illustration of what capping risk actually costs.

Short the 100 straddle. You receive 11.0586. If the stock finishes at 100 you keep all of it. If it finishes at 85 or 115 you lose 3.941. If it finishes at 130 you lose about nineteen, and there is no bound on that number.

Buy the 90/100/110 butterfly. You pay 2.7243. If the stock finishes at 100 you make 7.276. If it finishes at 85 or 115 you lose 2.7243 — your entire cost — and that is the worst that can ever happen, at any price, forever.

So the butterfly gives up about a third of the best case and removes the entire tail. The trade-off is priced, as it should be, and the question of which to prefer is a question about how you size rather than about which is "better".

The subtle part is what the two positions are made of. Both are short volatility, but the straddle is short volatility everywhere while the butterfly is short volatility only near the body and long the wings. So a move to 130 that would be catastrophic for the straddle is merely the butterfly's ordinary maximum loss — and a large rise in implied volatility hurts the straddle much more, because the butterfly's long wings absorb most of it.

Which points at the practical rule. Structures that look similar in payoff can behave very differently in vega, and structures with the same vega can behave very differently in the tail. Reading either dimension alone gets you a surprise eventually.

Try this

Narrow the butterfly's wings toward the body. Predict what happens to its cost and to its maximum profit — and to how much it resembles the straddle.

Check yourself

Implied volatility jumps eight points overnight with the stock unchanged. You are short a straddle and your colleague is long a butterfly on the same strikes. Who is hurt more?

The straddle, and it is not close. Both positions want the stock to sit still, and only one of them is meaningfully short vega. That is why a butterfly is often described as a "cheap" way to be short volatility — the description is right about the cash and wrong about the reason, which is that most of the vega has been hedged away by the wings rather than discounted.
07

Choosing, and the question that actually decides it

Not "am I right" but "how right, how soon, and how wrong can I afford to be".

Everything above assembles into a decision, and the decision is not made on the payoff diagram. Chapter 03 established that no structure dominates. What remains is to be explicit about the four questions whose answers pick one.

How right do you expect to be? A modest move favours spreads and short-premium structures; a large move favours outright long options, which is why the long call was the worst structure over most of chapter 03's range and the best at 130. Capping the upside is only cheap if you did not need it.

How wrong can you afford to be? The long call's maximum loss is its premium. The short put's is many times that. The ratio's is unbounded. These are not refinements of one another; they are different businesses, and position sizing has to be done on the worst case rather than on the delta.

What is your volatility view? Chapter 04 showed that structures matched on delta run gamma from +669 to −4,160. If you have no volatility view, the honest choice is the structure with the least of it — and that is usually a spread rather than an outright.

When do you need to be right? Theta runs from −8.30 a day to +7.57 across the same five structures. A long option needs the move to arrive before the premium decays; a short-premium structure needs the move not to arrive, and is happy to wait.

The figure puts those together as a scenario grid — outcome across, volatility change down, best structure in each cell. It is the same instrument as page 11's scenario matrix, applied to a choice rather than to a book, and it has the same lesson: the answer lives in the corners, and no single number on the entry ticket predicts it.

The honest summary of this page is that "which spread should I use" is underdetermined until you have said something about magnitude, timing, volatility and tolerance. Most bad structure choices are not errors of arithmetic. They are answers to a question that was never fully asked.

Check yourself

You are mildly bullish, have no volatility view, and cannot tolerate a large loss. Which of the five fits best?

The call spread. Work the constraints: no volatility view rules out the long call and the short put, both of which carry large vega; intolerance of a large loss rules out the short put and the ratio, whose worst cases are many times the premium. The ratio does profit most from a mild rise — and its unbounded tail fails the third constraint, which is exactly the trade-off chapter 05 is about.

Question 6 · chapter 06

A short straddle receives 11.06 and a long butterfly on the same body costs 2.72. What does the butterfly buy you?

A bounded worst case. Both positions want the stock to sit still; only one of them can remove a year of profit in a week. The butterfly also carries far less vega, because its long wings offset the short body — which is the part the payoff diagram does not show.

Every structure on this page is priced on a flat volatility surface, which is the one assumption that most changes the answers. A real skew makes the short put richer and the risk reversal cheaper than shown here, and it moves the ranking in chapter 03 — page 02 is the correction and it is not a small one. Transaction costs are excluded from every payoff, and a multi-leg structure crosses a spread on every leg, which page 05 prices at up to eleven volatility points on a short-dated wing. Margin treatment differs by structure and by account type and frequently decides the choice before any of this arithmetic does. And every short option carries the assignment risk of page 08, which no payoff diagram shows.
