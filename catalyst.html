One number holding two things

An implied volatility quoted into an earnings date is a blend of ordinary daily drift and one scheduled shock. They obey different rules and they are worth separating — and because variance adds, separating them takes one equation.

01

Two kinds of move, one number

A stock's variance between now and expiry has an ordinary part that accumulates smoothly and a scheduled part that arrives all at once.

Page 01 modelled a stock as pure diffusion — many small independent moves, variance accruing evenly with time. That is a decent description of most days and a terrible description of the morning after an earnings release, when the whole distribution of a quarter's worth of information resolves in a single print.

The fix is not a more complicated process. It is to admit there are two sources of variance and to add them, which you are allowed to do because variance is additive over independent components — the same fact page 01 used to derive √T scaling, applied along a different seam.

σimp² · T = σdiff² · T + J²

Reads as: total variance is the ordinary variance that accumulates with time, plus a fixed lump the event contributes regardless of how far away it is.

Look carefully at the asymmetry in that equation, because everything downstream comes from it. The diffusive term is multiplied by T — the longer the window, the more of it you accumulate. The event term is not. A single earnings release contributes the same J² whether it sits inside a one-week option or a one-year option.

Which immediately explains the shape of every earnings term structure you have ever seen. Divide through by T to get the quoted volatility, and the event's contribution is J²/T — enormous in the front month and negligible in the back. The front is not "expensive." It is carrying the same lump of event variance over far less time.

The figure shows the two components accumulating. Diffusion is the ramp; the event is the step. What the market quotes is neither — it is the square root of their sum, divided by elapsed time.

Check yourself

A name has 25% diffusive vol and an earnings event worth a 6% move. Why is the two-week implied vol so much higher than the two-month?

Same lump, less time. The event contributes a fixed J² to both windows. Quoted vol is √(total variance / T), so dividing that fixed contribution by a smaller T inflates the front-month number without anything about the event being different. This is why comparing front and back implied vols on an earnings name tells you almost nothing until you strip the event out — which is chapter 03.
02

Not all days are equal

Calendar time is a poor clock for volatility. Desks replace it with a weighted one, and the weights are a view.

Black–Scholes accrues variance uniformly in calendar time, which implies a weekend contains as much risk as a Wednesday. It does not. Markets are shut, and while some information still arrives, empirically a weekend behaves like a fraction of a trading day rather than two full ones.

The practical fix is to stop using calendar days as the clock and start assigning each day a weight: how much variance it contributes relative to an ordinary trading day. An ordinary day is 1.0. A weekend day is perhaps 0.1 to 0.2. A holiday is low. The day after a long weekend is often slightly above 1. And an earnings day is three to five, because a quarter's information lands in one session.

total variance = σref² × ( Σ wi ) / 252

Reads as: add up the weights of the days you actually hold the option, not the days on the calendar. σref is the vol of a single ordinary day, annualized.

Editing the weights below is the closest thing on this page to what a vol desk actually does before an earnings season. The term structure it produces is not fitted to anything — it falls out of the weights, and every kink in it is a day you decided was special.

Notice what happens as you slide the earnings date. Every expiry before the event is unaffected; every expiry after it jumps. With the event on day 21 and a weight of 4, the twenty-day implied vol is 25.97% and the twenty-one-day is 28.54% — a 2.6-point step from one expiry to the next, caused entirely by a single day crossing into the window.

03

Stripping the event out

Two expiries, two unknowns, one linear solve. The messy curve becomes a flat one and a single number.

Take two expiries that both contain the event. Each satisfies the same decomposition with the same two unknowns:

σ₁²T₁ = σd²T₁ + J²   ·   σ₂²T₂ = σd²T₂ + J²

Reads as: two equations, two unknowns. The event term is identical in both because it is the same event; only the time differs.

Subtract them and J² cancels, leaving the diffusive vol directly. Substitute back for J:

σd² = (σ₁²T₁ − σ₂²T₂) / (T₁ − T₂)   ·   J = √(σ₁²T₁ − σd²T₁)

Reads as: the slope of total variance against time is the diffusive variance; whatever is left over is the event.

That is the entire method, and it is exact rather than approximate. A front month at 39.54% and a back month at 29.05% — a wildly inverted, unhelpful-looking pair — recover σd = 25.000% and J = 6.000%. The inversion was never a signal about the diffusive vol. It was one number hiding in the curve.

The figure is the payoff for the whole page. On the left, the raw term structure: kinked, inverted, apparently telling you something complicated. On the right, the same curve with J² removed from every point. It goes flat. The complexity was one scalar.

Derivation — why the event term does not scale with time

Split the log return to expiry into two independent pieces: the accumulated diffusion, and the jump that occurs if the event falls inside the window.

ln(ST/S₀) = Xdiff + Xevent

Reads as: the total move is ordinary drift plus, once, the shock.

Independence means variances add with no cross term:

Var(total) = Var(Xdiff) + Var(Xevent) = σd²T + J²

Reads as: independent risks add in variance, so the two pieces simply stack. No cross term, because the event does not know what the diffusion did.

The diffusion is a sum of T years' worth of independent increments, so its variance is proportional to T. The event is a single draw. It happens once, and its variance is whatever it is, regardless of how much time surrounds it. That is the entire asymmetry, and everything on this page is a consequence of it.

Divide by T to get quoted volatility and the two terms behave completely differently:

σimp² = σd² + J²/T

Reads as: quoted variance is the diffusive level plus an event term that decays like 1/T. Plot this against 1/T and it is a straight line whose intercept is σd² and whose slope is J².

That linearization is worth knowing as a practical trick: plot quoted variance against 1/T across every expiry, and if the points fall on a line, one event explains the whole curve. Curvature means more than one thing is going on.

Where it breaks. Two events in one window give you two lumps that cannot be separated from two expiries alone — you need a third, and you need to know the dates. Vol also leaks: the market often bids the event premium up in the days before the print, so J measured a week out is not J measured the morning of. And an event whose date is not yet confirmed smears the lump across several possible days, which flattens the step and biases J downward.

04

The implied move, and the factor everyone drops

The number quoted on every earnings preview is not a standard deviation. It is about 80% of one.

Having recovered J, converting it to dollars looks trivial — multiply by spot. But the number the market quotes as the implied move is usually derived from the straddle price, and that is a different quantity from a one-standard-deviation move. The gap is large and it is a constant, so it is worth getting right once.

Here is the fact. For a driftless lognormal, the price of an at-the-money straddle equals the expected absolute move:

Straddle = E |ST − S₀| = √(2/π) · S · σ√T ≈ 0.7979 · S · σ√T

Reads as: a straddle costs exactly the average size of the move, ignoring direction. Which makes sense — that is what it pays.

A one-standard-deviation move is S·σ√T, with no 0.7979 in front. So the straddle-derived implied move is about 80% of a one-sigma move, and the two get used interchangeably all the time. At 20 vol over 30 days on a $100 stock the straddle is 4.57 while one sigma is 5.73 — a 25% difference in a number people quote to two decimals.

The consequence is concrete. If you read "the options imply a 4.6% move" and then reason about the probability of exceeding it as though it were a standard deviation, you will overstate how unusual a larger move is. For a lognormal, the stock exceeds the straddle-implied move roughly 58% of the time, and exceeds a genuine one-sigma move about 32% of the time. Those are very different claims.

Check yourself

An earnings preview says "the options are implying a 7% move." The stock moves 8%. Was the market wrong?

A single move above the implied move is the base case, not a miss. The straddle price is a mean of absolute moves, and for a right-skewed distribution the majority of outcomes sit above the straddle-implied level while a minority of large ones pull the average up. Roughly 58% of moves exceed it. Judging an implied move from one print is like judging a coin from one flip — and note the second answer's caveat, because a straddle buyer can still lose on an 8% move if the vol crush costs more than the gamma gained, which is chapter 05.
05

Through the print

A fairly priced earnings straddle loses money about two times in three. That is not a mispricing — it is the shape of the payoff.

Buy the straddle the afternoon before the print, sell it the morning after. Two things happen overnight and they pull against each other. The stock gaps, which helps you. And the implied volatility collapses, because J has left the window — every remaining day is now an ordinary day, and the option reprices at σdiff instead of σimp. That is the vol crush, and it is not a market overreaction. It is the arithmetic of chapter 01 running in reverse.

Simulate it a few thousand times with realized event vol set exactly equal to implied — a perfectly fair price, by construction — and look at the distribution.

The mean is about zero, as it must be for a fair price. The median is around −0.94, and the win rate is 33%. You lose, modestly, in two trades out of three, and the trade is nonetheless fairly priced, because the third outcome is large enough to pay for the other two.

This is the single most important thing to understand about event vol, and it is a psychological problem more than a mathematical one. A strategy that loses two thirds of the time feels broken long before the sample is large enough to say anything. Traders abandon fairly priced long-vol event trades constantly, and buy fairly priced short-vol event trades enthusiastically, because a 67% win rate feels like skill until the fourth trade.

Push realized above implied and the whole distribution shifts right — at 1.5× implied the win rate goes to 52% and the mean to +1.67. Push it below and the picture is brutal: at half implied you win 5% of the time. The asymmetry between those two cases is why selling event vol looks so attractive and why it periodically ends careers.

Check yourself

You have bought fairly priced earnings straddles eight times and lost on six. What does that tell you?

Six losses in eight is the base case. At a fair price the win rate is about a third, so losing six of eight is close to the mode of the outcome distribution and carries almost no information about whether you are being charged too much. Judging this strategy needs the sum of P&L over a large sample, not the hit rate — and the sample has to be large because the payoff is dominated by rare large wins. The fourth answer is the trap: it is exactly the inference that a 67% loss rate invites, and it puts you short a tail you have just watched not happen.
06

Expressing it

If your view is about the event rather than the stock, the front month alone is the wrong instrument.

Suppose the strip says the market is charging J = 6% for a print and you think it is worth 4%. Buying or selling the front month expresses that, but it expresses a great deal else besides — you are also taking a position in the diffusive vol, and in the direction, and in the gamma between now and expiry.

The cleaner expression is a calendar: trade the front month against the back. Because both expiries share the same σd and only the front carries a meaningful J²/T, the spread isolates the event. Sell the front and buy the back and you are short the event and roughly flat the diffusive level.

The numbers make the case. On a name with 25% diffusive vol and a 6% event, a two-week option prices at 39.5% and a two-month at 29.1%. If you think J is really 4%, the fair front-month vol is 31.3% — an 8-point difference in the front and under 2 points in the back. Nearly all of your edge is in the near expiry, and nearly all of the risk you did not want is in the shared diffusive component that the spread cancels.

Two warnings, both from chapter 01's asymmetry. First, a calendar is not a clean event bet: the legs have different gammas and different vegas, so you are still short front gamma when you sell the front, and a large move hurts you regardless of what the vol does. Second, the event has to actually happen inside the front expiry — a confirmed date matters more than the vol level, and a date that moves turns the position into something you did not choose.

07

Event drill

Stripping, implied moves, and the conversions that get fumbled under time pressure.

The arithmetic of event vol is variance arithmetic, which means the mistakes are always the same two: averaging volatilities instead of variances, and treating a straddle-implied move as a standard deviation. Both are drilled here. Tolerance is ±1% on Core and ±0.5% on Full.

This page models an event as a single independent shock with a known date and a symmetric distribution. Real events leak into prices beforehand, have dates that move, arrive in clusters through a reporting season, and produce moves that are neither symmetric nor independent of the diffusive regime. The decomposition survives all of that as an approximation and stops being exact the moment any of it bites — which is why the recovered σd drifting with your choice of expiries is worth treating as a diagnostic rather than noise.
