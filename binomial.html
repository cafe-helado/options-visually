The price that ignores the odds

Ask what a stock is likely to do and you get one number. Ask what it costs to guarantee the payoff and you get another. Only the second one is the option's price — and it does not change when you change the odds.

Scroll ↓

Start here

This is the page that should probably have come first. Page 01 derived the option price by simulating thousands of paths and averaging; this one derives it from a single step and no probability at all, and then shows the two answers converging on each other.

  1. Chapter 01 is the whole idea. One period, two outcomes, and a portfolio of stock and cash that reproduces the option exactly. Its cost is the price, and the argument never mentions how likely either outcome is.
  2. Chapter 02 names the thing that looks like a probability and isn't. The risk-neutral p is an artefact of the algebra — mistaking it for a forecast is the single most common misreading in derivatives.
  3. Chapter 05 is why the model survives. Black–Scholes is closed-form and cannot price an American put. A tree can, and that is the reason every desk still runs one.
  4. Read the sawtooth chapter before you trust a tree. Convergence is not smooth, and the standard fix is one line.
Notation — every symbol used on this page
u, dThe up and down multipliers on the stock over one step. In the CRR scheme d = 1/u, so the tree recombines.
ΔShares held in the replicating portfolio. Over one step it is the ratio of the spread in payoffs to the spread in stock prices.
BCash borrowed alongside the shares. Positive means you owe it.
pThe risk-neutral probability. Looks like a probability, behaves like one algebraically, and forecasts nothing.
NSteps in the tree. Each step is dt = T/N of a year.
RecombiningUp-then-down lands on the same node as down-then-up, so N steps give N+1 terminal nodes rather than 2N.

01

One step, two outcomes, one price

Build a portfolio that pays whatever the option pays. Its cost is the option's price, and nothing about likelihood entered the calculation.

Strip the problem to its smallest interesting version. A stock at 100. One period. Two possible outcomes: up to 110 or down to 90. A call struck at 100, which pays 10 in the up state and nothing in the down state. What is it worth?

The instinct is to ask how likely the up state is, multiply, and discount. Hold that thought — it is the wrong question, and chapter 02 explains why. Ask instead: can I build something out of stock and cash that pays exactly 10 and 0 in those two states?

Buy Δ shares and borrow B. In the up state the position is worth 110Δ − B; in the down state 90Δ − B. Set those equal to 10 and 0 and you have two equations in two unknowns. Subtracting gives 20Δ = 10, so Δ = 0.5, and back-substituting gives B = 45.

So half a share, funded by 45 of borrowing, pays exactly what the call pays in both states. It costs 0.5 × 100 − 45 = 5. That is the price. Any other price and you can buy the cheap one, sell the dear one, and collect the difference with no exposure at all — the portfolios are identical in every state of the world, so their prices must be too.

Now the part worth sitting with. Slide the real-world probability of an up move across the figure. At P(up) = 0.01 the option's expected payoff is 0.10. At P(up) = 0.99 it is 9.90 — a hundredfold difference in what you expect to receive. The replication cost, printed beside it, stays at 5.000000 throughout. Not approximately: the number never moves, because the probability never appeared in the derivation.

This is the single most counterintuitive result in the subject, and the binomial model is where it is easiest to see, because you can watch it happen with two numbers and no calculus.

Δ = (Cu − Cd) / (Su − Sd)     C = ΔS − B

Reads as: hold enough shares that their spread across the two outcomes matches the option's spread, then borrow whatever makes the low state come out right. The cost of that package is the option's value.

Check yourself

Two traders disagree completely about whether the stock will rise. One thinks 90% likely, the other 10%. What should they conclude about the option's price?

They must agree. They will disagree violently about whether the trade is good — the optimist expects to make money buying it — but not about what it costs, because the cost is set by a portfolio both of them can construct and observe. That separation between value and expectation is the foundation the whole subject stands on, and it is why an options market can function between people with opposite views.
Derivation — the two equations, and where the probability would have gone

Write the replicating portfolio's value in each state and demand that it match the option.

SuΔ − B(1+r) = Cu      SdΔ − B(1+r) = Cd

Reads as: in the up state and in the down state, the shares minus the loan must equal the option. Two equations, two unknowns, no probabilities anywhere.

Subtract the second from the first. The borrowing cancels because it is the same in both states — it is a certain amount, not a contingent one.

Δ (Su − Sd) = Cu − Cd

Reads as: the share count is fixed by matching the spread. This is delta, arrived at without differentiating anything.

Substitute back for B and the price is C = ΔS − B. Notice what is absent: there is no place in either equation for a probability, because the equations describe every state rather than an average over states. That is the whole trick, and it is why the answer is a price rather than a forecast.

Where it breaks. Two outcomes and two instruments is a square system, which is why it has a unique solution. Add a third possible outcome — a jump, a gap, a takeover at a different price — and you have three equations and two unknowns, no exact replication, and no single arbitrage-free price. The market is then said to be incomplete, and prices become a band whose width is somebody's risk appetite. Everything on this page assumes the world stays square.

02

The probability that is not a probability

Rearrange the answer and a number between zero and one falls out. It is not a forecast, and treating it as one is the classic error.

Take the price from chapter 01 and rearrange it. With a little algebra C = ΔS − B becomes something that looks startlingly like an expectation.

Define p = (er·dt − d) / (u − d). Then the price is exactly the discounted average of the two payoffs, weighted by p and 1−p. On our one-step example with zero rates, p works out to 0.500000, and the discounted expectation is 5.000000 — the same answer replication gave, as it must be, because it is the same equation written differently.

So there is a set of weights under which the naive "average the payoffs and discount" calculation is right. Those weights are the risk-neutral probabilities, and three things about them matter.

They are not anybody's forecast. Nothing about the real world went into them — only u, d and the interest rate. If the true probability of an up move is 90%, p is still whatever the algebra says.

They are exactly the weights that make the stock earn the risk-free rate. That is the defining property and the source of the name. Under p, the expected stock price is the forward. The figure checks it on a 200-step tree: the tree's expected terminal price is 105.12711 against a forward of 105.12711, agreeing to the last digit shown.

They exist because replication does. The reason you may price by taking an expectation under the wrong probabilities is that you were never really taking an expectation — you were pricing a portfolio, and the expectation is a bookkeeping device that produces the same number more conveniently.

Get this backwards and you will believe the market is forecasting a 32% chance of a stock finishing below some level when it is doing nothing of the kind. What the market is telling you is what the insurance costs. Converting that into a real-world probability requires a risk premium that the option price does not contain.

p = (er·dt − d) / (u − d)      C = e−r·dt [ p·Cu + (1−p)·Cd ]

Reads as: there is one weighting under which averaging the payoffs gives the right answer, and it is the one that makes the stock drift at the interest rate. Use it to compute; do not read it as a belief.

Check yourself

An option's price implies a 32% chance the stock finishes below 90. What can you conclude?

It is a price, expressed in the units of a probability. Risk-neutral probabilities systematically overweight bad outcomes relative to reality, because people pay up for protection — which is exactly the risk premium the option price contains and the real-world probability does not. The third answer over-corrects: the number is highly informative, it just answers "what does this cost" rather than "what will happen".
03

Two outcomes become a thousand

Chain the one-step argument and it becomes a lattice. Push the steps up and it becomes Black–Scholes.

One step is a caricature — a stock with exactly two possible futures. The fix is not to abandon the argument but to repeat it. Split the year into N periods, let the stock move up or down in each, and apply chapter 01's replication at every node, working backwards from expiry.

Two features make this practical. First the tree recombines: choosing d = 1/u means up-then-down lands exactly where down-then-up does, so N steps produce N+1 terminal prices rather than 2N. A 500-step tree has 501 endpoints, not a number with 150 digits in it.

Second, the up and down sizes are chosen so the tree's variance matches the volatility you are pricing with: u = eσ√dt. That is the Cox–Ross–Rubinstein choice, and it is what makes the lattice converge to the same distribution Black–Scholes assumes.

And converge it does. On a one-year at-the-money call at 30 volatility with rates at 5%, Black–Scholes says 14.2312. A one-step tree says 16.9640 — off by more than two and a half. Ten steps gives 13.9408. A hundred gives 14.2018. Sixteen hundred gives 14.2294, wrong in the third decimal.

The rate of convergence is worth committing to memory because it is the honest answer to "how many steps do I need". Doubling N halves the error — measured across N = 50 to 1600 the ratio is 1.998, 1.999, 2.001, 2.002, 2.005. That is first order: to get one more decimal place you need ten times the work, and the work grows with N². Trees are not cheap.

The figure draws the lattice with its node values and the convergence beside it. Watch the terminal distribution take shape as you add steps — by about thirty it is visibly a lognormal, which is the sense in which Black–Scholes is the limit of this construction rather than a separate theory.

u = eσ√dt    d = 1/u    dt = T/N

Reads as: make each step's up-move the size volatility says a step of that length should be, and make the down-move its exact reciprocal so the tree folds back on itself.

Check yourself

Your 200-step tree is accurate to about a cent. Roughly what does it cost to reach a tenth of a cent?

Ten times the steps, a hundred times the work. First-order convergence combined with quadratic cost is why nobody brute-forces a tree to high precision — you either use the closed form where one exists, or you use the averaging trick in the next chapter, which buys about two decimal places for one extra tree.
04

Convergence is a sawtooth, not a slope

Adding one step can make a tree less accurate. Knowing why turns a nuisance into a free decimal place.

The convergence table in chapter 03 hides something. The errors do not shrink smoothly toward zero — they alternate in sign, step by step, while their envelope decays. Look at four consecutive trees on the same option: N = 40 is low by 0.0734, N = 41 is high by 0.0672, N = 42 is low by 0.0699, N = 43 is high by 0.0640.

So going from a 40-step tree to a 41-step tree does not improve the answer. It moves it to the other side of the truth by about the same distance. Anyone who has tried to tune a tree by nudging N has met this and usually concluded something was broken.

The cause is where the strike sits relative to the terminal nodes. In a recombining CRR tree the terminal prices are S·u2j−N, so when N is even one node lands exactly at the starting price and when N is odd the strike falls between two nodes. An at-the-money option therefore sees a structurally different terminal grid depending on the parity of N, and the pricing error inherits that alternation.

Which points straight at the fix. If consecutive trees sit on opposite sides of the answer by roughly equal amounts, average them. Price with N and with N+1 and take the mean. On this option a 25-step tree is off by +0.1103 and the 25/26 average is off by −0.0012 — about ninety times better for one extra tree. At N = 400 the raw error is −0.0074 and the average is −0.0002.

This is the practical content of the chapter: an unaveraged tree is being needlessly bad, and the correction is one line of code. It also generalises — the same idea underlies the Richardson extrapolation used to accelerate almost every first-order numerical scheme.

Try this

Move the strike away from the money and predict what happens to the sawtooth's amplitude. The mechanism in the prose tells you the answer.

Check yourself

You price an option on a 100-step tree and again on 101 steps. The answers differ by six cents. What is wrong?

Nothing is wrong — that is the sawtooth. This is worth recognising on sight, because the natural response is to hunt for a bug that does not exist. The disagreement between the two trees is itself the useful signal: it is roughly twice the error in either one, so it tells you how far off you are, and averaging removes most of it.
05

What the lattice can do that the formula cannot

Black–Scholes is faster, exact and closed-form. It also cannot price an American put, which is most of the equity option market.

If the binomial model merely converged to Black–Scholes it would be a teaching device and nothing more. It survives on real desks because backward induction can answer questions the closed form cannot even express.

The reason is structural. Black–Scholes gives you a value at expiry and a formula for today, with nothing in between. A tree gives you a value at every node — every combination of price and time the model considers possible — and once you have that, you can apply a rule at each one.

Early exercise. At every node, compare the continuation value with what exercising would pay and take the larger. That single line converts a European pricer into an American one. On a one-year 100-strike put with spot at 80, 30 volatility and rates at 10%, the European value is 16.2413 and the American is 20.2680 — a premium of 4.0267, or 24.8% of the option's value. Page 08 is about when that premium exists; this is the machinery that computes it.

Discrete dividends. A real stock pays lumps on known dates, not a continuous yield. In a tree you subtract the dividend from the stock price at the nodes where it goes ex. There is no clean way to say that in a closed form.

Barriers and path rules. Anything that depends on where the price has been — knock-outs, triggers, exercise windows — can be enforced node by node.

The figure prices both styles on the same lattice and shades the nodes where exercising wins. Raise rates and the shaded region climbs; that region is the exercise boundary from page 08, drawn here as the discrete object the model actually computes rather than as a smooth curve.

The honest cost: the tree is slower, it converges as a sawtooth, and every extra feature slows it further. Desks use closed forms where they exist and lattices where they do not, which in equities means lattices for most of the book.

Vnode = max( e−r·dt[ pVu + (1−p)Vd ] ,   exercise value )

Reads as: at every node, take the better of holding on and cashing in. Deleting the max turns it back into a European pricer, which is the whole difference between the two models.

Check yourself

Why can't Black–Scholes price an American put?

There is nothing in between. The closed form is a single expression for today's value; the American problem is a free-boundary problem whose answer is a decision rule across the whole price-time plane. The last answer is a common trap: parity is a European relation and does not hold as an equality for American options, which is precisely why page 08's put chapter needs a tree.
06

Greeks fall out of the lattice for free

You already computed the neighbouring nodes. Delta and gamma are differences between numbers you have.

A tree gives you the option's value at nodes that surround today's price, which means it has already done the work for the first two derivatives. No re-pricing and no bumping required.

Delta is the difference between the two nodes one step out, divided by the difference in stock price between them — which is exactly chapter 01's Δ, now read off a big tree instead of a small one. On the one-year at-the-money call, a 500-step lattice gives 0.624192 against Black–Scholes' 0.624252: a gap of 6 × 10−5, well inside the price error at that step count.

Gamma comes from three nodes two steps out, as a second difference. Theta comes almost free as well: the central node two steps out sits at the same stock price as today but two periods later, so the difference between them is the passage of time with nothing else changed.

Vega and rho are the exceptions. Neither volatility nor the interest rate is a coordinate of the lattice — they are baked into u, d and p — so getting those requires rebuilding the tree with a bumped input and differencing. That asymmetry is worth knowing when you are counting how expensive a full greek set is: delta, gamma and theta are nearly free, and every other greek costs another tree.

One caution the figure makes visible. Because the price itself oscillates with N, so do the greeks read off it — and gamma, being a second difference, is noisier than delta. If a tree-derived gamma looks unstable across step counts, the tree is not broken; you are differencing a sawtooth.

Δ ≈ (Vu − Vd) / (Su − Sd)     Θ ≈ (Vud − V0) / 2dt

Reads as: delta is the slope between the two nodes beside you and theta is the change at the node directly ahead of you. Both are differences between values the tree has already computed.

Check yourself

Which greek costs an extra tree to compute?

Vega, and rho for the same reason. Delta, gamma and theta are differences between nodes the tree already contains, because price and time are its two coordinates. Anything that changes the geometry of the lattice itself has to be priced by building a second lattice.
07

Where it breaks

A tree with too few steps is not merely inaccurate. It can be arbitrageable.

The risk-neutral weight p = (er·dt − d)/(u − d) is only a probability when it lands between zero and one. Nothing in the construction guarantees that, and when it fails the tree stops describing any coherent market at all.

The condition is that the risk-free growth over one step must sit between the down move and the up move: d < er·dt < u. With the CRR choice that reduces to a neat requirement on the step length.

Fail it and the model says the stock will grow faster in its worst case than cash does, which is a free lunch. Concretely: at 10% volatility with rates at 8%, a two-year option priced on a single step gives p = 1.0761. Stretch it to four years and p is 1.3867. At 5% volatility with 10% rates, even a one-year single step gives 1.5388. Low volatility and high rates is exactly the combination that breaks it, and it is not an exotic corner — it is short-dated rates products and low-volatility credit-like underlyings.

The fix is more steps, since the requirement is a bound on dt: keeping each step shorter than (σ/r)² years is sufficient. It is worth checking rather than assuming, because a tree with p outside the unit interval does not crash. It returns a number, and the number is wrong in a way that no amount of eyeballing will reveal.

Three further limits worth naming.

Convergence is first order and the cost is quadratic. Chapter 03's arithmetic — ten times the steps for one more decimal, at a hundred times the work — is the reason lattices lose to closed forms and to finite-difference schemes wherever those are available.

The tree's distribution is not the world's. Everything here inherits Black–Scholes' assumption of constant volatility and continuous, jump-free movement. A recombining lattice with fixed u and d cannot represent a volatility smile at all, which is why the model is a pricing engine rather than a description of the market — page 02 is the correction.

Discrete dividends break recombination. Subtracting a fixed cash amount at an ex-date means up-then-down no longer lands where down-then-up does, and the tree stops folding. The usual repair is to build the lattice on the dividend-stripped price and add the dividend's present value back, which restores recombination at the cost of an approximation that page 08 flags in its own derivation.

d < er·dt < u   ⟺   dt < (σ / r)²

Reads as: each step must be short enough that volatility can outrun the interest rate over it. Violate it and the tree contains an arbitrage rather than a price.

Check yourself

Your tree returns p = 1.08. What does that mean?

The tree contains an arbitrage. p above one means er·dt > u: cash grows faster than the stock's best case, so shorting the stock and lending is riskless profit. The dangerous part is that nothing throws an error — you get a plausible number back. The fourth answer inverts the fix: it is low volatility, not high, that causes this.

Question 1 · chapter 01

A stock at 100 goes to 110 or 90. A 100-strike call is worth 5. What happens to that price if the up move becomes twice as likely?

Nothing at all. The replicating portfolio pays the option's payoff in both states, so its cost is fixed by the two stock prices rather than by their likelihoods. What changes is whether the trade is attractive, not what it costs.
Question 2 · chapter 02

What defines the risk-neutral probability?

The weighting that makes the stock drift at the risk-free rate. On a 200-step tree the implied expected terminal price is 105.12711 against a forward of 105.12711 — that identity is the definition, not a coincidence.
Question 3 · chapter 03

Doubling the number of steps in a CRR tree does what to the pricing error?

Halves it. First-order convergence with quadratic cost is the tree's central weakness, and it is why the averaging trick in chapter 04 matters so much in practice.
Question 4 · chapter 04

Why do consecutive step counts give prices on opposite sides of the true value?

The strike's position against the terminal grid alternates with parity. Recognising this on sight saves a bug hunt, and averaging N with N+1 turns the nuisance into roughly two extra decimal places.
Question 5 · chapter 05

What single change turns a European tree pricer into an American one?

One max at every node. That the American problem reduces to a single line is the reason lattices remain standard equipment — on a one-year 100-strike put with spot at 80 and rates at 10%, that line is worth 4.03 on a 16.24 option.
Question 6 · chapter 07

Which combination is most likely to push p outside (0, 1)?

Low volatility, high rates, long steps. At 5% volatility and 10% rates a one-year single step gives p = 1.5388 — and the tree returns a number rather than an error, which is what makes the condition worth checking rather than assuming.

Every tree on this page is Cox–Ross–Rubinstein with d = 1/u and constant volatility, priced on a European or American vanilla with no dividends. Real implementations differ in ways that matter: alternative parameterisations (Jarrow–Rudd, Leisen–Reimer, and the trinomial family) have different convergence behaviour and some remove the sawtooth entirely; discrete dividends require either a dividend-stripped lattice or a non-recombining one; and any production pricer has to handle the exercise schedule, settlement convention and corporate-action history of the specific contract. The model's deeper limitation is the one it shares with Black–Scholes — a single volatility and a continuous path — which is why it prices vanillas well and describes markets badly.
