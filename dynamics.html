What your hedge does when the surface moves

Every greek on a risk sheet holds something still that will not stay still. This page moves it, and shows what the sheet left out.

Scroll ↓

Start here

You know what delta, gamma, vega and theta are, and you have seen a skewed surface on page 02. This page is about the gap between those numbers and what a book actually does over a day: the delta that vega is hiding, the gamma that appears only when vol moves with spot, the vega total that changes sign depending on how the term structure shifts, the daily bill for owning skew, and the fact that "realized volatility" is several different numbers.

  1. One dial runs through the page. It is called R, and it says how far at-the-money vol slides along the skew when spot moves. Learn it in chapter 01 and the rest follows.
  2. Predict before you press. Each "try this" asks for a sign. Getting the sign wrong is the common failure here, not the size.
  3. Treat R, h and φ as assumptions. None of them can be read off a screen. They are on sliders for that reason.
Notation — every symbol used on this page
bSkew: the slope of implied vol in log-strike. At −0.35, a strike 10% below spot carries about 3.7 points more vol than at-the-money.
RThe skew-stickiness ratio. When spot rises 1%, at-the-money vol changes by R × b points. R = 0 is sticky delta, R = 1 sticky strike, R = 2 roughly what a local-volatility model produces.
βSpot–vol beta: the percentage fall in the vol on your strikes for a 1% rise in spot. With vol at 40, β = 1 means 0.4 points per 1%.
hHow fast a vol shock fades with tenor. A one-point move at three months reaches tenor T as (3m / T)h points. h = 0 is a parallel shift, h = 0.5 is the root-time convention.
φAutocorrelation of daily returns. Positive means trending, negative means mean-reverting, zero is a random walk.
vegaQuoted throughout in dollars per one volatility point.
01

One hedge, three outcomes

Buy a call, hedge it at the delta on the screen, and let spot move. What you make depends on something the screen did not ask you about.

You own one six-month 100-strike call at 30 vol and you are short 54 shares against it, which is the Black–Scholes delta. The figure moves spot and reprices. The grey line is what the textbook promises: a small gain whichever way spot goes, because you are long gamma. The blue line is the same position on a surface where at-the-money vol slides down the skew as spot rises.

Now hedge without knowing the dial. You are long 100 puts struck at 95, and every day you rehedge at the delta your chosen rule implies. The market follows its own rule, which you learn only at the end. A second book, hedged at the market's true delta, trades the same days beside you.

Trader's lens

A skew trader's delta is a statement about how the surface moves, and they say it out loud: "we hedge sticky strike in quiet markets and closer to sticky delta when things move". Getting it wrong does not show up as a model error; it shows up as an unexplained directional P&L that correlates with the market, which is exactly how it is caught in attribution.

  • If your hedged book makes money when the market rallies and loses when it falls, check your delta's dial before your view.
  • Estimate the dial from the data: how much at-the-money vol actually moves per 1% of spot.
  • The difference between rules is skew times vega times the move; it is largest in steep-skew names.

Rule of thumb: a hedged options book should not be correlated with the market. If it is, the delta is wrong.

Try this

At R = 2 the hedged call loses when spot rises. Predict what happens at R = 0, where the smile travels with spot instead: does the tilt vanish or reverse?

It reverses. For a 3% move in spot the hedged contract makes about $8 either way under sticky strike. At R = 0 it makes $37 on the way up and loses $21 on the way down. At R = 2 it is the mirror image: it loses $21 up and makes $38 down. Same option, same hedge, same move.

What changed is the vol on your strike. Under sticky strike it does not move, so the Black–Scholes delta of 0.542 is the whole exposure. At R = 2 a 1% rally takes 0.35 points off the vol on the 100 strike, and the option has about $28 of vega per point, so each 1% up costs roughly $10 that the delta never counted. The hedge that would have been flat is 0.444. At R = 0 the vol on the strike rises instead and the flat hedge is 0.640.

Δflat = ΔBS + vega × (R − 1) × b / S

Reads as: the delta that keeps you flat is the screen's delta plus your vega times how far the vol on your strike moves per dollar of spot. With negative skew and R above 1 the second term is negative for anything long vega.

Desks carry this as a single number. Measure how much the vol on your strikes falls, in percent, for a 1% rise in spot, and call it β. Then a book's hidden delta, in dollars of stock, is:

hidden delta = − vega × σ × β

Reads as: dollars of vega per point, times the vol level in points, times beta. Long $1,000 of vega at 40 vol with β = 1 is short $40,000 of stock that no delta column shows. To be flat you buy $40,000.

Neither the sign nor the size of β is a law. It is an estimate from how that name's surface has been behaving, and it changes with regime. The point of the chapter is narrower: a delta-neutral long-vega book in an equity index is short the market, and a delta-neutral short-vega book is long it, by an amount you can compute.

Check yourself

You are short $5,000 of vega in an index at 20 vol, flat delta on the sheet, and you believe β is 1.2. What are you really carrying?

Long $120,000. Hidden delta is −vega × σ × β = −(−5,000) × 20 × 1.2. A selloff lifts vol and a short-vega book loses, which is what being long looks like. The short answer has the sign of a long-vega book. The $6,000 answer forgot that vega is per point and vol is 20 points.
02

The gamma you were not shown

If vol moves with spot, delta changes for two reasons at once. The sheet shows one of them.

Now a put, because this is where it hurts. One 60-day 90-strike put with spot at 100. The dashed line is what the risk sheet tells you to expect: today's delta, changing at today's gamma. The solid lines are the delta the position really has as spot moves, which is the number of shares you must hold against it to be flat.

The sheet says −16.8 shares of delta per contract and 2.16 of gamma per dollar, so at $95 it expects −27.5. Under sticky strike the flat hedge at $95 is −30.0: gamma is not constant, and it grows as the put comes toward the money. At R = 2 the flat hedge is −35.9, because the selloff also raised the vol on the strike and a higher vol on an out-of-the-money put means more delta. On a hundred contracts that is 3,588 shares where the sheet said 2,753.

Divide the change by the move and you have the gamma you actually experienced: 3.11 per dollar on the way down against 2.16 on the sheet, and 2.24 on the way up. Traders call the extra part shadow gamma, and it is why a serious risk report has an up-gamma and a down-gamma instead of one number.

gammafelt ≈ gamma + 2 × vanna × dσ/dS

Reads as: the gamma you live through is the sheet's gamma plus the change in delta caused by vol moving, counted once for the delta you hold and once for the delta your vega was hiding. Both extra pieces carry the sign of vanna times the spot–vol move.

Check yourself

You are short out-of-the-money index puts, hedged at the sheet delta, and the market falls 5% with vol rising. Compared with what your gamma number predicted, you are:

Longer, selling more into the fall. A short put is long delta. Rising vol makes an out-of-the-money put behave as if it were closer to the money, so its delta grows faster than gamma said, and yours grows with it. This is the mechanism behind short-put books needing to sell the most at the worst moment.
03

A vega total is a guess about the term structure

Adding vega across expiries assumes every expiry moves by the same amount. They do not.

A book is short $40,000 of vega in the three-month and long $50,000 in the one-year. The sheet adds them and says long $10,000 a point. Slide h to choose how much of a three-month vol move reaches the one-year, and watch the total.

Short-dated vol moves more than long-dated vol. A common desk convention is that moves scale with one over the square root of time, which is h = 0.5: a point at three months is half a point at one year. Under it the long leg makes $25,000, the short leg loses $40,000, and the "long $10,000" book loses $15,000 on a vol spike.

weighted vega = Σ vegaT × (Tref / T)h

Reads as: before adding, scale each expiry's vega by how much that expiry is expected to move when the reference expiry moves one point. The sum is then in units of "points at the reference tenor".

There is a tidy consequence. Vega itself grows with the square root of time, so under root-time shocks the two effects cancel: a three-month at-the-money straddle gains $0.398 a share when its vol rises a point, and a one-year straddle gains $0.394 when its vol rises the matching half point. A one-for-one calendar spread, which the sheet shows as long vega, is close to flat. It is a position in the shape of the curve and should be sized as one.

h is an assumption. Estimate it by regressing changes in each tenor's vol on changes in the reference tenor, and expect it to rise in a panic, when the front moves and the back barely does.

Try this

Move the long leg out to two years at the root-time setting. Predict whether the book gets more short or less.

Check yourself

You sell one-month straddles and buy six-month straddles until the sheet shows zero vega. Vol spikes at the front. Under root-time shocks you:

Lose. Equal raw vega means the six-month leg needs the same point move to offset the one-month leg, and under root-time it gets 1/√6 = 0.41 of it. Zero vega on the sheet is short about 59% of the front leg's vega in weighted terms.
04

What skew pays and what it costs

Owning the put and selling the call has a daily bill. Whether it is worth paying comes down to the same dial.

Buy a hundred three-month 90 puts at 28.2 vol and sell 110 calls at 22.1 vol against them, sized so the vega nets to zero, then delta-hedge. You own skew: you are long the expensive vol and short the cheap one. The figure shows what that position expects to make per day, when the stock realizes exactly the at-the-money vol, as a function of how the surface moves.

Under sticky strike the position loses $137 a day and there is nothing on the other side. Half is theta: the put has the higher vol, so it decays faster than the call you sold pays you. The other half is gamma: for the same vega, a higher-vol option has less gamma, so you are net short gamma and realized movement costs you too.

skew theta ≈ vega × (σput − σcall) / 2T

Reads as: the yearly theta bill for a vega-neutral risk reversal is the vega of one leg times the vol gap between the strikes, divided by twice the time to expiry. Here $142,672 of vega per unit of vol times 0.060, over 0.5, is $17,200 a year, or $68 a trading day.

What pays for it is the surface moving. When spot falls, the position becomes long vega (about $1,250 a point at $92) and if vol rises at the same moment you are paid; when spot rises it becomes short vega ($1,270 a point at $108) and you want vol falling. Each unit of R adds about $135 a day, so at R = 1.5 you still lose $69 and you break even at R = 2.0.

That number is not an accident of these inputs. Move the skew or the expiry and the break-even stays close to 2, provided the stock realizes about the at-the-money vol. Push realized vol up to 40 and it falls to 1.7, because theta is a fixed bill while the spot–vol term grows with movement. A skew of slope b is the market charging for at-the-money vol to move 2b points per 1% of spot. If it moves less, skew was overpriced and the put seller wins; if it moves more, the put buyer does. Published estimates for equity indices mostly sit between 1 and 2 in ordinary markets and jump above 2 in a crash, which is the whole story of selling index skew: paid most days, and paid back in a hurry.

Notice also what the vega line in the panel says. A position with zero vega at $100 has $1,250 of it eight dollars lower. A greek that reads zero is a statement about one spot price.

Check yourself

An index has realized R near 1.3 for a year. A colleague says the skew is steep and therefore puts are expensive. The better statement is:

Overpriced at any steepness, on that history. The test is not how steep the skew is but whether vol has been moving against spot by as much as the skew implies. At R = 1.3 it has not, so skew sellers have been collecting. The catch is that R is not stable, and the year in which it reaches 3 is the year the trade gives everything back.
05

Which realized volatility

Implied gets compared with "realized". Realized depends on how often you look, and so does what a gamma hedger earns.

Take one long price history and measure its volatility three ways: from daily closes, from weekly closes, from monthly closes. On a random walk they agree. The slider lets yesterday's return lean on today's, and the three numbers come apart.

The extreme case makes it concrete. A stock that moves 1% a day is a 16% vol stock. If those moves all point the same way for a week it has moved 5%, and a 5% week annualizes to 35.5%. Keep going for twenty days and the 22% month annualizes to 78%. All three are honest measurements of the same path.

σk-day = σdaily × √( 1 + 2 Σj<k (1 − j/k) φj )

Reads as: vol sampled every k days equals daily vol times the square root of one plus twice the weighted sum of the autocorrelations inside the window. Positive autocorrelation pushes it up, negative pushes it down, zero leaves it alone.

This matters because a delta hedger earns the volatility sampled at the interval they hedge at. Long gamma in a trending name, hedged every day, sells each uptick and gives away the trend; hedged weekly it keeps more of it. In a name that chops, φ negative, the daily hedger harvests reversals the weekly hedger never sees: at φ = −0.15 the weekly number is 14.2% against 16% daily. So "implied is 17, realized is 16, sell it" is incomplete until you say which realized, and whether the person on the other side hedges at a different clock.

φ is the least stable assumption on this page. It is small, it changes sign, and estimates of it from a few months of data are mostly noise. Use the figure to know which way the error runs, not to forecast it.

Check yourself

You are long gamma in a stock that has been trending. Daily-close realized is 20 and you paid 22 implied. Which is true?

Hedging less often may earn more than 20. Frequency leaves the expectation alone only on a random walk. In a trend, wider sampling sees more variance and so does a wider hedge interval. The cost is a bumpier result, and the trend has to persist, which is the part nobody can promise.
Derivation — why the break-even in chapter 04 is 2

Write the vol of a strike as a line in log-strike with slope b, and let at-the-money vol move by R × b per unit of log-spot. A delta-hedged option's expected daily P&L has three parts: theta, gamma times realized variance, and the cross term between spot and the vol on its strike.

E[P&L] = θ dt + ½ Γ S² σr² dt + vanna × S × (R − 1) b × σr² dt

Reads as: decay, plus what movement pays through gamma, plus what movement pays through vol shifting on your strike while your vega is changing.

For a vega-neutral risk reversal the first two parts are each about −vega × (σput − σcall) / 2T when realized equals at-the-money vol, and the third is the same quantity times (R − 1) with the opposite sign and twice the size. Setting the sum to zero gives R = 2. The figure does not use this approximation; it reprices both legs over a one-day distribution of spot, and lands on 2.01.

Where it breaks. The surface here is a straight line with no curvature, the skew does not change as spot moves, and vol has no moves of its own. Real surfaces have all three, and each adds a term: curvature is paid for by vol-of-vol, and a skew that steepens in a selloff helps the put owner beyond what R captures.

Assumptions: zero rates and dividends; a smile that is linear in log-strike; a surface whose only motion is the one the dial describes; term-structure shocks that are a power of tenor; daily returns with a single autocorrelation. In the real world the failures are called vol-of-vol, skew dynamics, term-structure twists and regime change. Next: the surface itself, running a book, or drill it.
