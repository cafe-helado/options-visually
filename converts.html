Volatility bought through a bond

A convertible is a long-dated call option that a company sold cheaply to raise money. Convertible arbitrage is the business of buying it, hedging the stock, and finding out what else came attached.

Scroll ↓

Start here

You need delta hedging from page 01 and the idea from page 16 that a hedged option earns realized volatility against the implied volatility it was bought at. This page applies both to an instrument that is not listed on any options exchange. Volatility arbitrage in its plain form is chapter 02; the other two chapters are what makes the convertible version different.

  1. Chapter 01 takes the instrument apart: a bond floor, a conversion value, and the option between them.
  2. Chapter 02 is the trade. Long the bond, short the shares, and a yearly account of carry and volatility.
  3. Chapter 03 is the risk the option model leaves out. The bond inside is a loan to the company whose stock you are short.
Notation — every symbol used on this page
FaceWhat the bond repays at maturity. $1,000 here, in five years, with a 2% coupon.
Conversion ratioShares received per bond on conversion. Ten here, so the conversion price is $100 a share.
ParityWhat the bond is worth converted today: ten times the stock price.
Bond floorWhat the bond would be worth with the conversion right removed: its coupons and face, discounted at the risk-free rate plus the company's credit spread.

σi, σrThe volatility the bond's price implies, and the volatility the stock goes on to realize.
01

A bond with a call stapled to it

Below it sits a bond, above it sits the stock, and the price is a curve between the two.

A five-year bond with a 2% coupon that can be exchanged for ten shares. The stock is at $80, so converting today would get you $800 of stock for a bond that repays $1,000. Move the stock and watch the price travel between two straight lines.

At $80 the bond is worth $1,023. Its floor is $786 and its parity is $800, so a buyer pays 27.9% over conversion value. The difference between the price and the floor, $237, is the option: ten five-year calls struck at $100.

The same instrument reads three ways depending on where the stock is. At $40 it is $828, forty-two dollars over the floor, and almost all of its risk is the company's credit. At $200 it is $2,028, 1.4% over parity, and it is stock. Around the conversion price it is an option, with a delta of 6.4 shares a bond and $6.70 of value per volatility point.

convertible ≈ bond floor + conversion ratio × call( S, conversion price )

Reads as: the bond you would own without the conversion right, plus that many call options on the stock struck at the conversion price. It is an approximation and the last chapter is about where it fails.

Check yourself

A convertible trades at $1,020 with the stock far below the conversion price and a bond floor of $1,000. What are you mostly buying?

Credit. Twenty dollars of option on a thousand of bond. Its delta and vega are small and its price will move with the spread, so hedging it with stock hedges almost none of the risk. Desks call these busted convertibles and trade them as bonds.
02

The trade is volatility arbitrage, with a bond to finance

Buy the bond, short its delta in stock, and rehedge. What you earn is the gap between the volatility you paid and the volatility you get, plus or minus the carry.

This bond is priced at a 28% volatility. You buy it for $976 and short 6.09 shares against it. The upper figure moves the stock with the hedge left alone. The lower figure runs a year of rehedging and asks what it is expected to make, as a function of the volatility the stock actually delivers.

Start with the cash, before the stock moves at all. The bond pays a $20 coupon. Financing $976 at 5% costs $48.80. The short sale raises $487, which earns about $24, and the shares cost about $2.40 to borrow. Net carry is −$6.90 a bond a year.

Now the volatility. If the stock realizes the 28% the bond was priced at, rehedging earns nothing in expectation and the year loses the carry. If it realizes 38%, the hedging earns about $16.20 and the year makes $9.30. The stock has to realize 32.6% for the position to break even.

vol edge per year ≈ ½ Γ S² ( σr² − σi² )

Reads as: half the position's gamma, times the stock price squared, times the gap between realized and implied variance. This is the whole of volatility arbitrage in one line: you are long or short that gap, scaled by gamma, and everything else is cost.

That line is the same for a listed option, and the general trade is the one page 16 lists as delta-hedged volatility arbitrage: forecast realized volatility, buy what is priced below the forecast, sell what is priced above, hedge the delta, and size by vega. Three things it leaves out decide whether the forecast gets paid. The gap is earned through gamma, so it matters where the stock is when the movement comes, not only how much there is. Each rehedge crosses a spread. And the option can reprice against you long before the realized volatility shows up.

The reason to do it through a convertible is the price. Convertibles are issued by companies that want cheap financing, to buyers who mostly cannot short stock, so the option inside has often been priced below listed options on the same name. Here the bond would be worth $67 more if it were priced at 38% instead of 28%. That discount is the edge, and it is also a statement that nobody else wanted the package, which is the subject of the next chapter.

The carry line is the fragile part. Make the stock hard to borrow and the year turns negative even with volatility on your side.

Try this

The borrow fee goes from 0.5% to 6%. Predict the realized volatility the trade now needs.

At a 6% fee the carry is about −$34, the expected year is about −$17 even at 38% realized, and the break-even climbs to 46.4%. A convertible that looks ten points cheap against listed volatility can be fairly priced once the borrow is counted, and names with large convertible issues outstanding are often exactly the ones that are expensive to short, because every arbitrageur holding the bond is short the stock.

Check yourself

You buy a convertible at 28% implied, hedge it, and the stock realizes 28% all year. What did you make?

The carry. With realized equal to implied the gamma pays for the decay and no more. What is left is the plumbing, −$6.90 here. A position can have positive carry, when the coupon is high or funding is cheap, and then fair volatility is enough; the point is that the two are separate lines and both have to be written down.
03

The floor is not a floor

The bond inside the convertible is a loan to the company. When the stock falls far enough, the loan is what you are worried about.

Chapter 02's upper figure showed a hedged convertible making money when the stock falls. It assumed the credit spread stayed at 300 basis points while the equity lost a third of its value. The slider below lets the spread widen as the stock falls, by a fixed number of basis points for each 1%.

With the spread fixed, a 30% fall makes $19.90 a bond. Let it widen ten basis points for each 1% the stock loses and the spread is 600bp after the fall, the floor has dropped from $786 to $681, and the hedged position has lost $84.80. The break-even is 1.8 basis points of widening per 1% of stock, which is very little.

The short stock does hedge some of this, since a falling share price pays the short. It is the wrong size for it. The delta was set by an option model that knows nothing about default, and it shrinks as the stock falls, at the moment the credit exposure is growing. Desks address that by hedging more stock than the model delta on weak credits, or by buying credit protection or puts, and each of those costs carry that chapter 02 did not count.

Two more things belong here that no slider captures. The link between spread and stock is an assumption and it is not linear: it is quiet for long periods and violent in a few weeks. And the holders are concentrated. When many funds own the same bonds on leverage and one is forced to sell, the bonds cheapen, the others mark down, and the selling spreads, which is what happened across the convertible market in 2008. Cheapness that widens is a loss before it is an opportunity.

Run the position through it. You are long 100 bonds and short their delta in stock, rehedged every day, through thirty trading days that start calm and turn into a sell-off. The issuer's credit spread widens 10 basis points for every 1% the stock falls. You can carry credit protection on any share of the bond floor's spread risk; it pays when the floor falls and costs the day's spread while you hold it. Buy it whenever you like.

Trader's lens

A convertible arbitrageur hedges the stock with stock and the credit with credit, and spends most of their risk time on the link between the two. In a sell-off the equity hedge works and the bond floor does not, because the market reprices the issuer's chance of paying back at the same moment it reprices the stock.

  • Delta-hedged is not hedged: the floor carries credit risk the stock hedge cannot see.
  • Protection is cheapest when nobody wants it. Put it on in the calm, size it to the floor.
  • Watch borrow too: the stock you are short gets harder to borrow exactly when everyone is selling it.

Rule of thumb: if the issuer's spread moves with its stock, your delta hedge is short a put on its credit.

Check yourself

A hedged convertible book is long gamma on the risk report. The equity market falls 25% with credit spreads widening sharply. The likeliest result is:

A loss. The gamma on the report is the option's. The position also holds a corporate bond for each option, and in that scenario the bond is the larger risk. This is the general lesson of the page: an arbitrage is defined by what it hedges, and judged by what it does not.
Derivation — what the bond-plus-option model assumes

The page prices the convertible as two separate things: a stream of coupons and principal discounted at the risk-free rate plus a spread, and a number of call options struck at the conversion price and discounted at the risk-free rate alone.

V = Σ coupon × e−(r+s)t + face × e−(r+s)T + CR × C( S, face / CR, T, σ, r )

Reads as: the credit-risky bond, plus conversion-ratio calls with a strike equal to the face value per share.

Where it breaks. The strike is not fixed: converting means giving up the bond, whose value moves with rates and credit, so the option is really an exchange of one risky asset for another. Most convertibles can be called by the issuer once the stock is well above the conversion price, which cuts the option's life short and its value with it. Many carry investor puts, dividend protection and takeover clauses that change the payoff. Conversion creates new shares, which dilutes the stock the option is written on. And if the company defaults, the option is worth nothing at the same moment the bond is impaired, which the separate discount rates ignore. Production models put stock and default on one lattice; the decomposition here is for seeing the three behaviors, not for marking a book.

Assumptions: a non-callable five-year bond, no dividends, a flat risk-free rate, constant volatility, a credit spread that is either fixed or a straight-line function of the stock's fall, and a first-year volatility edge computed from today's gamma. In the real world the failures are called issuer calls, credit-equity correlation, borrow recalls and crowded exits.
