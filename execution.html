The hedge that costs something

01

Putting the bill back in

Page 01's replication chapter left one line off the invoice. Adding it inverts the conclusion.

Page 01 sold an option, hedged it mechanically, and measured what was left. The answer was clean: the hedging error has a standard deviation that falls like 1/√N, so more rebalancing is monotonically better and the continuous limit is exact. That result is correct and it is also missing something, because every one of those N rebalances is an order that crosses a spread.

Charge for them and two forces run in opposite directions. Hedging error still falls like 1/√N. But total turnover rises — each rebalance is smaller as N grows, yet there are more of them, and the sum grows toward √N. So the thing you are minimising is a sum of a decreasing term and an increasing one, which is the classic shape of a problem with an interior answer.

minimise   E[cost] + λ · sd(P&L)

Reads as: trade off the money you know you will spend against the uncertainty you are trying to remove. λ is how much you dislike the uncertainty.

At five basis points of cost and a moderate risk aversion, the objective bottoms out around 128 rehedges over a ninety-day option. Hedge less and the residual risk dominates; hedge more and you are paying spread to remove risk you had already mostly removed. The curve is genuinely U-shaped and the minimum moves with both sliders — raise costs and it slides left, raise risk aversion and it slides right.

One honest note about the scaling. Cost approaches √N only asymptotically — between N = 128 and N = 512 the observed ratio is 1.76 against a predicted 2.0, because at low rebalance counts the initial hedge and the final unwind dominate the turnover and neither scales with N at all.

Derivation — why the objective is U-shaped

Split the total into the two things that move in opposite directions as you rehedge more often.

hedging error sd ≈ c1 / √N

Reads as: the risk you fail to remove falls like one over the square root of the number of hedges. This is page 01's replication result, unchanged.

Now the bill. Each rehedge trades a smaller amount as N grows — the delta moves less over a shorter interval — but there are more of them. The expected absolute delta change over a step of length T/N scales like √(T/N), so total turnover scales like N·√(1/N) = √N.

expected cost ≈ c2 · k · √N

Reads as: the money you spend grows like the square root of how often you trade. Halving the interval does not double the bill, it multiplies it by about 1.41.

Add them with a risk aversion λ and differentiate. One term falls like N−1/2 and the other rises like N1/2, so the sum has an interior minimum wherever their derivatives cancel.

N* ∝ (c1λ / c2k)2/3

Reads as: hedge more often when risk aversion is high and less often when trading is expensive, with both effects entering to the two-thirds power. The exponent is why halving costs does not double the optimal frequency — it raises it by about 59%.

Where it breaks. The √N cost scaling is asymptotic and the figure's own numbers say so: between N = 128 and N = 512 the observed ratio is 1.76 against a predicted 2.0, because at low rebalance counts the initial hedge and the final unwind dominate turnover and neither depends on N at all. The formula also assumes cost is proportional to size, which is the first thing that fails once your order is large relative to volume — at that point cost becomes convex in size and the optimum moves further left than this expression suggests.

Check yourself

Your desk halves its commission rate. What happens to the optimal rehedge frequency?

It rises. The optimum sits where the marginal cost of one more rebalance equals the marginal risk it removes. Cheapen the marginal cost and you can afford to buy more risk reduction before it stops being worth it. Only at exactly zero cost does it run to infinity — which is page 01's continuous limit, and the reason that result is a theorem about a frictionless market rather than advice.
02

Impact against timing risk

Every parent order faces the same trade-off, and it has the same shape as chapter 01 for the same reason.

Having decided to buy fifty thousand shares, you face a second problem that is structurally identical to the first. Trade it all now and you pay a large, certain impact cost — you are the only buyer and you walk the book. Trade it over the day and impact falls, but the price wanders while you work, and where it ends up is uncertain.

So it is cost against variance again: fast is expensive and predictable, slow is cheap and risky. The optimal trajectory is neither instant nor infinitely patient, and it depends on how much you dislike the uncertainty.

The figure shows mean slippage and its standard deviation as the order is sliced more finely. Slippage falls quickly at first and then flattens, while the standard deviation keeps shrinking — but both curves are dominated at the fast end by impact and at the slow end by price risk.

The impact coefficient is an assumption. Real impact models are fitted to proprietary execution data, the parameters differ by name, size and regime, and any number printed here would be invented. It is on a slider and labelled, and the honest use of this figure is to reason about which direction things move rather than to read a number off it.

03

The menu

Each algorithm optimises something. Knowing which thing is the entire skill.

Execution algorithms are not interchangeable and they are not ranked. Each is the solution to a stated objective, and picking one is choosing an objective, usually without saying so.

04

The benchmark decides the verdict

One execution, two reports, two conclusions. This is why execution arguments never resolve.

Take a single set of fills — a uniform execution, buying steadily into a rising market. Score it two ways.

Against interval VWAP, it comes in at −0.00. Dead on benchmark. The report says the algorithm did precisely what it was asked to do.

Against arrival price, the same fills cost 1.47. The report says the execution lost a point and a half against the price at which the decision was made.

Both numbers are correct. They answer different questions. VWAP asks "did you trade better than the market's average during your window," which is a question about execution skill with the market's path taken as given. Arrival asks "what did the whole process cost relative to the moment you decided," which includes the market moving away from you while you worked.

Neither is right in the abstract, and the choice is not innocent. VWAP forgives trend and punishes deviation; arrival punishes delay. A trader measured on VWAP is incentivised to spread out; the same trader measured on arrival is incentivised to hurry. Hand those two mandates to identical traders and you will get systematically different behaviour and each will be optimising correctly.

The practical rule: pick the benchmark that matches why you are trading. Hedging a delta you want gone is an arrival problem, because the risk started when you decided. Rebalancing an index is a VWAP problem, because being average is the objective.

Try this

Set the trend to zero so the market drifts nowhere. Predict whether the two benchmarks still disagree, then turn the trend back up.

Check yourself

You are hedging a delta you want off the book today. Which benchmark should you be measured against?

Arrival. The reason you are trading is to remove a risk that exists from the moment you decided, so any drift between decision and completion is part of what the hedge cost you — and that is exactly what an arrival benchmark captures and a VWAP benchmark discards. Measuring a hedge against VWAP rewards spreading the order out, which is precisely the behaviour that leaves you exposed for longer. The last answer is a joke with a serious edge to it: the benchmark should be fixed before the trade, because afterwards there is always one that flatters.
05

Dark venues and the fills you wish you had not got

Resting at the midpoint saves the spread. The saving is not free, and what it costs is measurable.

A dark pool matches without displaying quotes, typically at the midpoint. The appeal is obvious: no spread paid, no information leaked by showing an order. The problem is subtler and it is about who chooses to trade with you.

When you rest passively, you do not decide when you get filled — your counterparty does. Uninformed flow crosses at random. Informed flow crosses when it is right, which is to say just before the price moves against you. So your fills are not a random sample of moments; they are selected toward the bad ones.

The simulation makes the bias legible. With no informed flow, the average price move immediately after your fills is +0.00 — no bias, as it should be. Raise the informed share to 25% and the average post-fill move is −0.20; at 50% it is −0.41. You are not being unlucky. You are being selected.

This is the mirror of the market-maker's problem: page 05 of this series looks at the same phenomenon from the quoting side, where the defence is widening. Here, resting passively, the defences are different — minimum quantity constraints to avoid being pinged by small informed orders, venue selection based on measured post-fill drift, and simply accepting that a mid fill in size is sometimes a warning rather than a win.

The number worth carrying: saving half a spread is not worth it if the post-fill drift exceeds half a spread, and whether it does is an empirical question about the venue, measurable from your own fills.

Take the informed fraction down to almost nothing. Predict what happens to the post-fill drift — does it flatten, or reverse?

06

Hedging with the basket

An ETF is a wrapper whose price is held to its contents by an arbitrage with a cost. That cost is the whole story.

Hedging index or sector exposure with an ETF is usually cheaper than trading the basket, and understanding why requires understanding creation and redemption.

An authorised participant can hand the fund a basket of the underlying shares and receive new ETF shares, or hand back ETF shares and receive the basket. That right is what pins the ETF price to its NAV. If the ETF trades above NAV, an AP buys the basket, creates shares, sells them, and pockets the difference — which pushes the premium back down.

The mechanism is not free, so it does not pin exactly. It creates a band: the price can wander from NAV by up to the cost of doing a creation before the arbitrage becomes worth executing. Inside the band, nothing happens. At the edge, APs step in.

Which gives the practical rule. When creation costs are low — a large liquid domestic equity ETF — the band is a couple of basis points and the ETF is effectively the basket. When creation costs are high — illiquid underlyings, different time zones, closed markets, credit instruments that do not trade — the band is wide, and the ETF's price is its own thing that happens to be related to NAV. Premiums and discounts in those products are not mispricings; they are the band doing its job.

One more consequence worth knowing: ETF liquidity is inherited, not intrinsic. A thin-looking ETF on a liquid basket can absorb size, because an AP can always create against the underlying. A thick-looking ETF on an illiquid basket cannot. Screen liquidity is the wrong number to look at; the basket's liquidity is the right one.

Try this

Raise the creation cost to its maximum and watch tracking loosen. Predict whether the premium wanders symmetrically around zero or develops a bias.

Check yourself

An ETF shows thin volume on screen but its underlying basket is highly liquid. Can you hedge size in it?

Liquidity is inherited. A market maker quoting that ETF is not limited to the shares currently changing hands — they can take the other side and hedge in the basket, then create or redeem to square up. So the size you can do is set by the underlying, not by the ETF's own tape. The reverse is the trap worth remembering: an ETF with heavy screen volume on an illiquid basket looks tradeable and is not, because the arbitrage that would absorb your order is expensive to run.
07

Where it breaks

Every number on this page rests on an impact model, and impact is the least observable quantity in the whole series.

The U-curve, the optimal trajectory and the dark-venue simulation all share one input that nobody can measure directly. This chapter is about how much weight that input can carry.

Impact models are fitted, not observed. You never see the price that would have prevailed had you not traded, so impact is inferred by comparing executions against a counterfactual that does not exist. Every estimate is therefore a model output conditioned on the sample of orders a firm happened to send, which is itself selected — you trade more when you think you can. The coefficient in this page's figures is a slider precisely because no honest version of it is a constant.

Slippage attribution is contentious. Chapter 04 showed the same fills scoring on benchmark against interval VWAP and losing a point and a half against arrival. That is not a curiosity; it is the daily argument between a trading desk and the desk it trades for. Whoever picks the benchmark has picked the answer, and the choice is usually made by whichever side wrote the report.

The close is crowded. A large share of daily volume prints in the closing auction, which makes it the cheapest place to trade and simultaneously the place where everyone else's order also is. Impact models calibrated on continuous trading understate what happens when a lot of correlated demand arrives in the same fifteen seconds, and the auction's price-formation mechanism is not the one the model assumes.

Costs are not linear in size. The √N argument in chapter 01 assumed cost proportional to the amount traded. Beyond a few percent of daily volume that stops being true and cost becomes convex, which moves every optimum on this page to the left — hedge less often than the linear model says, because the marginal rebalance is more expensive than the average one.

And the hedge itself is a choice. Chapter 06 hedged with an ETF, which introduces basis risk that none of the other figures price. A hedge that is cheap to execute and imperfectly correlated is not obviously better than one that is expensive and exact, and that trade-off is a portfolio question rather than an execution one.

What survives all of this is the shape of the arguments rather than the levels. Costs create an interior optimum; trading faster buys certainty and pays impact; benchmarks encode a view; dark fills are adversely selected; ETF tracking loosens with creation cost. Those conclusions are robust to the parameters. The specific numbers are illustrations, and this page has tried to keep every one of them on a slider so you can see how much they move.

Check yourself

Your broker's transaction cost analysis shows your algo beating VWAP by three basis points every month for a year. What have you learned?

Very little on its own. Consistently beating a benchmark by a small margin is usually evidence about the benchmark rather than about the algorithm. The diagnostic is to score the same fills against arrival: if the two disagree persistently and in the same direction, you are measuring the interaction between your trading style and the benchmark's blind spot, not skill.
