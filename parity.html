Two ways to own the same thing

Put-call parity is not an identity to memorize. It is a machine for building any one of stock, calls, puts and cash out of the other three — and the price of the machine is a number you can read off the screen and compare to your funding.

01

One identity, and everything that falls out of it

Parity is usually presented as a formula to check. It is better read as a statement that two portfolios are the same portfolio.

Buy a call and sell a put at the same strike and expiry. At expiry you will own the stock at K no matter what happens — if it finishes above, you exercise; if below, you are assigned. There is no outcome in which you do not end up long stock at K. That combination is therefore not similar to a forward on the stock. It is a forward on the stock.

Two things that are the same must cost the same, and the only difference between owning the stock today and agreeing to own it at expiry is the carry in between: the financing you pay or earn, less any dividends you would have collected. Write that down and you have parity:

C − P = S + r/c − K

Reads as: the price of the synthetic minus the price of the actual is exactly the carry. Nothing in it is a model — no volatility, no distribution, no assumption about how the stock moves.

That last point is worth pausing on, because it makes this page different from every other page in the series. Black–Scholes is a model and can be wrong. Parity is an arbitrage relationship: if it fails by more than transaction costs, you can construct a portfolio that takes in money today and can never lose. There is no view involved, no distributional assumption, and no volatility input. It holds for any process the stock could possibly follow.

Everything else on your screen follows from rearranging it. The combo is the left side. The straddle is the sum rather than the difference. And each of the four instruments can be written in terms of the other three.

Proof — the four-line arbitrage argument

Build two portfolios today.

Portfolio A: buy one call, sell one put, both struck at K. Cost: C − P.

Portfolio B: buy the stock, borrow the present value of K. Cost: S − PV(K).

At expiry, whatever the stock does:

A pays ST − K   ·   B pays ST − K

Reads as: identical payoffs in every state of the world. If ST > K the call is exercised; if ST < K the put is assigned. Either way you own stock at K.

Two portfolios with identical payoffs in every state must have identical prices today, or you buy the cheap one, sell the dear one, and hold to expiry with no residual risk. Setting the costs equal:

C − P = S − PV(K) = S + r/c − K

Reads as: the difference between discounting K and simply subtracting it is the carry. Writing it as "+ r/c − K" instead of "− PV(K)" is the desk convention, because r/c is the number actually being traded.

Where it breaks. Three places, all real. American options can be exercised early, so the put leg of a conversion can be assigned before expiry and the relationship becomes an inequality rather than an equation. Dividends must be announced — an unexpected special dividend changes r/c after you have traded. And the short stock leg requires a borrow that can be recalled, which is chapter 08's subject and the single most common way this "riskless" trade loses money.

Check yourself

Parity requires no assumption about volatility. What does that actually buy you?

It converts a disagreement into a trade. If your model says an option is cheap, you might be wrong about vol. If parity is violated, no view is required — you construct the box, hold it, and collect the difference regardless of what the stock does. That is why revcon desks are financing desks rather than volatility desks: they are not taking a position on the distribution, they are lending and borrowing money through the options market and competing on rate.
02

The synthetic, and what it costs to be wrong about carry

Build stock out of options and watch it track. Then mis-mark the rate and watch the gap open by exactly as much as theory says it should.

A long call and a short put at the same strike is synthetic long stock. Its P&L against spot is a straight line with slope one, identical to owning shares. The figure below draws both; at the correct carry they lie on top of each other and you cannot see two lines.

Now mis-price the carry. Drag the rate away from fair and the synthetic separates from the stock by a constant — not a widening wedge, a parallel shift. That shape matters: a carry error is a level error, not a directional one. You do not lose more when the stock moves against you; you lose the same amount in every state, which is exactly what makes it a financing trade rather than a market trade.

The size of that shift is what the whole revcon market competes over. On a $300 strike for 38 days, ten basis points of rate is about three cents of package. Desks fight over that because it is riskless if you have the borrow and the balance sheet.

03

The trade that has no distribution

Page 01 spent a chapter showing that a delta hedge shrinks your risk toward zero. This one removes it outright, and the difference is the whole reason a financing desk is not a volatility desk.

Run the same few thousand random price paths through three positions and look at what each one is worth at the end. Long stock gives you the distribution you expect — wide, roughly symmetric in logs, a real gamble. A short call delta-hedged sixty times gives you something much tighter, because that is what hedging does: page 01 measured the residual and found it falling like 1/√N, so more rebalancing buys a narrower distribution and it never quite reaches zero.

The conversion gives you a vertical line. Not a narrow distribution. Not a distribution with small standard deviation. A single value, achieved on every path, with no rebalancing and no model.

That is worth sitting with, because it is easy to read the three panels as "risky, less risky, least risky" and miss that the third one is a different kind of object. The middle panel is literally page 01's replication experiment replayed — same construction, same 1/√N residual. The delta hedge is an approximation that improves with effort — it needs a volatility input, it needs you to trade all day, and it is wrong if the vol you assumed was wrong. The conversion is an identity. It needs no vol input, no rebalancing, and no view. You set it up once and the arithmetic does the rest.

So the question a revcon desk asks is never "where is the stock going" or even "what is realized vol going to be." It is only: what rate did I lock, and what does my funding cost? Everything on the rest of this page is machinery for answering that precisely.

Try this

Raise the number of hedge steps and predict whether the conversion's distribution narrows the way the delta-hedged call's does. It does not — and that is the chapter.

Check yourself

You double implied volatility in the simulation. What happens to the three distributions?

The conversion does not move. It holds two options, but they are locked against each other and against the stock so that every path ends at the same place — you deliver at K whether the call is assigned or the put is exercised. Volatility is the parameter that describes how paths spread out, and the conversion's payoff is constant across paths, so vol cannot touch it. This is exactly why a revcon is quoted as a rate rather than a vol: there is no vol in it.
04

The combo and the straddle

Difference and sum. Two numbers that between them contain both option prices.

Desks rarely quote a call and a put separately when trading them as a package. They quote the combo — the difference C − P — and the straddle, the sum C + P. Those two numbers carry exactly the same information as the two option prices, and converting between the representations is one line of algebra each way.

C = (Straddle + Combo) / 2   ·   P = (Straddle − Combo) / 2

Reads as: average the sum and the difference to recover either leg. Add them and the put cancels; subtract and the call cancels.

This is worth having automatic because it is how you sanity-check a screen. Given a straddle market and a combo market you can produce both option markets in your head, and if the resulting call or put disagrees with what is quoted, one of the three is stale.

The last identity on the desk sheet is the buy-write, and it carries a condition that is almost never written down:

C − intrinsic = P + r/c

Reads as: a call's extrinsic value equals the put's value plus carry. The condition: "intrinsic" here means the signed quantity S − K, not max(S − K, 0).

That distinction is not pedantry. With S = 90 and K = 95, using the signed amount the identity holds exactly; using true intrinsic, which floors at zero, the two sides come out 1.61 and 6.61. Desk usage always means the signed parity amount, and the identity is stated without the caveat because on a buy-write the call is normally in the money. Take the sheet literally on an out-of-the-money strike and you will get a wrong answer with no warning.

Check yourself

A straddle is quoted at 7.80 and the combo at 5.42. What is the call worth?

6.61. Average the sum and the difference. The third option, 1.19, is the put — and picking it is the most common slip, because the two formulas differ only in a sign and both look plausible. A useful check: the call must exceed the put whenever the combo is positive, since combo is C − P by definition.
05

What r/c actually is

Carry is two things netted against each other, over a period defined by settlement dates rather than trade dates.

Carry has exactly two components and one date convention, and nearly every pricing error is a mistake in one of the three.

Interest. A conversion is long stock, which costs money to hold, so the converter is effectively lending. A reversal is short stock, which raises cash, so the reverser is borrowing. The interest component is that financing over the life of the trade, computed on the strike rather than the spot — because K is what the forward settles at.

Dividends. Whoever is long the stock collects them, and the synthetic does not. Dividends therefore subtract from the package: a higher dividend means a lower or more negative revcon price at the same implied rate. Only dividends with an ex-date falling between trade settlement and expiry settlement count. One that goes ex before you settle is already in the stock price; one after expiry is somebody else's problem.

r/c = Interest − Dividends   ⟹   C − P = Interest − Div + Comm

Reads as: the package price is financing minus dividends, plus what the broker takes. S and K have cancelled because you trade the synthetic against the stock at the same strike.

The dates. This is where the errors live. The clock runs from trade settlement to expiry settlement, not from today to expiry Friday. Options settle T+1, so a Thursday trade settles Friday, but a Friday trade settles Monday — three calendar days. Expiry Friday settles the following Monday. A holiday anywhere in either window pushes the date out and adds a day of interest, and on a large strike a single day is real money.

The convention is ACT/360: actual calendar days, divided by 360. That 360 is not an approximation of the year, it is the money-market convention that SOFR and EFFR use, and it is what makes the resulting rate directly comparable to your funding.

Check yourself

Why is the interest component computed on the strike K rather than on the stock price S?

K is the financed amount. The synthetic obliges you to pay K at expiry, so the present-value calculation discounts K — that is what appears in parity as PV(K), and the difference between K and PV(K) is the interest term. Using S instead would be a different trade, and on a strike far from spot it would be wrong by a lot. It also explains why the implied rate divides interest by K.
06

The conversion and the reversal

One formula. It works for both directions, any dividend, any strike, bid or offer. The direction changes only what you compare the answer to.

A conversion is long stock against a short combo: you buy shares, sell the call, buy the put. You end up flat the market and long a financing position — you have effectively lent money to expiry. A reversal is the mirror: short stock, long combo, and you have borrowed.

Both price off the same equation, and there are no special cases:

Interest = (C − P) + Div − Comm

Reads as: back the financing out of the quoted package by adding back the dividend and removing the broker's cut.

The sign convention is where people go wrong, and the rule that actually works is not the one usually taught. The sign reflects your cash flow, not the word on the ticket. Money leaving your pocket enters negative; money arriving enters positive.

Most of the time that reduces to "bid is negative, offer is positive," which is why the shortcut survives. But it is a shortcut, and it breaks on a case you will meet: a listed reverser on a hard-to-borrow name. Somebody shows you a reverser, so you would be converting — you are on the bid. On an HTB name the stock loan is valuable, so the converter gets paid. That 0.65 is a credit coming to you, and it enters positive despite being a bid. Enter it negative because the ticket says bid and your implied rate comes out with the wrong sign entirely.

Commission is simpler and has no such subtlety: it always subtracts, with no bid/offer distinction, because it is a cost in either direction. It sits in parity as + Comm on the right-hand side, so solving for interest turns it into − Comm. At the standard $0.25 per option that is 0.25 ÷ 100 × 2 = $0.005 per share across the two legs.

What the direction does change is the benchmark. A converter is lending, so the comparison is the short funding rate, conventionally EFFR minus 25 basis points, and the converter wants the implied rate above it. A reverser is borrowing, so the comparison is EFFR plus 25, and the reverser wants the implied rate below it. Same number, opposite verdict — which is why a screen that flags "cheap" without knowing your direction is useless.

The last one is the case that breaks the shortcut. It is a bid to convert — and it enters positive, because on a hard-to-borrow name the converter is being paid for the stock loan. Notice the first three: nearly the same strike, dividend and tenor, and rates spanning 110 basis points. On a dividend-dominant name the implied rate is enormously sensitive to the package price, which is why a penny of mis-marked market is a real number.

Derivation — four formulas that look right and are not

This equation was arrived at by discarding several plausible-looking alternatives, each of which worked on the examples it was built from and failed elsewhere. The failures are more instructive than the answer.

Attempt 1 — Interest = Div − M. Define M as the quoted price and solve. It works, but only if you already know whether the dividend or the interest dominates, because that determines M's sign. The trader reads a positive number off a screen; the sign is not in the quote. Fails outright on no-dividend names where the converter receives.

Attempt 2 — Interest = M + Div ± Comm, with M = C − P − S + K. Takes the sign from parity directly, which is theoretically clean and practically unusable: it requires entering negative numbers for dividend-dominant names and positive for interest-dominant ones, so you must know the answer to enter the question. It also leaves the commission sign genuinely ambiguous.

Attempt 3 — commission flips with bid/offer. Tempting, since the price does. But commission is a cost in both directions, and making it conditional produces errors on one side or the other depending on which examples you calibrated against.

Attempt 4 — adjust the quote for commission first, then add the dividend. An externally suggested simplification. On a dividend-dominant convert bid it returns 64.8% where the answer is 2.96% — because it assumes the quote and the dividend are always additive, and on those names the price subtracts from the dividend instead.

What survives is the version with no cleverness in it at all:

Interest = (C − P) + Div − Comm

Reads as: signed price, plus dividend, minus commission. The direction is carried entirely by the sign of the input; the formula itself has no conditionals.

The lesson generalizes. Every attempted simplification moved a piece of the sign logic into the formula, where it then had to be right for all cases at once. Leaving all of it in a single signed input — where a human decides once, from cash-flow direction — is what makes it work for high dividend and none, HTB and ETB, convert and reverse, without a single special case.

Check yourself

A convert prices out at an implied rate of 2.96%. EFFR is 3.64%. Is this a good trade?

It is a pass. A positive implied rate is not the test; beating your funding is. The converter lends at 2.96% while funding at 3.39% and loses 43 basis points for the privilege of tying up balance sheet. This is the single most common misreading of a revcon screen — the rate looks fine in isolation and is only meaningful against the correct side's funding benchmark.
Check yourself

A listed reverser on a hard-to-borrow name is shown at 0.65. You would be converting, so you are on the bid. What sign does 0.65 take?

Positive. This is the case that kills the "bid is negative" shortcut. The sign encodes cash-flow direction and nothing else: the stock loan is valuable, the converter receives 0.65, so it enters as a credit. Interest becomes 0.65 − 0.005 = 0.645, and the implied rate is 41.5% — you are being paid 41.5% annualized to lend the stock. Enter it negative because the ticket says "bid" and you get a large negative rate that looks like a screaming trade in the opposite direction.
07

Simple, compound, and which one the screen means

Two conventions that agree to a basis point on short dates and diverge on long ones. Mixing them is a real error with a real size.

Having backed out the interest in dollars, you annualize it. There are two conventions and both are in daily use.

Simple = (Interest / K) × (360 / Days) × 100

Reads as: what fraction of the strike did you earn, scaled up to a year. No reinvestment.

Compound = 360 × ( (K / (K − Interest))1/Days − 1 ) × 100

Reads as: what daily rate, compounded every day, produces that same dollar interest.

Under thirty days the two agree to well under a basis point — on the 17-day example they are 2.9647% and 2.9669%, a difference of about a fifth of a basis point. Past a few months the gap widens, and on a jelly roll spanning several months it is worth several basis points, which is more than the spread being competed over.

The trap. Take a jelly roll priced with compound interest at exactly 4.00% and run it back through the simple-rate formula and you get 4.007%, not 4.000%. Nothing is broken — you asked a different question. But if the pricer quotes compound and the screen you compare against quotes simple, you are looking at a spread that is partly a units artifact. Fix the convention before you argue about the number.

One more asymmetry worth knowing: for positive rates compound sits slightly above simple, and for negative rates it sits slightly closer to zero. That is ordinary compounding behaviour and not a sign error in the pricer, but it surprises people the first time a negative implied rate comes back less negative under compounding.

Check yourself

Your pricer shows a jelly roll implying 4.007% simple. A counterparty quotes the same structure at 4.000%. What is the most likely explanation?

Convention mismatch. That specific 0.7bp gap is exactly what you get running a compound-priced jelly roll through the simple-rate formula. A day-count difference is also possible and worth ruling out second — it would usually be larger. The general discipline: before treating a small rate difference as edge, confirm you are both on ACT/360, both on the same settlement dates, and both on the same compounding convention. Most apparent edge at this scale is one of those three.
08

Jelly rolls

Two revcons in different expiries. The stock cancels, and what is left is the pure interest and dividend differential between two dates.

A jelly roll is a conversion in one expiry against a reversal in another, on the same underlying. The two stock legs are opposite and equal, so they net to nothing and you are left flat the market. What survives is the carry between the near expiry and the far one — a pure term-structure-of-financing trade, with no stock position and no volatility exposure.

The common use is rolling: you are carrying a conversion that is about to expire and you want the same position further out, so you reverse the near leg and convert the far one in a single package.

Price each leg independently. This is the part that is often shortcut and should not be, because the near and far legs frequently have different strikes. Each leg is a standalone revcon with its own K and its own day count measured from the same trade settlement:

Leg = K × ( (1 + r/360)days − 1 ) − Div + Comm   ·   JR = Far − Near

Reads as: compute what each expiry's financing is worth on its own strike, then take the difference.

When both legs share a strike it collapses to the interest between the two expiry settlement dates, which is the version most people carry in their head. Note also that only dividends going ex between the two expiry settlements matter: anything before the near expiry affects both legs identically and cancels.

Check yourself

On a jelly roll, a dividend goes ex two days before the near expiry. How does it affect the price?

It cancels. Both legs are priced from the same trade settlement, so a dividend before the near expiry sits inside both periods and subtracts from both leg prices identically. The difference is unchanged. Only dividends going ex between near and far expiry settlement survive into the jelly roll price — which is exactly the window to check when you are pricing one, and a good reason to look at the dividend calendar rather than a single "annual dividend" field.
09

Borrow, and why the riskless trade isn't

Hard-to-borrow names break the benchmark, not the formula. And the borrow is the thing that actually loses money.

Everything so far assumed you can short the stock at a normal rate. When you cannot, nothing about the pricing changes — the formula is identical — but the number you compare against does. Instead of EFFR ± 25 basis points you use the actual borrow rate for that name, and the pricer needs a manual override for it.

This produces results that look alarming and are not. Negative implied rates are ordinary on hard-to-borrow names. A negative rate means the converter is paying to hold the trade, which makes sense when the cost of borrowing exceeds the interest earned on the short sale proceeds. The test is never whether the rate is positive. It is whether the rate beats your funding: a convert implying 12% against a 15% borrow is a three-point loss even though 12% sounds enormous.

Where the real risk lives. A conversion is described as riskless and is not, and the gap is almost entirely the borrow. If the stock becomes harder to borrow after you put the trade on, your funding cost rises while the package price is fixed — you are locked into lending at a rate that was fair and no longer is. Worse, a recall forces you to buy in the short leg at whatever the market offers, which converts a financing trade into a directional one at the worst possible moment. This is the mechanism behind most of the memorable revcon losses, and it is not in any of the formulas above.

Two smaller ones worth naming. Early exercise: the options are American, so a short in-the-money call can be exercised against you before an ex-dividend date, unwinding one leg and taking away the dividend you had priced in. And unannounced dividends: parity requires knowing r/c, and a special dividend declared after you trade changes it retroactively.

Early assignment is worth seeing rather than describing, because it does something specific to the picture from chapter 03. The rule governing it is exact: a rational holder exercises a call early, giving up its remaining time value, only when the dividend exceeds that time value. Since the time value of an at-the-money call equals the corresponding put, the condition is just put value < dividend.

Feed that rule into the same simulation and the vertical line from chapter 03 splits into two. Most paths still pay exactly the carry. The ones where the call goes deep enough in the money before the ex-date get assigned, you deliver the stock early, and you never collect the dividend you had already priced into the package. The gap between the two modes is essentially that dividend.

This is the honest version of "riskless." The trade has no market risk — no path dependence, no vol exposure, nothing chapter 03 could not eliminate. What it has is a set of discrete, structural ways to lose: assignment, recall, and a dividend that changes. None of them are in the pricing formula, and all of them are in the P&L.

Check yourself

You are long a conversion. The stock goes special and the borrow rate jumps from 0.5% to 18%. What happened to your position?

You are locked in on the wrong side of it. The revcon fixed your return; it did not fix your cost of funds. When borrow spikes, everyone else's fair value for that package moves with it, so unwinding means paying the new level. This is the specific way "riskless arbitrage" produces real losses, and it is why a revcon book is monitored on borrow just as closely as on rate. A conversion is a lending trade with a short-stock leg attached, and the leg is where the risk is.

Question 1 · chapter 01

What is put–call parity actually for?

It is a machine for construction. Reading it as an identity to verify misses everything the page is about — conversions, reversals, boxes and jelly rolls are all the same rearrangement applied twice.
Question 2 · chapter 03

A delta-hedged short call has a P&L standard deviation of 0.63. What does a conversion have?

Zero, exactly. Hedging is an approximation that improves with effort; a conversion is an identity that needs none. Plotting them side by side is the fastest way to feel the difference between the two ideas.
Question 3 · chapter 05

What is r/c on a revcon screen?

An implied financing rate. The whole point of the revcon market is that this number is observable and tradeable, and it frequently disagrees with any published rate.
Question 4 · chapter 09

Why is a 'riskless' arbitrage not riskless?

The identity is riskless; the position is financed. Switch on early assignment and the vertical line splits in two — at a $1.50 dividend, 33% of paths get assigned and the mean falls to −0.40.
Question 5 · chapter 04

What is a combo?

Synthetic stock. It is parity's first and most useful rearrangement, and once you see it the conversion is just a combo traded against the actual shares.
Question 6 · chapter 08

Where does the financing assumption enter a jelly roll?

The rate and the dividends between the dates. Everything else cancels, which is what makes the jelly roll a clean way to trade the forward financing curve rather than a view on the stock.

Parity is an arbitrage relationship and holds regardless of how the stock moves — but it assumes European exercise, announced dividends, a stable borrow, and frictionless settlement. American options can be assigned early, specials get declared, borrow gets recalled, and settlement dates move for holidays. Every one of those is a way a riskless trade has actually lost money.
