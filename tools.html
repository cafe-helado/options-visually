The toolkit

Five calculators to keep open beside a screen. Each one is a page of the series turned into something you can type your own numbers into, with the page it comes from linked so the reasoning is one click away.

  1. Vol to moves — the conversion you do fifty times a day, with a printable cheat sheet.
  2. Vol cone — paste a column of closing prices and see where today's realized vol sits against its own history, and against the implied you are being quoted.
  3. Hedged P&L — what a delta-hedged option is expected to earn if your realized-vol view is right.
  4. Bet sizing — the Kelly fraction for an option structure, given what it costs, what it pays, and the probability you believe.
  5. Vol drag — why volatility costs a compounding investor money, and what daily rebalancing does to a leveraged ETF.
01

Turn a vol into a move before anyone finishes the sentence

Annual vol over sixteen is a daily move. Everything else is the same square root.

A volatility quote is a standard deviation per year. To use it you need it per day, per week, per expiry, and in dollars. The square root of time does all of it, and √252 ≈ 16 is close enough that a 32-vol stock moves about 2% a day and a 16-vol index about 1%. Page 01 derives the √T; page 18 explains why the straddle's move is only 80% of a standard deviation.

daily move ≈ σ / 16 · move over T ≈ S · σ · √T

Reads as: divide the annual vol by sixteen for a day, and scale by the square root of the time for anything else.

02

Is this vol high? Only against its own history

A vol cone puts today's realized vol at every lookback inside the range it has lived in. Implied gets judged against that, not against a feeling.

"Thirty vol is expensive" means nothing until you know what thirty vol is for this name. A vol cone answers it. Take a history of closes, compute rolling realized vol over 10, 21, 42, 63, 126 and 252 days, and for each window record the range it has spanned. Short windows swing wildly and long ones barely move, so the bands narrow into a cone.

Then lay two things over it: where each window's realized vol sits today, and the implied vol you are being quoted. Implied near the top of the cone at the matching tenor is rich against history; near the bottom, cheap. Sinclair's work on volatility trading popularized the tool, and it is still the fastest honest answer to "is this high". Paste any column of closes below — a CSV with dates works, because the last number on each line is read as the close.

σ_realized(w) = √( 252 · Var[ ln(Sₜ / Sₜ₋₁) ] over the last w days )

Reads as: the standard deviation of daily log returns over the window, annualized by √252. Page 18 covers what changes if you use absolute returns instead.

03

What a hedged option earns if your vol view is right

Buy at implied, hedge daily, and the expected P&L is the gap between realized and implied variance, scaled by vega.

A delta-hedged long option earns ½Γ(ΔS)² on each move and pays theta for it. Theta is set by the implied vol you paid; the gamma income is set by the vol that actually happens. Average it out and the expected P&L is vega × (σ_r² − σ_i²)/(2σ_i) — close to vega times the vol difference for small gaps, and convex for large ones because the payoff is in variance.

Two caveats to carry. This is an expectation: page 16 chapter 07 shows the same realized vol paying anywhere from −2.61 to +6.12 depending on when it arrives relative to your gamma. And it assumes the option stays near the money; far from the strike, gamma fades and so does the income.

E[P&L] ≈ vega · (σ_r² − σ_i²) / (2σ_i)

Reads as: you are paid the difference in variances, converted into vol points through vega. Short the option and the sign flips.

04

Having an edge is half of it; sizing is the other half

An option bet with a positive expectation can still ruin you if it is too large. The Kelly fraction is the size that maximizes long-run growth.

Price a structure as a bet: it costs c and pays W if you are right. The market's implied probability is c/W. If you believe a higher probability p, you have an edge — and the question every trader underweights is how much of the bankroll to put on it.

The Kelly criterion answers: bet f* = p − (1 − p)/b, where b = (W − c)/c is the payout odds. Growth rises to a peak at f* and falls away on both sides. For a small, even-odds edge, twice Kelly gives back all of the growth and half Kelly keeps three-quarters of it; for a long-shot payoff the curve is lopsided — on this trade half Kelly keeps 86% — but the penalty for oversizing always arrives. Because your p is an estimate, most practitioners bet a fraction of Kelly. The default here is page 20's biotech trade: a 10/5 put spread at $0.19 that pays $5 if the failure price is as bad as your team thinks.

f* = p − (1 − p) / b · g(f) = p·ln(1 + f·b) + (1 − p)·ln(1 − f)

Reads as: bet your edge divided by the odds; growth is the probability-weighted log of what happens to your bankroll.

05

Volatility is a cost to anyone who compounds

The median investor earns the average return minus half the variance. Lever it daily and the drag grows with the square of the leverage.

Up 50% then down 50% leaves you at 75%. Volatility pulls compounded growth below the arithmetic average by about σ²/2 a year — the same −σ²/2 that sits in page 01's lognormal. It is why the median outcome lags the mean, and why reducing volatility at the same average return raises wealth.

A leveraged ETF resets to its multiple every day, so its compounded growth is Lμ − L²σ²/2. Compare that with L times the index's growth and the difference is −(L² − L)σ²/2: a drag that grows with the square of the leverage and the square of the vol. At 20 vol, a 3× fund loses about 12% a year to it — more than many years' worth of the index's excess return. Inverse funds pay it too, and more: a −1× fund has L² − L = 2.

growth ≈ μ − σ²/2 · L× daily reset: Lμ − L²σ²/2

Reads as: volatility costs half its square in growth, and leverage multiplies the return by L but the cost by L². Continuous rebalancing and lognormal returns are assumed; fees and financing make it worse.

These tools are calculators, not forecasts. The hedged-P&L tool gives an expectation that real paths scatter widely around; the vol cone describes history, not the future; Kelly sizing is only as good as the probability you feed it, and over-confident inputs are the usual way it goes wrong; the leveraged-ETF figures assume continuous daily rebalancing and ignore fees, financing and borrow. Inspired by the habit, common among practitioners who write about volatility, of turning every concept into a number you can compute in your head.
