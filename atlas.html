Every structure, on every axis

Page 04 reduced a structure to five bars at one point in space. This is the atlas underneath that: what each greek actually looks like across spot and time, which of them are secretly the same number, and what any of it is worth to a desk that hedges its delta away.

Start here

Page 04's fingerprint is a snapshot — one spot, one expiry, five bars. It is the right first tool and it hides three things that decide whether a trade works. This page is those three things.

  1. Chapter 01 is the one that reorganizes everything. At a flat volatility, theta and gamma are the same number in different units, always. Vega joins them whenever every leg shares an expiry.   2. Chapter 03 is why a bar chart is not enough. A butterfly's gamma is negative at the money and positive in the wings — it changes sign at spot 91.45 on the example here. A single number for "gamma" describes one point and misleads everywhere else.

01

Four axes, not seven

Three of the greeks people quote separately are one number wearing different units. Knowing which three saves a great deal of wasted structuring.

Write out the greeks of a single option with zero rates and two identities fall out that are usually left in the appendix:

Look at what is not in each. Theta carries σ² and no T; vega carries T. That difference decides everything on this page.

Because theta is a fixed multiple of gamma with no expiry in the constant, theta and gamma stay locked together across any mix of strikes and any mix of expiries, as long as every leg is marked at the same volatility. Sum any weights you like: the ratio survives.

Vega is different. Its constant carries T, so vega tracks gamma only while every leg shares one expiry. Mix expiries and the link breaks — which is precisely what a calendar is for. A 30-day/90-day calendar on the example here runs gamma −0.0196 and vega +8.33: short gamma and long vega at once, which no single-expiry structure can do at any strikes and any weights.

So the honest count of independent axes at one expiry is four — direction, the fused gamma-theta-vega axis, vanna and volga. Adding a second expiry unlocks a fifth: the gamma/vega split itself. That is the whole structural reason calendars exist and it is not a matter of taste.

θ = −½ · Γ · S² · σ² ν = Γ · S² · σ · T (r = q = 0)

Reads as: theta is gamma priced in time, and vega is gamma priced in time and expiry. The missing T on the left is why theta never separates from gamma, and the T on the right is the only handle you have.

Check yourself

You want to be long gamma and short vega. What strikes do you pick?

It is impossible at a single expiry. ν = Γ·S²σT with the same T for every leg, so any weighted sum scales both identically — the signs cannot differ. A reverse calendar does it: short the 90-day, long the 30-day gives positive gamma and negative vega. Every other answer here is strike selection, which cannot change a ratio that does not depend on strike.
02

The atlas

Twelve structures, every axis, one scale. Pick a greek and read down the column.

Sixteen structures, normalized against a one-lot at-the-money straddle at the same spot, expiry and vol, so the columns are comparable and the straddle reads 1.00 by construction.

Two readings the table gives up immediately. Sign before size — most structures are obvious once you know whether they are long or short each axis, and the magnitudes only matter afterwards. And look for the zeros, because what a structure does not have is usually more informative than what it does.

The pairs worth staring at are the ones that separate vanna from volga, since that is where structures stop being interchangeable. A straddle has essentially no vanna (0.005) and no volga (−0.006) — it is a pure bet on the fused axis and nothing else. A strangle has the same near-zero vanna but volga of +0.95: it is a straddle plus a bet that vol moves a lot. A risk reversal inverts that — vanna +0.057, volga −0.016 — a pure skew position with almost no view on vol convexity.

That is why all three exist and why substituting one for another is not a cosmetic choice. They are long different things.

And then the atlas does something a payoff diagram cannot: it shows you two rows that are the same row.

A covered call is a short put. Not similar to one — identical, greek for greek. Long stock at 100 against a short 100-strike call gives delta +0.4828, gamma −0.50, vega −0.50, vanna −0.0025, volga +0.0031. A naked short 100-strike put gives delta +0.4828, gamma −0.50, vega −0.50, vanna −0.0025, volga +0.0031. Every figure agrees to twelve decimals, because put-call parity says they are the same instrument written two ways.

The mirror holds too: a covered put — short stock against a short put — is a short call, delta −0.5172 and all.

Notice also what the stock leg touches. Comparing a covered call against a naked short call, every greek is identical except delta: gamma −0.0463, vega −11.43, theta +20.85, vanna and volga all unchanged. Stock has delta one and no other greek, so covering a position is a statement about direction and about nothing else.

Which lands hard on a volatility desk. Hedge the delta out of a covered call and what remains is a short at-the-money option — short gamma, short vega, collecting theta, breaking even on the same 1.5703% daily move as everything else in chapter 05. The premium collected, $343 a contract, is not income; it is the price of the volatility you just sold. The word "covered" describes the delta leg, and the delta leg is the part a vol fund removes before it starts.

Check yourself

A straddle and a strangle both read long on the gamma-vega-theta axis. What separates them?

Volga, and it is the whole difference. An at-the-money straddle has its vega maximized already, so a vol move barely changes it. A strangle's wings gain vega as vol rises, which is why a strangle outperforms in a genuine vol explosion and underperforms in a grind. The first option is the common description and it is wrong in a way that costs money in exactly the scenario people buy strangles for.
03

A profile, not a number

A butterfly is short gamma at the money and long gamma in the wings. One number for its gamma is right at one spot and wrong everywhere else.

Every entry in chapter 02's atlas is a reading at one spot. Move spot and the readings do not merely shrink — several of them change sign.

The butterfly is the clearest case. At the money its gamma is −0.16 of a straddle's; at spot 92 it is −0.015, and below 91.45 it turns positive. You are short gamma in the body and long it in the wings, which is what the structure is: a short straddle with insurance bolted on.

The same is true of the iron condor, which flips at both edges, and of the ratio spread, whose gamma gets steadily more negative as spot rises toward the short strikes and then recovers past them.

This is not a technicality. A desk that is short a butterfly and reads "short gamma −0.16" has described the position at the only spot where it is currently sitting. The interesting question — what happens if the underlying moves five percent — is answered by the shape, and the shape is the opposite sign.

The practical habit is to read a greek as a curve over spot and note where it crosses zero. Those crossings are where the position changes character, and they are the levels at which a hedge that was working stops.

Check yourself

Your risk report says a long butterfly is short gamma. Where is that true?

Near the body only, and the sign flips at the wings. That is the structure's entire appeal: it is short gamma where you expect to sit and long gamma where you would be hurt. A risk number quoted at current spot describes the first half and hides the second, which is why the profile matters more than the reading.
04

What time does to a profile

Gamma concentrates and explodes; vega drains away. Both at rates you can compute exactly.

A structure's profile is not fixed. As expiry approaches, at-the-money gamma grows like 1/√T while vega shrinks like √T, and the position you put on stops being the position you are holding.

Concretely, on the 30-vol example: an at-the-money straddle's gamma goes from 0.0535 at 90 days to 0.0927 at 30 days to 0.508 at one day. Moving from 30 days to 7 multiplies gamma by 2.072, against a theoretical √(30/7) = 2.070. Over the same move vega multiplies by 0.483, against √(7/30) = 0.483.

So a short-dated book is a gamma book whatever anyone intended, and a long-dated book is a vega book. The transition is not a decision you make; it happens to you at a rate the square root sets.

The effect is sharper for structures with a zero crossing. A butterfly's gamma at the body goes from −0.0029 at 90 days to −0.505 at one day — a factor of 170 — while its gamma out at 93 turns positive somewhere between 30 and 14 days and keeps climbing. The structure does not just get bigger; the geography of it redraws itself. That is what makes short-dated butterflies and condors so much livelier than their greeks at inception suggest.

ΓATM ∝ 1 ÷ √T νATM ∝ √T

Reads as: halving the time left multiplies gamma by 1.41 and multiplies vega by 0.71. A position drifts from a vega trade to a gamma trade without anyone doing anything.

Check yourself

You hold a 30-day straddle and do nothing. Three weeks later, what has changed?

30 days to 7 multiplies gamma by 2.07 and vega by 0.48. Both follow from the square root of time and neither is optional. It is the reason a book has to be re-sized as it ages rather than merely re-hedged, and the reason "I am long vega" is a statement with a shelf life.
05

Delta-hedged, they all break even on the same move

At a flat volatility every structure on this page needs an identical daily move to pay its theta. Skew is what breaks that, and the break is the trade.

A delta-hedged position's daily P&L, to second order, is gamma against theta. Set the two equal and solve for the move that breaks even:

Substitute chapter 01's identity θ = −½ΓS²σ² and the gamma cancels completely. What is left is σ/√365 — the implied daily move, and nothing else. For a 30-vol name that is 1.5703%.

So at a flat volatility, a straddle, a strangle, a butterfly, a condor, a ratio, a calendar and a diagonal all break even on exactly the same 1.5703% move. Not approximately — identically, to every decimal, because the structure-dependent term divided out.

That is worth sitting with, because it kills a whole family of intuitions. A structure cannot make a hedged position "cheaper to carry" or "easier to break even on." What a structure decides is where across spot your gamma is concentrated and what sign it has there. The breakeven per unit of gamma is fixed by the vol you paid.

And then skew breaks it. Mark each strike at its own implied volatility and theta stops tracking gamma, because each leg's theta carries its own σi². On a downward skew of 40 basis points of vol per point of strike, the breakevens separate: a straddle still needs 1.5703%, but a put spread needs only 1.1185%, a risk reversal 1.3277%, and a butterfly needs 1.5957% — slightly more.

Every one of those gaps is a structure that is short the expensive strikes and long the cheap ones, or the reverse. That difference is the only thing that makes one hedged structure genuinely better carry than another, and it is a statement about the surface rather than about the shape. Which is page 02's point arriving with a number attached.

Where this comes from

Over one day a delta-hedged position earns ½·Γ·(ΔS)² from the move and pays θ/365 in carry. Breaking even means ½·Γ·(ΔS)² = −θ/365, so ΔS = √(−2θ / (365·Γ)).

With θ = −½ΓS²σ² that becomes ΔS = √(ΓS²σ²/(365Γ)) = S·σ/√365, and as a percentage of spot it is just σ/√365. Gamma appears in both the numerator and the denominator and leaves. Nothing about strikes, weights or expiries survives the cancellation, which is why the answer is the same for every structure.

Where it breaks: the cancellation needs one volatility. With a surface, θ = −½S²·Σ Γiσi² while gamma is Σ Γi, and the two no longer differ by a constant — the breakeven becomes a gamma-weighted average of the leg volatilities rather than a single number. It also assumes continuous hedging and a second-order expansion: on a genuine jump the (ΔS)² term understates a long-gamma position's gain and understates a short one's loss, which is page 15's subject. And it uses σ√(1/365) as the daily move, which prices calendar days; a desk hedging on trading days should use √(1/252) and gets 1.890% instead.

Check yourself

Someone proposes a butterfly instead of a short straddle because "it is easier to break even on." At a flat vol, are they right?

Identical breakevens, at a flat vol. Less gamma means less theta in exactly the same proportion. The butterfly's real arguments are that the wings cap the tail and that the gamma changes sign out there — both genuine and neither about breakeven. Once skew is on, the comparison becomes real again, and on a normal equity surface the butterfly is the slightly worse carry, not the better one.
06

In fund units

A volatility desk does not size in lots. It sizes in dollars per volatility point, and every other number follows from that one.

Delta-hedged, a position's daily P&L decomposes into five terms. Everything a volatility fund does is an opinion about which of them it wants:

Put a fund's numbers on it. A one-lot 30-day at-the-money straddle at 30 vol on a $100 stock carries $22.85 of vega per volatility point and pays $11.43 of theta per day, per contract.

A desk wanting $100,000 of vega per point therefore holds about 4,376 contracts. That is $43.8m of option notional and $3.0m of premium, and it bleeds $50,000 a day in theta.

Against that bleed, a 1% move earns $20,278 of gamma, a 2% move earns $81,111, and a 3% move earns $182,500. The gamma grows with the square of the move while theta is linear in time, which is the entire asymmetry the position exists to harvest. Breakeven is the same 1.570% as ever.

And the number that reframes the whole position: one point of implied vol is worth $100,000, which is two days of theta. A desk long this straddle is not primarily running a gamma trade at all — a two-point mark-up covers four days of carry, and the vol mark will move by two points long before the realized path has settled anything. The gamma-theta duel is the story people tell; the vega mark is usually the P&L.

dP&L = ½Γ(ΔS)² + θΔt + ν·Δσ + vanna·ΔS·Δσ + ½·volga·(Δσ)²

Reads as: movement, carry, the vol mark, the two moving together, and the vol move being large. The first two are the trade people describe; the third is usually the one that pays or does not.

Check yourself

A desk is long $100k of vega per point in 30-day straddles and pays $50k a day in theta. What is the position really about?

The vega mark dominates over any short horizon. The gamma-theta duel resolves over weeks; the vol mark moves points in a session. This is why volatility funds report in vega notional and why "we were right about realized and lost money anyway" is an ordinary sentence — the fourth option has the sign backwards, since a long straddle pays theta rather than collecting it.

Question 1 · chapter 01

Which greeks are locked together across any mix of expiries at a flat vol?

Gamma and theta, always. Vega carries a T in its constant and gamma does not, which is why mixing expiries separates vega from the pair — and why a calendar can be short gamma and long vega at the same time when no single-expiry structure can.
Question 2 · chapter 01

Can a single-expiry structure be long gamma and short vega?

Impossible at one expiry, at any strikes. A reverse calendar — long the near option, short the far — is the trade that does it. Strike selection cannot help, because the constant relating vega to gamma does not depend on strike.
Question 3 · chapter 03

Where is a long butterfly's gamma positive?

Short in the body, long in the wings. That is the structure. A risk report quoting one gamma number describes the body and hides the wings, which is exactly the part that matters when the move that worries you arrives.
Question 4 · chapter 04

A 30-day straddle ages to 7 days. What happens to its gamma and vega?

Gamma up 2.07×, vega down to 0.48×. Both are the square root of the time ratio and neither is optional. A book has to be re-sized as it ages, not merely re-hedged, and "I am long vega" has a shelf life measured in weeks.
Question 5 · chapter 05

At a flat 30 vol, which delta-hedged structure needs the smallest daily move to break even?

They are identical, to every decimal. Less gamma buys proportionally less theta. What a structure decides is where across spot the gamma sits and what sign it has there — never the breakeven. Skew is the only thing that separates them, and on an equity surface it moves a put spread down to about 1.12%.
Question 6 · chapter 06

A desk holds $100k of vega per point in 30-day ATM straddles at 30 vol. How much theta does it pay daily?

$50,000, which is two days per vol point. The last two options are the per-contract figures — $11.43 of theta and $22.85 of vega — and the ratio between them is what generalizes: vega ÷ daily theta = 2T·365/... in short, a 30-day option gives two days of carry per point, and a 90-day one gives six.

Every identity here assumes zero rates and no dividends, which is where θ = −½ΓS²σ² comes from; with rates the theta picks up a term in rK·e^(−rT)·N(d₂) and the collinearity with gamma becomes approximate rather than exact, though at present short rates and one-month expiries the error is well under a percent. The breakeven derivation is a second-order expansion and assumes continuous delta hedging: a real desk hedges discretely, which adds the variance page 06 measures, and a genuine jump breaks the (ΔS)² term entirely. The daily move uses calendar days, σ/√365; a desk that hedges only on trading days should use σ/√252 and gets 1.890% rather than 1.570% for the same 30 vol, and the two conventions are mixed constantly. The skew used in chapter 05 is a linear 40 basis points of vol per point of strike, which is a caricature of a real surface — actual skew is convex and steepens into expiry, so the separations shown are the right sign and roughly the right size rather than a quotation. The atlas normalizes against a one-lot ATM straddle at one spot and one expiry; every entry moves the moment any of those change, which is chapters 03 and 04. And the fund figures assume a hundred-share contract with no financing, borrow or margin cost, all of which a real desk pays and none of which appear in any greek.
