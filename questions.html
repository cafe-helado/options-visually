The questions they ask you

Twenty-two questions on a ladder, from ones with a single right answer to ones with no formula at all — and six of them worked at full length. Each is a trap built around a plausible wrong answer, and each resolves in a figure you can move.

This one takes the subjects apart and puts them back together in the shape a question comes in — because knowing what gamma is and being able to answer "you are long a 25-delta call and the stock rallies five percent; are you longer or shorter?" turn out to be different skills, and only the second one ever gets tested.

Notation — every symbol used on this page
S, K, TSpot, strike, and time to expiry in years. T = 0.5 is six months.
r, qThe financing rate and the dividend-or-borrow rate, both continuously compounded.
σVolatility, annualized. 25% means one standard deviation of 25% over a year.
IntrinsicWhat the option would pay if expiry were now: S − K for a call, K − S for a put, floored at zero.
WidthThe distance between the two strikes of a vertical spread. The most it can ever pay at expiry.
e−rTThe discount factor. A dollar arriving at T is worth this many dollars now — always less than one.
Dollar gammaS²Γ. The weight the market puts on each moment of a hedged position's life, and the reason two paths with the same volatility pay differently.
Implied correlationThe average pairwise correlation that makes an index's option price consistent with the prices of its components.
01

Start with the ones that have a single right answer

Twenty-two questions on three rungs. Read, decide, then press — and watch the figure settle it.

A good question is not a test of memory. It is a small trap built around a belief that is almost right, and the useful part of answering it is discovering which of your beliefs was the almost.

The deck below runs in three tiers. Warm-up questions have one right answer and a one-sentence reason; if any of them surprises you, that is a gap worth closing before anything else on this page. Working questions need two ideas held at the same time — a bound and a discount factor, a greek and a time horizon. Hard questions have no clean answer at all: the honest response is a range, and knowing why it is a range is the thing being tested.

Each card resolves in the chart beside it, drawn live from Black–Scholes rather than stored. Move up a tier only when the current one feels obvious.

Try this

Two warm-up cards ask the same question about a call and about a put: can it trade below its intrinsic value? Predict whether the two answers match, then look at both.

Check yourself

Why is a question with a plausible wrong answer worth more than a hard calculation?

It locates a belief. Every card in this deck is built around something that is true in a neighbouring case — puts and calls do behave alike in most respects, more time is worth more money, more hedging usually is better. The question marks the place where the neighbouring case stops applying, and finding that place is the entire skill.
02

A six-wide call spread that can never be worth six

The stock is far above both strikes and the spread still trades under its width. Two separate reasons, and only one of them ever goes away.

You own the 100/106 call spread — long the 100 call, short the 106. It is six points wide, so six is the most it can pay at expiry. The stock is at 130, thirty points through the upper strike, six months to run, and the screen says 5.35. Why is it not six?

The first answer everyone reaches for is that the stock could come back. That is right, it is not the whole answer, and separating the two halves is the point of the question. Push the spot slider to 200, where coming back is out of the question: the spread goes to 5.88 and stops. It does not go to 6.00, and no amount of further rallying will take it there.

What is left is a discount factor. At expiry the spread pays six dollars — but it pays them then. Today you can only be paid the present value of a certainty, and 6 × e−0.04 × 0.5 is 5.8812. The ceiling on a European vertical is not the width. It is the width discounted, which is the price of a zero-coupon bond whose face value is the width.

C(K₁) − C(K₂) ≤ (K₂ − K₁) · e−rT

Reads as: a vertical spread can never be worth more than its width discounted back to today. The width is what it pays at expiry; the discount factor is the price of having to wait for it.

So the shortfall at a spot of 130 splits cleanly. Twelve cents of it is interest you will not be paid — the difference between six dollars now and six dollars in six months. The rest is the residual chance that the stock finishes below 106, which at these parameters is about twelve percent. As expiry approaches the second piece collapses and the first shrinks toward nothing, and the spread walks up to six.

The desk version of this question is: I am long a deep in-the-money vertical and I want out. Why is the market paying me less than the width? Because the market is buying a bond off you, and bonds trade below par. If rates were zero the ceiling would be exactly six — set the rate slider to zero and watch the green line rise onto the grey one.

Try this

Predict what happens to the ceiling when you cut the time to two weeks. Does the spread get closer to six, further from it, or neither? Then press.

Check yourself

The 100/106 call spread with the stock at 500 and six months to run. What is its delta, roughly?

Zero delta, and a rho equal to the bond's. Once both legs are certain the spread stops being an option position at all. It is 6 × e−rT of pure discounting: vega zero, gamma zero, and the only thing that moves it is the rate. This is the instrument page 07 builds out of conversions, arrived at from the other end.
Derivation — where the ceiling comes from

Start at expiry. The spread pays min(max(ST − K₁, 0), K₂ − K₁), which is bounded above by the width W = K₂ − K₁ and below by zero. It is a bounded, non-negative claim.

0 ≤ payoff at T ≤ W

Reads as: whatever happens, the spread pays somewhere between nothing and the width.

Now price both bounds. A claim paying exactly W at time T is a zero-coupon bond, worth W·e−rT today. A claim paying nothing is worth nothing. Because pricing is linear and monotone — if one payoff is never larger than another, its price is never larger either — the spread's price sits between them.

0 ≤ V₀ ≤ W · e−rT

Reads as: the discounted width is a hard ceiling, not an approximation. A price above it would be an arbitrage: sell the spread, buy the bond, keep the difference and let both expire.

How fast the price approaches that ceiling is governed by the chance of finishing between the strikes. At a spot of 130 the risk-neutral probability of ending below 106 is 11.9%, and that residual is what the remaining fifty-odd cents pays for.

Where it breaks. On an American spread the short leg can be assigned early, which caps you at the width rather than the discounted width and can hand you the shortfall as a real loss. A hard-to-borrow underlying moves the ceiling again, because the relevant discount is no longer the plain financing rate — page 08 prices the assignment side and page 07 the borrow side. And on a futures spread the whole discussion inverts, because there the option itself is discounted rather than the underlying: page 13.

03

Four quarterly options cost twice the year

Same underlying, same strike, same twelve months of coverage. Twice the premium, and the reason is a square root.

You want a year of upside on a stock at 100. You can buy the one-year at-the-money call for 9.95, or you can buy the three-month call four times in a row. The four cost 19.93. Same coverage, same strike, same year — and one route costs precisely twice the other.

The answer is the most useful single fact in the subject: option value scales with the square root of time, not with time. A three-month option is not a quarter of a one-year option. It is half of one, because √(1/4) = ½. Buy four of them and you have paid 4 × ½ = 2 times the annual price.

Generalize and the arithmetic gets brutal. Rolling n times over the same horizon costs about √n times buying the horizon once. Monthly is 3.47×. Weekly is 7.23×. Daily is 15.9×.

price(T) ∝ √T ⟹ n × price(T/n) = √n × price(T)

Reads as: cutting the tenor by a factor of n only cuts the price by √n, so buying n of the short ones costs √n times the long one.

It pays to see where the √T comes from, because it is not a property of options. It is a property of independent increments. Variances add over time, so a year holds four times the variance of a quarter — but standard deviation is the square root of variance, so it is only twice as large. An option's value is essentially proportional to the standard deviation of the terminal price, and it inherits the square root from there.

Two consequences a junior trader needs on day one. First: never compare option prices across expiries without dividing by √T, because the raw numbers are meaningless. Second, and more useful: this is why the front end of the curve carries all the gamma and none of the vega. Halving the tenor halves the price but leaves you needing the same percentage move to break even — which is another way of saying gamma doubles as vega halves, and that is the trade in chapter 04.

Try this

Set the roll frequency to 52 and predict the multiple before you look. Most people guess between 3× and 5×. The square root says 7.23.

Check yourself

A one-month option is quoted at 2.88 and a four-month at 5.75, both at-the-money on the same stock. Which is dearer in volatility terms?

Identical. Dividing each price by √T gives the same number, which is what "the term structure is flat" means in premium terms. The reflex you want is to normalize by √T before any comparison — it turns a table of prices that looks like a slope into one that is flat, and vice versa. Page 03 uses exactly this to strip an earnings date out of a term structure.
Derivation — the at-the-money approximation, and how wrong it is

For an at-the-money option with zero rates, the Black–Scholes price collapses to something you can do in your head.

C ≈ 0.4 · S · σ · √T

Reads as: an at-the-money option costs about forty percent of the stock, times the volatility, times the square root of the time.

It comes from expanding N(d₁) − N(−d₁) about zero. With K = S and r = 0 we have d₁ = σ√T/2, and for small x, 2N(x) − 1 ≈ 2φ(0)x = 0.798x. Substituting gives C ≈ S · 0.798 · σ√T / 2 ≈ 0.399 · S · σ√T.

At 25% volatility and one year the approximation reads 9.97 against a true 9.95 — an error of 0.3%. It degrades as σ√T grows, because the linearization of N is only good near zero.

Where it breaks. The formula assumes zero rates and no dividends, and at long tenors that assumption costs more than the linearization does. Use it to sanity-check a screen, never to quote. And the √T law itself is an assumption about independence: a stock with a scheduled event does not obey it, which is the subject of page 03, and a stock with jumps obeys it for volatility but not for the shape of the distribution, which is page 15.

04

Long gamma and short vega, from one pair of options

They sound like the same exposure with opposite signs. One variable separates them, and it is time.

Here is the question in its usual form: you think the stock is about to move around a lot, but you also think implied volatility is too high. What do you buy? The answer most people give is "nothing — that is a contradiction." It is not.

Gamma and vega are both the convexity of the same position, but they scale in opposite directions with time. Gamma goes as 1/√T; it lives at the front. Vega goes as √T; it lives at the back. So the same instrument at two expiries gives two different mixtures, and any mixture you want is available by choosing the ratio between them.

Buy 3.34 of the one-month at-the-money against a sale of one one-year, and the vega nets to zero to the penny. What is left is gamma of 0.169 — eleven times the one-year's own — and the package pays you 1.68 to put on.

Γ ∝ 1/(S σ √T) · ν ∝ S √T ⟹ Γ/ν ∝ 1/T

Reads as: gamma per unit of vega is inversely proportional to time. A one-month option gives twelve times the gamma per unit of vega that a one-year does, and that ratio is the entire trade.

Now the part the question is really testing: what you pay for it. Nothing here is free, and the bill arrives as theta. The package bleeds 0.157 a day against the far option's 0.019 — eight and a half times the decay. And that is only the opening rate: theta accelerates, so a month in which the stock does not move at all costs 9.58, against the 1.68 you were paid. The stock has to travel about 3.5% by the front expiry just to break even.

And the concentration cuts both ways. The gamma advantage is enormous at the strike and gone six points away, because a one-month option that has moved is barely an option any more. That shape is what makes page 05's dealer gamma so violent near expiry, and it is why the honest description of this trade is not "long gamma, short vega" but "long gamma here, short vega everywhere, paying rent for the privilege."

Try this

Push the near expiry out to three months and predict what happens to the ratio you need. It falls — but does the gamma advantage fall faster than the theta bill, or slower?

Check yourself

You want the opposite exposure: short gamma, long vega. What do you do?

Sell the front, buy the back — a long calendar. You collect the front's rapid decay and own the back's vega. The risk is a large move soon, which is precisely the exposure the reversed package was buying. Both trades are bets on the shape of the term structure rather than its level, which makes them the natural companion to page 03.
05

A fifty-cent arbitrage that is really a borrow rate

Put–call parity is violated on your screen. Before you trade it, work out what the violation is quoting.

The stock is 100. The six-month 100 call is 5.00 and the 100 put is 3.50. Rates are 4%. Parity says call minus put should equal S − K·e−rT = 1.98, and the screen says 1.50. Forty-eight cents of free money. Do you take it?

The trade would be: buy the call, sell the put, sell the stock — a package that is short the forward at a good price. To hold it you must borrow shares. Which is the answer: the forty-eight cents is not an arbitrage, it is the market quoting you a borrow rate. Solve parity for the dividend-or-borrow term instead of assuming it is zero and the gap becomes 0.96% a year, which on a hundred shares over six months is $48.13 — precisely the forty-eight cents you thought you were being handed.

C − P = S · e−qT − K · e−rT

Reads as: the call minus the put is the discounted forward price of the stock. With q unknown this is one equation in one unknown, so the option market always quotes a borrow rate whether or not anyone means to.

The discipline generalizes past parity. Whenever a no-arbitrage relationship appears broken there are exactly three possibilities, in this order of likelihood. One: a parameter you assumed is wrong — a borrow rate, a dividend, a settlement convention. Two: the prices are not simultaneously tradeable — a stale mid, or your size is a hundred lots and the market is ten. Three: it is real, in which case it will be gone before you can click. Assume the first until you have eliminated it.

And the reverse reading is worth more than the arbitrage would have been. Because the relationship holds by construction, the option market becomes a borrow-rate screen: a name whose parity-implied borrow climbs from forty basis points to nine percent is telling you that shorting it has become expensive, usually before the stock-loan desk gets around to saying so. Page 07 builds the machinery; this is what it is for.

Try this

Drag the put price to 3.02 and watch the implied borrow. Then push it to 5.50 and read off what the market would be saying about that name.

Check yourself

The same gap appears, but the stock pays no dividend and borrows at ten basis points. Now what?

Check the spreads first. Forty-eight cents sounds like a lot until you notice the options are thirty cents wide each: crossing both, plus the stock's spread, can consume the entire gap — and you would be executing against a mid that nobody will trade. Page 05 is about exactly this difference between a price and a quote, and it is the second thing to eliminate, not the last.
06

Every name at 40 vol, the index at 25

Nothing here is mispriced. There is a third number in the problem, and nobody quotes it.

Twenty stocks, equally weighted, each with options trading at 40% implied volatility. The index made of exactly those twenty trades at 25%. Someone asks whether the index options are cheap.

They are neither cheap nor dear. The gap is not an opinion about volatility at all — it is an opinion about correlation, and it is the only number in the problem that nobody prints on a screen. Back it out and this market is quoting an average pairwise correlation of 0.359.

The bounds are worth committing to memory, because they make the whole thing intuitive. If the twenty names were independent, diversification would take the index to 40%/√20 = 8.94%. If they moved as one, the index would be exactly 40%. Every index volatility between those two is a correlation, and 25% sits about a third of the way up.

σI² = σ² · [ ρ + (1 − ρ)/n ]

Reads as: index variance is the average single-name variance scaled by correlation, plus a diversification term that vanishes as the index gets large. Solve it for ρ and you have the only number the market did not tell you.

The follow-up is where most people come apart. One of the twenty names halves its volatility, from 40 to 20. What does that do to the index? The instinct is that a name going quiet should calm the index noticeably. It moves it from 25.00% to 24.39% — six-tenths of a point — because the name is a twentieth of the weight and it enters through a variance rather than through a level. To move the index a full point you need the whole basket to change, and that is exactly what makes index volatility a correlation instrument rather than a volatility one.

Which is the trade: sell the index straddle, buy the twenty single-name straddles, delta-hedge everything, and your P&L is almost free of the volatility level and almost purely a bet on realized correlation. Page 10 runs it, and adds the part that matters — correlation is bounded at one, and it gets there on the day you can least afford it.

Try this

Predict the implied correlation for an index at 30% before you move anything. The relationship is quadratic, so a fifth more index volatility is a great deal more than a fifth more correlation.

Check yourself

Someone tells you the implied correlation on an index is 1.05. What have they found?

A broken calculation, almost always. The usual culprit is comparing an at-the-money index implied against component volatilities taken at a fixed delta — on a skewed surface those are different strikes, and the mismatch surfaces as impossible correlation. The bound is a free consistency check on the whole computation, which is what makes it worth knowing.
07

You sold at 30, it realized 25, and you lost

The hardest question here, because the intuitive answer is a specific number and the true answer is a range.

You sell a one-year at-the-money option at 30% implied and hedge the delta every day. The year ends, you measure realized volatility, and it comes in at exactly 25%. Five points of edge. How much did you make?

The answer everybody gives is vega times the difference: about two dollars a share. The answer is that you made anywhere from −2.61 to +6.12 on identical realized volatility, and which one you got was decided entirely by when the volatility arrived.

Both paths in the figure realize 25.000% for the year. In one, the market's movement is concentrated in a six-week window at the start; in the other, the same six-week window sits at the end. The first pays 6.12. The second loses 2.61. Nothing else differs — not the volatility, not the implied, not the hedging schedule.

P&L = ½ ∫ St² Γt ( σimp² − σreal,t² ) dt

Reads as: you earn the gap between implied and realized variance, but only in proportion to how much dollar gamma you were carrying at that moment. Volatility that arrives when your gamma is small is volatility you were never paid for; volatility that arrives when your gamma is enormous is charged at full rate.

Read the integral and the result stops being surprising. The weight on each moment is S²Γ, the dollar gamma, which for a near-the-money option grows without bound as time runs out. Volatility in the final six weeks is charged against a gamma several times larger than volatility in the first six. A year that is quiet then wild is a completely different trade from a year that is wild then quiet, and the summary statistic — 25% realized — is blind to both.

This is the correction to the sentence everyone learns first, that selling options when implied exceeds realized is profitable. It is profitable in expectation, averaged over paths. On any one path the spread around that expectation is enormous, and it is not noise: it is a systematic function of where in the option's life the movement showed up. Which is why a desk measures a short-volatility book in dollar gamma rather than vega, and why page 09's variance swap exists at all — it is the contract engineered to strip this weighting out and pay realized variance flat.

Try this

Put the loud window in the middle of the year and predict whether the result is closer to +6 or to −4. Then widen the window to the whole year and watch both extremes collapse toward the textbook answer.

Check yourself

You are short the same option and the stock gaps 30% away from the strike in week two, then does nothing all year. Realized volatility comes in at 30% — exactly what you sold. Where do you end up?

Up, and the reason is the same integral. After the gap you carry almost no dollar gamma, so the year's remaining movement costs you nothing while the option's time value decays into your pocket. It is the mirror image of the losing path in the figure — there the volatility landed on top of maximum gamma, here it landed once and left you holding a position that had stopped being sensitive. Realized volatility describes neither case.
Derivation — where the dollar-gamma weighting comes from

Hold a short option and hedge its delta continuously at the implied volatility. Over one instant the position's value changes by its theta plus its gamma acting on the realized move, with the delta term hedged away.

dΠ = −Θ dt − ½ Γ (dS)²

Reads as: you collect theta and pay for the square of whatever the stock actually did.

Black–Scholes fixes theta in terms of the implied volatility that priced the option: Θ = −½ σimp² S² Γ, ignoring the rate terms. And the realized move over that instant has (dS)² = σreal² S² dt. Substituting both:

dΠ = ½ S² Γ ( σimp² − σreal² ) dt

Reads as: each instant pays the variance difference, weighted by dollar gamma. Integrate over the life and you have the formula above.

The weight is the whole story. Two paths with the same integrated realized variance but different placement of that variance relative to the Γ profile produce different integrals — in the figure, nearly nine dollars of difference on an option sold for twelve.

Where it breaks. The derivation assumes continuous hedging and no jumps, so that (dS)² is genuinely σ²S²dt. Hedge daily and you add the rebalancing error page 01 measures; add a jump and (dS)² stops scaling with dt at all, which is page 15's finding that more frequent hedging stops helping. Transaction costs subtract at every rebalance, and page 06 shows they put a floor under how finely you can hedge in the first place.

08

How to take one you have never seen

Four moves that will carry you through most of them, then a drill at the same three tiers.

Every question on this page yields to the same four moves, applied in order. They are not clever. They are what a trader does out loud while somebody waits.

One — find the bound, then ask whether it is attained. Almost every options question contains a quantity that cannot be exceeded: a spread cannot beat its width, a call cannot beat the stock, a correlation cannot beat one, an index cannot be more volatile than its members. Name the bound first. Then the real question is nearly always why the price sits below it, and the answer is nearly always some mixture of discounting and residual uncertainty. That was chapters 02 and 06.

Two — take every variable to its extreme. What happens as T → 0? As σ → ∞? As the stock goes to zero, or to a thousand? Limits are where the structure of a formula shows itself, and they are free — you do not need the formula to work out that a call worth more than the stock would be an arbitrage. Half the warm-up deck is nothing but limits.

Three — split the answer into what must be true and what is a view. Put–call parity must be true; the borrow rate inside it is a market's opinion. The √T law must be true under independence; whether a given stock has independent increments is a view. This is the move that turned chapter 05 from an arbitrage into a rate quote, and it is the most valuable habit on the list.

Four — put a number on it, even a rough one. "The spread is worth less than six because of interest" is half an answer. "About twelve cents of interest and fifty-odd cents of remaining optionality" is an answer. Carry three approximations and you can do this in your head: an at-the-money option is 0.4·S·σ·√T, a discount factor is roughly 1 − rT, and one standard deviation of a move is σ√T.

Six questions drawn from across the page.

0 / 6 Answered nothing yet. Questions are scored as you go.
Question 1 · chapter 02

A 10-wide call spread, one year to run, rates at 5%, and the stock is at a thousand. What is it worth?

9.51. Once both legs are certain the position is a zero-coupon bond with a face value of the width, and no amount of further rally changes that. Delta, gamma and vega are all zero; the only greek left is rho.
Question 2 · chapter 03

A three-month at-the-money call costs 4.98. What should the one-year cost, on the same volatility?

Twice, not four times. Which is why rolling short-dated options is so expensive: four quarterlies cost 19.93 against 9.95 for the year, and the extra 9.98 buys nothing but the right to change your mind three times.
Question 3 · chapter 04

You are long gamma and short vega through a front-month against back-month package. What is the bill?

Theta, and it dwarfs the credit. The credit is not income — it is the market pricing the fact that you own the leg which decays fastest. The package needs the stock to travel about 3.5% inside four weeks just to break even, which is the honest description of every long-gamma position.
Question 4 · chapter 05

Put–call parity is off by half a point and will not close. Most likely explanation?

An unmodelled rate, nearly always. Parity is derived from payoffs alone, so it cannot be broken by a model being wrong — only by an input being wrong or by the prices not being simultaneously tradeable. Those are the first two things to eliminate, in that order.
Question 5 · chapter 06

Twenty names at 40 vol, the index at 25, and one name's volatility halves to 20. What does the index do?

24.39% — barely anything. Which is why index volatility trades as a correlation instrument rather than a volatility one: single-name volatility moves it slowly and correlation moves it fast. Halving all twenty takes the index to 12.50%.
Question 6 · chapter 07

Sold at 30, realized exactly 25, hedged daily for a year. Your P&L?

A nine-point range on an option sold for twelve. The hedging P&L is the variance difference weighted by S²Γ at every instant, so volatility arriving in the final weeks — when a near-the-money option's dollar gamma is at its largest — is charged at a completely different rate from volatility arriving in the first month. Realized volatility for the year is blind to this, which is why it is the wrong summary statistic.

Every figure here prices on a flat volatility surface with continuous rates and no transaction costs, which makes the answers clean and three of them optimistic. A real skew changes the correlation arithmetic of chapter 06, because index and component implieds get read at different moneyness — page 10 handles it properly. Chapter 07's paths are hedged daily and frictionlessly; page 06 prices the rebalancing and page 15 adds the jumps that break the argument entirely. And the bounds in chapters 02 and 05 assume European exercise: on American options the short leg can be assigned at any moment, which page 08 prices, and which turns several of these ceilings into things you can be forced through.
