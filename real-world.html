Every assumption, and what it costs

Page 01 proved that hedging error falls like one over the square root of how often you hedge. Add jumps and thirty-two times the hedging buys you 1.9× instead of 5.5×. This page breaks every assumption in turn and measures the bill.

Start here

This is the page that argues with the other fourteen. Every result in this series rests on assumptions, most of them stated in a footer and then not returned to. Here they are collected, broken one at a time, and priced.

  1. Chapter 02 is the one that matters. Page 01's central result — more hedging is monotonically better — is a theorem about continuous paths. Introduce jumps and the error stops falling, no matter how often you trade.
  2. Chapter 03 separates two things people conflate. A smile is not evidence that variance is misestimated. A fat-tailed world with identical variance produces one anyway.
  3. The model is not wrong so much as literal. Chapter 05 argues for what it is actually good at, which is being a language rather than a description.

01

The ledger

Nine assumptions, and the page of this series that measured what each one is worth.

Black–Scholes is usually introduced as a formula and criticised as a philosophy. Neither is very useful. What is useful is a list of the specific things it assumes, and a number attached to each — because the assumptions do not fail equally, and knowing which ones are expensive is most of what separates using a model from believing it.

The table below is that list. Almost every line has already been measured somewhere in this series, which is the point: the failures are not vague, they have magnitudes.

Three deserve highlighting because their cost is larger than people expect.

Continuous trading. Page 06 showed that adding transaction costs turns "hedge as often as possible" from a theorem into a corner solution that is wrong — the objective becomes a U-curve with a minimum around 128 rehedges on a ninety-day option.

Continuous paths. This is the assumption chapter 02 breaks, and it is the most damaging on the list because it invalidates the replication argument itself rather than merely adding a cost to it.

Constant volatility. Page 02 measured a delta difference of 0.097 — about ten shares a contract — arising purely from which sticky convention you assume, with identical prices on the screen.

What the table also shows is that the assumptions are not independent. Jumps produce fat tails; fat tails produce a smile; a smile makes "the volatility" ambiguous; the ambiguity makes delta model-dependent. Most of this page is one failure propagating.

Check yourself

Which failing assumption breaks the argument rather than just adding a cost?

Jumps. Every other item on the list leaves the replication argument intact and charges you for it. A jump means the delta hedge is set for a price the stock is no longer at, with no opportunity to correct, so the position is genuinely unhedged over exactly the move that matters. That is a difference in kind, and chapter 02 measures it.
02

The hedging error stops falling

Page 01's headline result is a theorem about continuous paths. Introduce jumps and more hedging buys almost nothing.

Page 01 sold a call, delta-hedged it mechanically, and measured what was left. The answer was clean and it is worth restating precisely: the standard deviation of the hedging error falls like 1/√N, so the residual can be made as small as you like by trading often enough.

The figure reproduces that. Under pure diffusion, the standard deviation falls from 2.90 at eight rebalances to 0.535 at 256 — and the diagnostic quantity sd × √N stays flat at roughly 8.2 to 8.7 across the whole range, which is what a clean 1/√N law looks like.

Now turn on jumps: one expected jump a year, averaging −12%. Everything else identical.

The standard deviation now falls from 3.45 to 1.89 — and stops. The diagnostic climbs from 9.75 to 30.17, which means the error is no longer falling like 1/√N. It is approaching a floor, and the floor is the jump risk, which no amount of rebalancing can touch because the whole point of a jump is that you do not get to trade during it.

Put as a single comparison: going from 8 rebalances to 256 — thirty-two times the trading — buys you a 5.5× reduction in error under the model, almost exactly the √32 = 5.66 the theory predicts. In the jumpy world it buys 1.9×. You have paid thirty-two times the transaction costs of page 06 for a third of the benefit.

That is the most important practical consequence in this series. The instruction "hedge more often" is derived from an assumption, and in a market with gaps it stops paying long before it stops costing. Where exactly it stops is an empirical question about the underlying, which is why hedging frequency is a desk-level judgement rather than a formula.

sd(error) · √N  =  constant   (diffusion)      sd(error) → floor   (with jumps)

Reads as: under continuous paths the error shrinks without limit as you trade more; with jumps it converges to something you cannot hedge away. The diagnostic is the product — flat means the law holds, rising means it does not.

Set jump intensity to zero. Predict what the diagnostic panel does, then turn jumps up to three a year and predict again.

Check yourself

Your desk doubles its hedging frequency and the realized hedging error barely improves. What does that tell you?

You have hit the jump floor. This is a genuinely useful diagnostic: plot your realized error against rebalance frequency and see whether sd × √N is flat. If it rises, extra hedging is buying less than the theory promises, and the right response is to stop trading more and start thinking about the tail — which usually means buying options rather than shares.
Derivation — why a jump cannot be hedged and a diffusion can

Over a small interval, a delta-hedged position's P&L is the part the hedge misses — the curvature times the square of the move.

P&L ≈ ½ Γ (ΔS)²

Reads as: what survives the hedge is gamma multiplied by the move squared. This is page 01's gamma rent.

Under diffusion, ΔS over an interval of length dt scales like √dt, so (ΔS)² scales like dt — and summing N of them gives a total that converges as N grows. Shrinking the interval shrinks the uncertainty in the sum, which is the 1/√N law.

A jump does not scale with dt at all. Its size is drawn from a fixed distribution regardless of how short the interval is, so (ΔS)² is O(1) rather than O(dt), and shrinking the interval does not shrink it.

diffusion: (ΔS)² ~ σ²S² dt      jump: (ΔS)² ~ J²S², independent of dt

Reads as: you can outrun a diffusion by trading faster and you cannot outrun a jump, because the jump does not get smaller when you do.

So the error decomposes into a piece that vanishes with more hedging and a piece that does not. The second piece is the floor in the figure.

Where it breaks. The simulation uses a single jump size and intensity, which is a caricature — real gaps cluster, arrive with warning some of the time, and have a size distribution that is itself uncertain. The direction of the result is robust and the level is a parameter. Note also what is not claimed: the jump risk is unhedgeable with the underlying, but it is perfectly hedgeable with other options, which is the entire reason the wing of a surface has the shape page 02 described.

03

Fat tails, at exactly the same variance

A smile is not evidence that the market disagrees about volatility. It is evidence that it disagrees about shape.

The lognormal is not wrong about how much stocks move. It is wrong about how that movement is distributed between ordinary days and extraordinary ones — and that distinction is invisible in a variance number.

Here is the cleanest demonstration. Build a world where 85% of the time volatility is 18 and 15% of the time it is 42. The total variance of that mixture is exactly the variance of a single volatility of 23.238%. Same variance. Different shape.

Now price options in both worlds and invert to implied volatility. The mixture produces 25.27% at the 70 strike, 22.00% at 90, 21.60% at the money, 22.11% at 120 and 22.87% at 130 — a smile, with its minimum near the money and both wings lifted.

Read that again, because it is the point. Nobody changed the variance. Nobody expressed a view about the level of volatility. A perfectly ordinary fat-tailed distribution with the same second moment produces a smile on its own.

Which reframes the whole of page 02. The smile is not primarily the market saying "volatility is higher than you think out there". It is the market saying "the distribution has more weight in the tails than a lognormal does", and the only way to say that in Black–Scholes' vocabulary — which has one parameter — is to quote a different parameter at each strike.

The single-volatility model's errors are correspondingly structured rather than random. Priced at the variance-matched 23.238%, it overprices the body — 10.67 against the mixture's 10.03 at the money — and underprices the far left tail, 0.380 against 0.566 at the 70 strike. A desk short the body and long the wings against that model is systematically, not occasionally, mispriced.

Try this

Set both regimes to the same volatility. Predict what the smile does. Then make the rare regime rarer but more violent, holding total variance fixed.

Check yourself

A market shows a pronounced smile. What is the minimum you can conclude?

Only that the distribution is not lognormal. Variance and shape are separate facts, and a smile is a statement about the second. This is why page 09 warns against reading a variance strike above the at-the-money vol as a forecast — it is largely the shape being averaged into a single number.
04

What one volatility costs on a real surface

Price a skewed market at the at-the-money volatility and the errors run from −74% to +213%.

Chapter 03 explained why a surface exists. This chapter prices the mistake of ignoring it — which is a mistake people make less often deliberately than by inheritance, from a spreadsheet or a risk system with one volatility field per underlying.

Take a market quoting a skewed surface: 25 at the money over six months with a −0.30 skew, which is unremarkable for an equity index. Price the whole chain at the at-the-money volatility instead and compare.

At the 75 strike the market pays 1.0296 and the flat model says 0.2689 — the model is low by 74%. At 85 it is low by 40%. At the money the error is a tolerable 2%. And then it inverts: at 115 the flat model is 46% high, and at 125 it is 213% high — pricing an option at more than three times what the market will pay.

Three things follow.

The error is not noise, it is a shape. It is systematically negative on the downside and positive on the upside, so a book that is long puts and short calls will be marked wrong in the same direction every single day. A random error averages out over positions; this one accumulates.

The worst percentage errors are on the cheapest options. The 213% error is on an option worth 0.37, so the dollar mistake is small — until it is a large position, which is exactly what cheap wing options tend to be.

The at-the-money option is fine. Which is precisely what makes the practice survive: the model is validated where it is checked, and wrong where nobody looks.

Try this

Flatten the skew to zero. Predict what the error panel does, then set it to −0.6 and see how far the wings move.

Check yourself

Why does a single-volatility model survive in production despite these errors?

It is right where anyone looks. The errors do not cancel — they are systematically one sign below the money and the other above, so a book with any directional option position accumulates them. This is the general shape of model risk: not that a model is wrong everywhere, but that it is right in the region used to validate it.
05

What survives

Every assumption fails and the model is still the right thing to use. Both halves of that sentence are load-bearing.

Fourteen pages built on a model, and this one has spent four chapters breaking it. The obvious conclusion — that the model should be replaced — is wrong, and understanding why is the last thing this series has to say.

The model is a language, not a description. Nobody believes volatility is constant. Quoting an option in implied volatility is not a claim that the model is true; it is a change of units that makes prices comparable across strikes, expiries and underlyings. A 3.40 option and a 12.10 option cannot be compared; a 23 vol and a 31 vol can. The model's job here is to be a shared denominator, and a wrong model does that job perfectly well as long as everyone uses the same wrong model.

The failures are mapped. That is what the surface is. The market has taken the model's inadequacy and priced it strike by strike, so the quoted surface contains exactly the information the model omits. Using Black–Scholes with a surface is not using a broken model; it is using the model as an interpolation scheme with the corrections supplied by the market.

The hedging argument survives in the direction that matters. Chapter 02 showed the error has a floor, not that hedging is pointless — at 256 rebalances the jumpy world's error is still 1.89 against an unhedged position's many multiples of that. Delta hedging removes the first-order risk exactly as advertised. What it cannot remove is the part that was never first-order.

And the alternatives inherit the same problem. Stochastic volatility, jump-diffusion and local volatility models all fit better and all contain parameters that must be estimated from data that is short, noisy and non-stationary. A more flexible model is not automatically a more accurate one; it is a model with more ways to be confidently wrong.

So the working position is neither belief nor rejection. Use the model, quote in its units, take the surface as the market's correction to it, and keep an explicit list of the places where it is not merely imprecise but structurally silent — jumps, liquidity, financing and the behaviour of other people. That list is this series, and its most useful property is that every entry has a number next to it.

Check yourself

Given everything on this page, what is implied volatility actually for?

It is a quoting convention. That sounds deflationary and is the opposite — a common language for price is enormously valuable, and it does not require the model generating it to be true. The whole surface is then the market's correction, which is why "use Black–Scholes with a surface" is a coherent position rather than a contradiction.

Question 1 · chapter 02

Thirty-two times the hedging frequency buys how much error reduction?

5.5× under the model, 1.9× with jumps. The theory's own prediction is √32 = 5.66, so the diffusion case confirms page 01 almost exactly. The jump case is where "hedge more often" stops paying while continuing to cost.
Question 2 · chapter 02

Why can't a delta hedge remove jump risk?

The jump does not get smaller when you trade faster. Unpredictability is not the issue — diffusive moves are unpredictable too, and they hedge fine. The issue is scaling: (ΔS)² is proportional to dt for a diffusion and independent of it for a jump.
Question 3 · chapter 03

A mixture of an 18 vol regime and a 42 vol regime has the same total variance as a single 23.2 vol. What do options do?

They smile, at identical variance. This separates two ideas that get conflated constantly: how much the stock moves, and how that movement is distributed. A smile is a statement about the second.
Question 4 · chapter 04

Pricing a skewed chain at the at-the-money volatility. Where are the errors largest in percentage terms?

The wings, by a wide margin. And the errors have opposite signs either side of the money, so they accumulate on any directional book rather than cancelling. The model is accurate exactly where it gets checked.
Question 5 · chapter 05

If every assumption fails, why keep using the model?

It is a language with market-supplied corrections. The failures are not small — this page measured several of them at tens of percent. What makes the model workable is that the surface encodes precisely what the model omits, so using it with a surface is an interpolation scheme rather than an act of faith.
Question 6 · chapter 05

Jump risk cannot be hedged with the underlying. What can hedge it?

Other options. The unhedgeability in chapter 02 is specifically with respect to shares. This is the connective tissue of the whole series: the wing is expensive because it insures something shares cannot, and someone short it is being paid for exactly the risk the model says does not exist.

The jump model here is Merton's — one intensity, one average jump size, lognormally distributed — which is the simplest thing that produces the effect and a caricature of a real gap. Real jumps cluster in time, arrive with partial warning, and have a size distribution that is itself uncertain and time-varying. The mixture in chapter 03 is two regimes with fixed weights, chosen for clarity rather than realism. Every simulation here is Monte Carlo, so the numbers move a little between runs; the diagnostics are stable and the third decimal is not. And this page criticises one model using another one — the comparisons are all inside a world we specified, which is itself the limitation being described.
